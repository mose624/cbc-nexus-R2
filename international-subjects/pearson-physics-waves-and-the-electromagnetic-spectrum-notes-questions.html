7. Waves and the electromagnetic spectrum

A wave transfers energy and information without an overall transfer of matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to it. Sound in air is longitudinal; electromagnetic waves are transverse.

wave speed v = frequency f × wavelength λ

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz), wavelength in metres (m), and wave speed in m s⁻¹. Period T is the time for one complete oscillation: T = 1 ÷ f.

Reflection changes direction at a boundary; refraction is a change in direction caused by a change in wave speed; diffraction is spreading as waves pass through a gap or around an obstacle. Diffraction is most noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.

Electromagnetic spectrum

In order of increasing frequency: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can damage living tissue; exposure should be controlled.

Further textbook notes: wave behaviour

Wave quantities: Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium. Wavelength λ is the distance between adjacent points in phase. Frequency f is the number of complete waves passing a point each second; period T is the time for one cycle, with T = 1/f. Wave speed v = fλ.

Wave types: In a transverse wave, oscillations are perpendicular to energy transfer; in a longitudinal wave, oscillations are parallel. Sound is longitudinal in air and requires a medium. Electromagnetic waves are transverse and can travel through a vacuum.

Wave interactions: Reflection changes direction at a boundary. Refraction occurs when wave speed changes on entering another medium, causing a change in direction unless it enters normally. Diffraction is spreading around an obstacle or through a gap; it is more noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.

Electromagnetic spectrum: In vacuum all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.0×10⁸ m/s. From radio to gamma, frequency and photon energy increase while wavelength decreases. Uses and hazards depend on frequency and intensity.

Worked example: A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 0.80 m. Speed = fλ = 50×0.80 = 40 m/s.

Check your understanding: Compare transverse and longitudinal waves, and explain why diffraction is greatest when a gap is close to one wavelength wide.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A wave has frequency 25 Hz and wavelength 0.40 m. v=fλ=10 m/s.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A wave travels at 12 m/s with frequency 4 Hz. Calculate wavelength.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Use v=fλ (1); λ=12/4=3.0 m (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Rearrange the wave equation correctly and give metres.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A wave has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength 0.8 m. Calculate its speed. State what happens to wavelength if speed stays constant and frequency doubles.

Show answer and mark scheme

v = fλ = 5 × 0.8 = 4 m/s (2); wavelength halves to 0.4 m (1).
