3. Forces, mass, weight and Newton’s laws

A force is an interaction that can change an object’s motion or shape. Forces are vectors and are measured in newtons. The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

resultant force F = mass m × acceleration a

For a constant mass, an unbalanced resultant force causes acceleration in the direction of the resultant force. If the resultant force is zero, an object remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity.

weight W = mass m × gravitational field strength g

Mass is measured in kilograms and does not depend on location. Weight is a force measured in newtons and changes with gravitational field strength. Near Earth’s surface, g is approximately 9.8 N kg⁻¹ (some questions may instruct you to use 10 N kg⁻¹).

Newton’s laws

  1. First law: An object remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted on by a resultant external force.
  2. Second law: Resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, F = ma.
  3. Third law: When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. The forces act on different objects.

Worked example

A 1,200 kg car accelerates at 2.0 m s⁻². Resultant force = 1,200 × 2.0 = 2,400 N.

Friction and air resistance oppose relative motion. At terminal velocity, resistive force balances the driving force or weight component, so resultant force and acceleration are zero.

Further textbook notes: force, inertia and momentum

Free-body diagrams: Isolate one object and draw only forces acting on that object. Weight acts vertically downwards; a normal contact force acts perpendicular to a surface; friction acts parallel to the surface and opposes relative motion or its tendency. Tension acts along a taut string. Do not draw an object's own force on another object on the same free-body diagram.

Equilibrium: If forces balance, the resultant force is zero. This means no acceleration, not necessarily no motion. A moving object can continue at constant velocity when the resultant force is zero. In vertical equilibrium on a level surface, normal reaction may equal weight, provided no other vertical forces act.

Inertia: Mass measures inertia—the resistance to a change in velocity. For the same resultant force, a larger mass has a smaller acceleration. Newton’s third-law pairs are equal and opposite but act on different bodies, so they do not cancel on a single-body diagram.

Worked example: A 5 kg trolley is pulled with 18 N while friction is 3 N. Resultant force = 18−3 = 15 N. Acceleration = 15/5 = 3 m/s².

Check your understanding: Draw a free-body diagram for a falling object before and after it reaches terminal velocity. Explain why acceleration becomes zero at terminal velocity.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 900 kg car accelerates at 1.5 m/s². Resultant force = ma = 900×1.5 = 1350 N.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A 2 kg trolley is pulled with 14 N; friction is 4 N. Calculate acceleration.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Resultant force = 14−4 = 10 N (1); use F=ma (1); a=10/2=5 m/s² (1).

Examiner’s explanation: The key mark is subtracting friction before applying Newton’s second law.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A 5 kg trolley experiences a resultant force of 15 N. Calculate its acceleration. State the effect of doubling the resultant force if mass stays constant.

Show answer and mark scheme

F = ma, so a = 15/5 = 3 m/s² (2); doubling force doubles acceleration to 6 m/s² (1).
