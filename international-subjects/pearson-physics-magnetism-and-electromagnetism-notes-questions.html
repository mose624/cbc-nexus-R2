9. Magnetism and electromagnetism

Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract. Magnetic field direction is defined as the direction a north pole would move. Field lines go from north to south outside a magnet; closer lines indicate a stronger field.

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A coil or solenoid produces a field similar to that of a bar magnet. The field strength of an electromagnet can be increased by increasing current, increasing the number of turns per unit length, or inserting a suitable magnetic core.

The motor effect is the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. The direction depends on current and magnetic field direction. Electromagnetic induction occurs when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or the magnetic flux linking a coil changes; the induced potential difference can drive a current in a complete circuit.

For induction questions, state what changes (field strength, motion, angle or number of turns) and connect it to the size of the induced potential difference.

Further textbook notes: magnetic fields and induction

Magnetic fields: A magnetic field is a region where a magnetic pole or moving charge experiences a force. Field lines form continuous loops; outside a bar magnet they run from north to south. The field is stronger where lines are closer together. Magnetic materials include iron, nickel and cobalt.

Electromagnets: A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A solenoid produces a field similar to a bar magnet; inserting a soft iron core strengthens it. Field strength can be increased by increasing current, adding turns per unit length or using a suitable core.

Motor effect: A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force. The direction depends on current and field direction; Fleming’s left-hand rule can help when the syllabus requires it. Reversing either current or magnetic field reverses the force.

Electromagnetic induction: An emf is induced when magnetic flux linkage changes. Faster relative motion, a stronger magnetic field or more coil turns can increase induced emf. A generator uses induction to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy; a transformer changes alternating potential difference using electromagnetic induction.

Worked example: A coil is moved into a magnetic field. If it is moved twice as quickly, the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage increases, so the induced emf is larger (assuming other factors remain constant).

Check your understanding: State two ways to increase the strength of an electromagnet and explain how a generator produces an alternating output.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: An electromagnet becomes stronger if current or the number of turns is increased; a soft iron core also strengthens it.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

State two ways to increase the strength of a solenoid and explain electromagnetic induction.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Increase current (1); increase turns per unit length or add a soft iron core (1); changing magnetic flux linkage induces an emf (1).

Examiner’s explanation: A generator requires relative motion/change in flux; a steady unchanging field alone does not induce an emf.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Describe the magnetic field pattern around a straight current-carrying wire and state how to increase its strength.

Show answer and mark scheme

Field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire (1); increase the current (1); the field becomes stronger (1).
