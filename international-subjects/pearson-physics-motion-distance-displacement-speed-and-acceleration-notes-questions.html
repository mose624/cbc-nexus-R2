2. Motion: distance, displacement, speed and acceleration

Distance is the total path length travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position in a stated direction. Speed is the rate of change of distance, while velocity is the rate of change of displacement.

average speed = total distance ÷ total time
average velocity = displacement ÷ time
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time

Worked example

A cyclist increases velocity from 4 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹ in 3 s.

Change in velocity = 10 − 4 = 6 m s⁻¹. Acceleration = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m s⁻².

Distance–time and velocity–time graphs

  • The gradient of a distance–time graph gives speed.
  • A horizontal distance–time line means the object is stationary.
  • The gradient of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement (or distance travelled when velocity remains non-negative).
Common mistake: Do not confuse the height of a velocity–time graph (velocity) with its gradient (acceleration).

Further textbook notes: interpreting motion

Distance and displacement: Distance is scalar and depends on the complete route; displacement is the vector from initial to final position. An athlete completing one lap has travelled a non-zero distance but has zero displacement if they finish at the starting point. Speed is scalar; velocity includes direction.

Acceleration: Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. An object can accelerate while slowing down: its acceleration is then opposite to its velocity. Uniform acceleration means velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.

Graph skills: On a displacement–time graph, gradient gives velocity. On a speed–time graph, area under the graph gives distance. On a velocity–time graph, signed area gives displacement; areas below the time axis count as negative. A curved graph has a changing gradient, so instantaneous velocity is found from the tangent.

Worked example: A runner accelerates uniformly from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s. Acceleration = (8−2)/3 = 2 m/s². If the velocity–time graph is a straight line, displacement during this interval is average velocity × time = ((2+8)/2)×3 = 15 m.

Check your understanding: Sketch a velocity–time graph for an object that moves forward, stops, then reverses direction. Explain the sign of its displacement.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A car changes velocity from 6 m/s to 18 m/s in 4 s. a=(18−6)/4=3 m/s².
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A cyclist travels 150 m in 25 s. Calculate average speed.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Speed = distance/time (1) = 150/25 = 6.0 m/s (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Include the correct unit. Do not use displacement unless direction and net change in position are requested.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A car accelerates uniformly from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 6 s. Calculate its acceleration and state what the gradient of a velocity–time graph represents.

Show answer and mark scheme

a = (v − u)/t = (16 − 4)/6 = 2.0 m/s² (2); gradient represents acceleration (1).
