Chapter 5: Respiration and gas exchange

Respiration is the set of cellular reactions that transfer energy from nutrient molecules to ATP. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy than anaerobic respiration.

Core explanation

Aerobic respiration can be summarised as glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. In humans, anaerobic respiration in muscles converts glucose to lactic acid; in yeast, glucose is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide. ATP supplies energy for active transport, muscle contraction and synthesis.

Worked example / application

Gas exchange in mammalian lungs occurs in alveoli. They provide a large surface area, moist lining, thin walls and dense capillary network. Ventilation maintains steep concentration gradients: oxygen diffuses into blood and carbon dioxide diffuses into alveolar air.

Further textbook notes: respiration and gas exchange

Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose using oxygen: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Energy is transferred to ATP for cellular processes. Anaerobic respiration releases less energy; in human muscles it produces lactate, while yeast produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Alveoli provide a large surface area, thin moist walls and a dense capillary supply. Ventilation maintains a steep oxygen and carbon dioxide concentration gradient. Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into blood and binds to haemoglobin; carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into alveoli to be exhaled. Smoking damages cilia and alveolar walls and can reduce gas-exchange efficiency.

Practise: Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration and explain how ventilation maintains diffusion gradients.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy than anaerobic respiration. Alveoli have thin moist walls, large surface area and rich blood supply.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

Explain how an alveolus is adapted for efficient gas exchange.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Large surface area (1); thin wall/short diffusion distance (1); moist surface allows gases to dissolve (1); dense capillary supply/ventilation maintains steep gradient (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Each adaptation should be linked to faster diffusion.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Describe the pathway of oxygen from inhaled air into the blood at an alveolus.

Show answer and mark scheme

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air across the thin alveolar and capillary walls (1); into blood (1); down its concentration gradient into red blood cells/haemoglobin (1).
