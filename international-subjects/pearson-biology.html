Chapter 1: Cell structure and organisation

Cells are the basic structural and functional units of living organisms. Eukaryotic cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus and are generally smaller. Animal cells contain a cell-surface membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes. Plant cells also have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues and a permanent vacuole containing cell sap.

Core explanation

The nucleus contains DNA and controls cell activities. Mitochondria are the main site of aerobic respiration and release energy for cellular processes. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy. The cell membrane controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.

Worked example / application

A specialised cell has adaptations for its function. A sperm cell has a flagellum and many mitochondria; a root hair cell has a long projection for absorption; a palisade cell contains many chloroplasts. Magnification = image size ÷ actual size, using the same units.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Compare plant, animal and bacterial cells. Explain how a root hair cell is adapted to absorption. Calculate actual size when an image is 36 mm long at ×600 magnification.

Chapter 2: Movement of substances

Substances cross cell membranes by diffusion, osmosis and active transport. Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, due to random motion.

Core explanation

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution (higher water potential) to a more concentrated solution (lower water potential). Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration and carrier proteins.

Worked example / application

Factors affecting diffusion include concentration gradient, temperature, surface area and diffusion distance. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces with a large surface area, thin barriers and good transport systems.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Define diffusion and osmosis precisely. Explain why a large surface-area-to-volume ratio benefits exchange. Predict what happens to a plant cell in a concentrated salt solution.

Chapter 3: Biological molecules and enzymes

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins are major biological molecules. Carbohydrates provide energy and can form structural materials; lipids are energy stores and components of membranes; proteins form enzymes, antibodies, transport molecules and structural tissues.

Core explanation

Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that lower activation energy. A substrate binds to an enzyme's active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex. The active site is complementary to the substrate; changes in temperature or pH can alter its shape (denaturation).

Worked example / application

Increasing temperature initially increases collision frequency and reaction rate until an optimum is reached. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme structure are disrupted. Extreme pH can also denature an enzyme. Enzyme activity can be investigated by measuring product formed or substrate used per unit time.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Explain the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. Distinguish a catalyst from a reactant. Describe how to investigate the effect of pH on amylase.

Chapter 4: Nutrition, digestion and absorption

A balanced diet supplies carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water in suitable amounts. Digestion breaks large insoluble food molecules into smaller soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

Core explanation

Mechanical digestion increases surface area. Chemical digestion uses enzymes: amylase breaks starch into sugars, proteases break proteins into amino acids, and lipases break lipids into fatty acids and glycerol. Bile is made by the liver, stored in the gall bladder, neutralises acidic chyme and emulsifies fats.

Worked example / application

The small intestine is adapted for absorption by its length, folds, villi and microvilli. Each villus has a thin epithelium, blood capillaries and a lacteal. Glucose and amino acids enter blood; products of fat digestion enter the lacteal.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Trace the route of food through the alimentary canal. Explain how villi are adapted for absorption. State the role of bile and explain why it is not an enzyme.

Chapter 5: Respiration and gas exchange

Respiration is the set of cellular reactions that transfer energy from nutrient molecules to ATP. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy than anaerobic respiration.

Core explanation

Aerobic respiration can be summarised as glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. In humans, anaerobic respiration in muscles converts glucose to lactic acid; in yeast, glucose is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide. ATP supplies energy for active transport, muscle contraction and synthesis.

Worked example / application

Gas exchange in mammalian lungs occurs in alveoli. They provide a large surface area, moist lining, thin walls and dense capillary network. Ventilation maintains steep concentration gradients: oxygen diffuses into blood and carbon dioxide diffuses into alveolar air.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Write the word equation for aerobic respiration. Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration. Explain four adaptations of alveoli for gas exchange.

Chapter 6: Transport in plants and animals

Transport systems move materials between exchange surfaces and cells. Mammalian blood contains plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. Red blood cells contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen reversibly.

Core explanation

The heart is a double pump. The right side sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side sends oxygenated blood to the body. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood, and capillaries allow exchange with tissues.

Worked example / application

In plants, xylem transports water and mineral ions from roots to leaves and also provides support. Phloem translocates assimilates such as sucrose between sources and sinks. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, mainly through stomata.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Compare arteries, veins and capillaries. Explain how haemoglobin supports oxygen transport. Distinguish xylem transport from phloem translocation.

Chapter 7: Coordination, response and homeostasis

Coordination enables organisms to detect and respond to changes. The nervous system uses electrical impulses along neurones; hormonal coordination uses chemical messengers carried in blood. A reflex arc produces a rapid, automatic response.

Core explanation

A receptor detects a stimulus. Sensory neurones carry impulses to the central nervous system; relay neurones connect pathways; motor neurones carry impulses to effectors. A synapse is a junction where neurotransmitters transmit a signal between neurones.

Worked example / application

Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment. Negative feedback detects deviation from a set point and activates effectors that oppose the change. Thermoregulation involves sweating and vasodilation when hot, and shivering and vasoconstriction when cold.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Draw and label a reflex arc. Explain why a reflex is protective. Describe negative feedback in temperature regulation.

Chapter 8: Inheritance, variation and evolution

DNA is the hereditary material. A gene is a section of DNA that influences a characteristic by coding for a functional product. Alternative forms of a gene are alleles. Chromosomes carry genes and are found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells.

Core explanation

During meiosis, chromosome number is halved to form genetically different gametes. Fertilisation restores the diploid chromosome number. In a monohybrid cross, alleles separate into gametes and combine randomly at fertilisation. Genotype describes alleles; phenotype describes observable characteristics.

Worked example / application

Variation may be genetic, environmental or both. Natural selection occurs when individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics survive and reproduce more successfully, passing alleles to offspring. Over many generations, allele frequencies can change.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Distinguish genotype from phenotype. Explain how meiosis produces variation. Use a genetic diagram to predict offspring from two heterozygous parents.

Chapter 9: Ecology, ecosystems and human impacts

An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms interacting with each other and with the abiotic environment. A habitat is the place where an organism lives; a population is members of one species in an area; a community is all populations in an area.

Core explanation

Energy flows through food chains and food webs. Producers convert light energy into chemical energy; consumers obtain energy by feeding. Biomass and energy are lost between trophic levels through respiration, movement, waste and uneaten material. Nutrients are recycled, for example through decomposition.

Worked example / application

Sampling methods include quadrats for estimating abundance or percentage cover and transects for studying distribution across an environmental gradient. Human activities such as deforestation, pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions can alter ecosystems and biodiversity.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Construct a food chain and identify trophic levels. Explain why energy transfer between trophic levels is inefficient. Describe a valid quadrat sampling method.

Chapter 10: Practical biology, data and scientific evaluation

Biology practical work requires a clear question, hypothesis, independent variable, dependent variable and controlled variables. A valid method changes one independent variable while controlling other factors that could affect the result.

Core explanation

Use suitable apparatus, repeat measurements, calculate means and identify anomalous results using a stated rule. Tables need headings and units. Graphs should use appropriate scales, labelled axes and a best-fit line or curve where suitable. Conclusions must be supported by data.

Worked example / application

Reliability is improved by repetition and consistent technique. Validity depends on whether the method measures what it claims to measure and controls relevant variables. Evaluate limitations and suggest realistic improvements, such as a more precise instrument or a larger sample.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Plan an investigation of light intensity and photosynthesis. Identify variables and safety precautions. Explain the difference between accuracy, reliability and validity.
