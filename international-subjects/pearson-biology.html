Chapter 1: Cell structure and organisation

Cells are the basic structural and functional units of living organisms. Eukaryotic cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus and are generally smaller. Animal cells contain a cell-surface membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes. Plant cells also have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues and a permanent vacuole containing cell sap.

Core explanation

The nucleus contains DNA and controls cell activities. Mitochondria are the main site of aerobic respiration and release energy for cellular processes. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy. The cell membrane controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.

Worked example / application

A specialised cell has adaptations for its function. A sperm cell has a flagellum and many mitochondria; a root hair cell has a long projection for absorption; a palisade cell contains many chloroplasts. Magnification = image size ÷ actual size, using the same units.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Compare plant, animal and bacterial cells. Explain how a root hair cell is adapted to absorption. Calculate actual size when an image is 36 mm long at ×600 magnification.

Further textbook notes: cell ultrastructure

The cell-surface membrane controls movement of substances into and out of the cell. The nucleus contains DNA and controls cell activities through gene expression. Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration; ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis. In plants, the cellulose cell wall provides support, the permanent vacuole contains cell sap and chloroplasts contain chlorophyll for photosynthesis.

Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles. Their DNA is free in the cytoplasm, often as a circular molecule, and they may contain plasmids. Eukaryotic cells include animal, plant and fungal cells. Microscope magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Convert units before calculating: 1 mm = 1000 μm.

Practise: Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and calculate actual size from an image scale bar.

Worked example: A cell image is 40 mm long at ×400 magnification. Actual length=40/400=0.10 mm=100 μm.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Prokaryote has no nucleus (1); its DNA is free in cytoplasm/circular and it lacks membrane-bound organelles (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use paired comparisons; bacteria are prokaryotes.

Chapter 2: Movement of substances

Substances cross cell membranes by diffusion, osmosis and active transport. Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, due to random motion.

Core explanation

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution (higher water potential) to a more concentrated solution (lower water potential). Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration and carrier proteins.

Worked example / application

Factors affecting diffusion include concentration gradient, temperature, surface area and diffusion distance. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces with a large surface area, thin barriers and good transport systems.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Define diffusion and osmosis precisely. Explain why a large surface-area-to-volume ratio benefits exchange. Predict what happens to a plant cell in a concentrated salt solution.

Further textbook notes: membrane transport

Diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient due to random motion. Its rate increases with a steeper gradient, higher temperature and larger surface area, and decreases with a longer diffusion distance. Osmosis is the net movement of water through a partially permeable membrane from higher water potential to lower water potential.

Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration and carrier proteins. It is important in mineral ion uptake by root hair cells and glucose absorption in certain tissues. Plant cells may become turgid, flaccid or plasmolysed depending on water movement.

Practise: Predict changes in plant and animal cells placed in dilute and concentrated solutions, and explain why root hair cells need active transport.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Water moves by osmosis from a dilute solution with higher water potential to a more concentrated solution with lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A plant cell is placed in a concentrated sugar solution. Explain what happens.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Water leaves the cell by osmosis (1); vacuole/cytoplasm volume decreases (1); membrane pulls away from cell wall/plasmolysis may occur (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Name osmosis and state the direction of water movement.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A red blood cell is placed in distilled water. Predict what happens and explain why.

Show answer and mark scheme

Water enters by osmosis (1); distilled water has a higher water potential than the cell cytoplasm (1); the cell swells and may burst because it has no cell wall (1).

Chapter 3: Biological molecules and enzymes

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins are major biological molecules. Carbohydrates provide energy and can form structural materials; lipids are energy stores and components of membranes; proteins form enzymes, antibodies, transport molecules and structural tissues.

Core explanation

Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that lower activation energy. A substrate binds to an enzyme's active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex. The active site is complementary to the substrate; changes in temperature or pH can alter its shape (denaturation).

Worked example / application

Increasing temperature initially increases collision frequency and reaction rate until an optimum is reached. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme structure are disrupted. Extreme pH can also denature an enzyme. Enzyme activity can be investigated by measuring product formed or substrate used per unit time.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Explain the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. Distinguish a catalyst from a reactant. Describe how to investigate the effect of pH on amylase.

Further textbook notes: biological molecules and enzyme action

Carbohydrates are built from sugar units; starch and glycogen are storage polysaccharides, while cellulose forms plant cell walls. Lipids contain glycerol and fatty acids and are useful long-term energy stores and membrane components. Proteins are polymers of amino acids and have structural, transport, defence and catalytic roles.

Enzymes are biological catalysts with active sites complementary to substrates. Substrate binding forms an enzyme–substrate complex. High temperature can denature an enzyme by changing the active-site shape; extreme pH can also disrupt bonds maintaining its structure. Rate depends on substrate concentration until active sites become saturated.

Practise: Explain enzyme specificity and interpret a graph showing enzyme activity against temperature or pH.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: At high temperature an enzyme's active-site shape changes due to denaturation, so the substrate is no longer complementary and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Explain why an enzyme's rate falls sharply above its optimum temperature.

Show model answer and mark allocation

High temperature disrupts bonds maintaining enzyme structure (1); active site changes shape/denatures (1); substrate no longer fits so fewer complexes form (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Distinguish denaturation from simply slowing molecular movement.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

An enzyme works fastest at 37 °C but activity falls sharply at 70 °C. Explain why.

Show answer and mark scheme

At high temperature bonds maintaining the enzyme's shape are disrupted (1); active site changes shape (1); substrate no longer fits, so enzyme is denatured (1).

Chapter 4: Nutrition, digestion and absorption

A balanced diet supplies carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water in suitable amounts. Digestion breaks large insoluble food molecules into smaller soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

Core explanation

Mechanical digestion increases surface area. Chemical digestion uses enzymes: amylase breaks starch into sugars, proteases break proteins into amino acids, and lipases break lipids into fatty acids and glycerol. Bile is made by the liver, stored in the gall bladder, neutralises acidic chyme and emulsifies fats.

Worked example / application

The small intestine is adapted for absorption by its length, folds, villi and microvilli. Each villus has a thin epithelium, blood capillaries and a lacteal. Glucose and amino acids enter blood; products of fat digestion enter the lacteal.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Trace the route of food through the alimentary canal. Explain how villi are adapted for absorption. State the role of bile and explain why it is not an enzyme.

Further textbook notes: digestion and absorption

Mechanical digestion increases surface area; chemical digestion uses enzymes to hydrolyse large insoluble molecules into small soluble products. Amylase breaks starch into maltose; proteases break proteins into peptides and amino acids; lipases break lipids into fatty acids and glycerol. Bile is made by the liver and stored in the gall bladder; it neutralises acidic chyme and emulsifies fats, increasing surface area for lipase.

The small intestine is adapted for absorption by its length, folds, villi and microvilli. Each villus has a thin epithelium, a rich capillary network for sugars and amino acids, and a lacteal for absorbed lipid products.

Practise: Match enzymes to substrates and products, and explain how villus structure increases absorption rate.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Amylase digests starch to maltose; protease digests proteins to peptides/amino acids; lipase digests lipids to fatty acids and glycerol.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

Explain two adaptations of a small-intestinal villus for absorption.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Large surface area from villi/microvilli (1) with increased absorption (1); thin epithelium/short diffusion distance (1); capillary network maintains concentration gradient (1).

Examiner’s explanation: For full marks link each structural feature to its function.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Explain how the small intestine is adapted for absorption of digested nutrients.

Show answer and mark scheme

Many villi/microvilli provide large surface area (1); thin epithelium gives short diffusion distance (1); rich blood supply maintains concentration gradient (1).

Chapter 5: Respiration and gas exchange

Respiration is the set of cellular reactions that transfer energy from nutrient molecules to ATP. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy than anaerobic respiration.

Core explanation

Aerobic respiration can be summarised as glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. In humans, anaerobic respiration in muscles converts glucose to lactic acid; in yeast, glucose is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide. ATP supplies energy for active transport, muscle contraction and synthesis.

Worked example / application

Gas exchange in mammalian lungs occurs in alveoli. They provide a large surface area, moist lining, thin walls and dense capillary network. Ventilation maintains steep concentration gradients: oxygen diffuses into blood and carbon dioxide diffuses into alveolar air.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Write the word equation for aerobic respiration. Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration. Explain four adaptations of alveoli for gas exchange.

Further textbook notes: respiration and gas exchange

Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose using oxygen: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Energy is transferred to ATP for cellular processes. Anaerobic respiration releases less energy; in human muscles it produces lactate, while yeast produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Alveoli provide a large surface area, thin moist walls and a dense capillary supply. Ventilation maintains a steep oxygen and carbon dioxide concentration gradient. Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into blood and binds to haemoglobin; carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into alveoli to be exhaled. Smoking damages cilia and alveolar walls and can reduce gas-exchange efficiency.

Practise: Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration and explain how ventilation maintains diffusion gradients.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy than anaerobic respiration. Alveoli have thin moist walls, large surface area and rich blood supply.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

Explain how an alveolus is adapted for efficient gas exchange.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Large surface area (1); thin wall/short diffusion distance (1); moist surface allows gases to dissolve (1); dense capillary supply/ventilation maintains steep gradient (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Each adaptation should be linked to faster diffusion.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Describe the pathway of oxygen from inhaled air into the blood at an alveolus.

Show answer and mark scheme

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air across the thin alveolar and capillary walls (1); into blood (1); down its concentration gradient into red blood cells/haemoglobin (1).

Chapter 6: Transport in plants and animals

Transport systems move materials between exchange surfaces and cells. Mammalian blood contains plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. Red blood cells contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen reversibly.

Core explanation

The heart is a double pump. The right side sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side sends oxygenated blood to the body. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood, and capillaries allow exchange with tissues.

Worked example / application

In plants, xylem transports water and mineral ions from roots to leaves and also provides support. Phloem translocates assimilates such as sucrose between sources and sinks. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, mainly through stomata.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Compare arteries, veins and capillaries. Explain how haemoglobin supports oxygen transport. Distinguish xylem transport from phloem translocation.

Further textbook notes: transport in plants and mammals

Xylem vessels transport water and mineral ions from roots to leaves and provide support. Transpiration is evaporation of water from leaf surfaces followed by diffusion of water vapour through stomata. It creates a pull that draws water up the xylem. Transpiration rate is affected by light intensity, temperature, humidity and air movement.

Phloem translocates dissolved sugars and other organic substances between sources and sinks. Mammalian double circulation sends blood from the heart to lungs and back, then to the body and back. Red blood cells carry oxygen using haemoglobin; white blood cells defend against pathogens; platelets help blood clot.

Practise: Trace blood through the heart and explain how xylem and phloem differ in structure and function.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Blood travels right atrium→right ventricle→pulmonary artery→lungs→pulmonary vein→left atrium→left ventricle→aorta. Xylem transports water; phloem transports assimilates.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Describe the route of blood from the right ventricle to the left atrium.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Right ventricle pumps blood into pulmonary artery (1); blood passes through lung capillaries for gas exchange (1); returns through pulmonary vein to left atrium (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Do not confuse pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein; name is based on direction relative to heart.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Explain how the structure of a xylem vessel supports its function.

Show answer and mark scheme

Hollow tube with no end walls allows continuous water flow (1); lignified walls strengthen and prevent collapse (1); pits allow sideways movement of water (1).

Chapter 7: Coordination, response and homeostasis

Coordination enables organisms to detect and respond to changes. The nervous system uses electrical impulses along neurones; hormonal coordination uses chemical messengers carried in blood. A reflex arc produces a rapid, automatic response.

Core explanation

A receptor detects a stimulus. Sensory neurones carry impulses to the central nervous system; relay neurones connect pathways; motor neurones carry impulses to effectors. A synapse is a junction where neurotransmitters transmit a signal between neurones.

Worked example / application

Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment. Negative feedback detects deviation from a set point and activates effectors that oppose the change. Thermoregulation involves sweating and vasodilation when hot, and shivering and vasoconstriction when cold.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Draw and label a reflex arc. Explain why a reflex is protective. Describe negative feedback in temperature regulation.

Further textbook notes: nervous and hormonal coordination

A stimulus is detected by receptors; sensory neurones carry impulses to the central nervous system, and motor neurones carry impulses to effectors. At a synapse, neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the next neurone. Reflex actions are rapid and automatic, helping protect the body.

Hormones are chemical messengers carried in blood to target organs. They usually act more slowly than nerve impulses but their effects can last longer. Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment, including blood glucose, body temperature and water balance. Negative feedback reverses a deviation from a set point.

Practise: Label a reflex arc and explain how insulin and glucagon regulate blood glucose.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A reflex arc: receptor→sensory neurone→CNS/relay neurone→motor neurone→effector. Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting uptake and glycogen formation.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

Describe the pathway of a simple reflex action.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Receptor detects stimulus (1); sensory neurone carries impulse to CNS (1); relay neurone passes signal to motor neurone (1); motor neurone stimulates effector to respond (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use the correct sequence and distinguish sensory from motor neurones.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A person touches a hot object and quickly withdraws a hand. Name the pathway and explain why the response is rapid.

Show answer and mark scheme

Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone in spinal cord → motor neurone → effector (2); it is a reflex action that is rapid and automatic, reducing injury (1).

Chapter 8: Inheritance, variation and evolution

DNA is the hereditary material. A gene is a section of DNA that influences a characteristic by coding for a functional product. Alternative forms of a gene are alleles. Chromosomes carry genes and are found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells.

Core explanation

During meiosis, chromosome number is halved to form genetically different gametes. Fertilisation restores the diploid chromosome number. In a monohybrid cross, alleles separate into gametes and combine randomly at fertilisation. Genotype describes alleles; phenotype describes observable characteristics.

Worked example / application

Variation may be genetic, environmental or both. Natural selection occurs when individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics survive and reproduce more successfully, passing alleles to offspring. Over many generations, allele frequencies can change.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Distinguish genotype from phenotype. Explain how meiosis produces variation. Use a genetic diagram to predict offspring from two heterozygous parents.

Further textbook notes: DNA, inheritance and selection

DNA is a double helix made of nucleotide units. A gene is a sequence of DNA bases that codes for a functional product. Alleles are alternative forms of a gene. During meiosis, gametes receive one allele for each gene; fertilisation restores pairs of alleles. A dominant allele is expressed in a heterozygote, while a recessive phenotype requires two recessive alleles in a simple inheritance model.

Use a genetic diagram to show parental genotypes, gametes and possible offspring genotypes. Variation arises from genetic differences and environmental influences. Natural selection occurs when individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics survive and reproduce more successfully, changing allele frequencies over generations.

Practise: Complete monohybrid crosses, calculate genotype and phenotype ratios, and distinguish continuous from discontinuous variation.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: For Tt×Tt, possible offspring genotypes are TT, Tt, Tt, tt. Genotype ratio 1:2:1; dominant phenotype:recessive phenotype=3:1.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Two heterozygous parents are crossed. What is the probability of a recessive phenotype?

Show model answer and mark allocation

Gametes T and t from each parent (1); offspring genotypes TT,Tt,Tt,tt (1); probability tt=1/4 or 25% (1).

Examiner’s explanation: State the probability, not just the ratio.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

In a genetic cross, two heterozygous tall plants (Tt × Tt) are crossed. Give the genotype ratio and probability of a short offspring.

Show answer and mark scheme

Offspring genotypes TT, Tt, Tt, tt (1); genotype ratio 1:2:1 (1); probability of short tt = 1/4 or 25% (1).

Chapter 9: Ecology, ecosystems and human impacts

An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms interacting with each other and with the abiotic environment. A habitat is the place where an organism lives; a population is members of one species in an area; a community is all populations in an area.

Core explanation

Energy flows through food chains and food webs. Producers convert light energy into chemical energy; consumers obtain energy by feeding. Biomass and energy are lost between trophic levels through respiration, movement, waste and uneaten material. Nutrients are recycled, for example through decomposition.

Worked example / application

Sampling methods include quadrats for estimating abundance or percentage cover and transects for studying distribution across an environmental gradient. Human activities such as deforestation, pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions can alter ecosystems and biodiversity.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Construct a food chain and identify trophic levels. Explain why energy transfer between trophic levels is inefficient. Describe a valid quadrat sampling method.

Further textbook notes: ecosystems and human impact

Energy enters most ecosystems through photosynthesis and passes along food chains. At each trophic level, energy is lost through respiration, movement, heat, waste and uneaten material, so only a fraction is transferred to the next level. Food webs show interconnected feeding relationships and population changes can affect several species.

Decomposers break down dead organisms and waste, returning mineral ions to the environment. The carbon cycle includes photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, decomposition and combustion. Human activities such as deforestation and burning fossil fuels increase atmospheric carbon dioxide. Fertiliser runoff can cause eutrophication: algal growth, reduced light, decomposition and oxygen depletion.

Practise: Interpret a food web, explain energy transfer inefficiency and describe how nutrient pollution reduces dissolved oxygen.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: In eutrophication, nitrate enrichment causes algal growth; algae block light, aquatic plants die, decomposers increase and respiration reduces dissolved oxygen.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

Explain how fertiliser runoff can lead to fish deaths in a lake.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Nitrates/phosphates enter water (1); algal bloom blocks light and plants die (1); decomposers break down dead material and respire (1); dissolved oxygen falls, causing fish to suffocate (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Show the causal sequence rather than listing isolated facts.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Explain how removing a predator from a food web may affect prey and vegetation.

Show answer and mark scheme

Prey numbers may increase because predation decreases (1); greater feeding pressure reduces vegetation (1); this can affect other organisms and disrupt the food web (1).

Chapter 10: Practical biology, data and scientific evaluation

Biology practical work requires a clear question, hypothesis, independent variable, dependent variable and controlled variables. A valid method changes one independent variable while controlling other factors that could affect the result.

Core explanation

Use suitable apparatus, repeat measurements, calculate means and identify anomalous results using a stated rule. Tables need headings and units. Graphs should use appropriate scales, labelled axes and a best-fit line or curve where suitable. Conclusions must be supported by data.

Worked example / application

Reliability is improved by repetition and consistent technique. Validity depends on whether the method measures what it claims to measure and controls relevant variables. Evaluate limitations and suggest realistic improvements, such as a more precise instrument or a larger sample.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Plan an investigation of light intensity and photosynthesis. Identify variables and safety precautions. Explain the difference between accuracy, reliability and validity.

Further textbook notes: practical planning and evaluation

A strong investigation begins with a focused question and a testable hypothesis. Identify the independent variable to change, dependent variable to measure and control variables to keep constant. Select suitable apparatus and a range of values; repeat each measurement to identify anomalies and calculate a mean when appropriate.

Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether the method measures what it intends to measure. Accuracy concerns closeness to the true value. Improve reliability with repeats, validity by controlling variables and accuracy by using suitable calibrated equipment. Present data in a table with headings and units, then choose an appropriate graph.

Practise: Evaluate a practical method by identifying a limitation, its effect on results and a realistic improvement.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A valid investigation changes one independent variable, measures the dependent variable and controls other factors. Repeats help identify anomalies and calculate a mean.
Exam-style question (4 marks)

A student tests how light intensity affects photosynthesis. State one variable to measure, two controls and one way to improve reliability.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Measure oxygen bubbles/volume of oxygen per unit time (1); control temperature (1) and carbon dioxide concentration or plant species/length (1); repeat at each light intensity and calculate mean (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Bubbles are a less precise measure than collected gas volume; an improvement may mention a gas syringe or oxygen sensor.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A student investigates the effect of light intensity on photosynthesis using pondweed. State the independent variable, dependent variable and two control variables.

Show answer and mark scheme

Independent variable = light intensity/distance from lamp (1); dependent = oxygen bubbles per minute/oxygen volume (1); controls include temperature and carbon dioxide concentration or pondweed length/species (1).
