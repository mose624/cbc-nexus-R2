11. Thermal physics: particle model and heat transfer

In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions; in liquids, particles remain close but move past one another; in gases, particles are widely spaced and move rapidly and randomly. Temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of particles, while internal energy is the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy.

energy for temperature change: ΔE = m c ΔT
energy for change of state: E = m L

During a change of state, energy changes particle arrangement or separation rather than increasing temperature, so temperature remains constant for a pure substance at constant pressure.

Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions and free electrons in metals. Convection transfers energy by bulk movement of fluids. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and does not require a medium.

Further textbook notes: thermal physics

Particle model: Temperature is related to the average random kinetic energy of particles. Heating a substance may raise its temperature or change its state. During a change of state, energy changes particle arrangement or separation while temperature remains constant for a pure substance at constant pressure.

Specific heat capacity: The energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K is its specific heat capacity c. Use ΔE = mcΔT. Specific latent heat is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg without changing temperature: E = mL.

Heat transfer: Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions and, in metals, mobile electrons. Convection occurs when warmer, less dense fluid rises and cooler fluid sinks, creating circulation. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and can travel through a vacuum. Dark, matt surfaces are generally good absorbers and emitters.

Worked example: Heating 0.50 kg of water by 20°C, using c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹, requires E = mcΔT = 0.50×4200×20 = 42,000 J.

Check your understanding: Explain why temperature stays constant during melting and compare conduction, convection and radiation.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Heating 0.20 kg of water by 10°C with c=4200 J/kg°C requires E=mcΔT=8400 J.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Calculate energy needed to heat 0.50 kg of a material by 8°C if c=900 J/kg°C.

Show model answer and mark allocation

E=mcΔT (1); E=0.50×900×8=3600 J (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use the temperature change, mass in kg and the supplied specific heat capacity.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A 2 kg metal block absorbs 9000 J and its temperature rises by 10 °C. Calculate its specific heat capacity.

Show answer and mark scheme

Use E = mcΔT (1); c = 9000/(2 × 10) (1); c = 450 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (1).
