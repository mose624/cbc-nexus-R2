1. Physical quantities, units and measurement

Physical quantities and SI units

A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as a numerical value and a unit. The SI base units commonly used in school physics include metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current and kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature. Derived quantities are formed from base quantities.

QuantityCommon symbolSI unit
Distance / displacements or xmetre (m)
Timetsecond (s)
Speed / velocityvm s⁻¹
Accelerationam s⁻²
ForceFnewton (N)
Energy / workE / Wjoule (J)
PowerPwatt (W)

Accuracy, precision and errors

Accuracy describes closeness to the accepted value; precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A systematic error shifts readings consistently, while a random error causes unpredictable variation. Repeat measurements, identify anomalous readings and calculate a mean where appropriate. Record a sensible number of significant figures consistent with the measuring instrument.

Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only, such as mass, time, energy and speed. A vector has magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration and force. When adding vectors, directions must be considered; perpendicular components can be combined using Pythagoras’ theorem.

Exam habit: Convert units before substituting. For example, 250 cm = 2.50 m and 3 minutes = 180 s.

Further textbook notes: measuring accurately

Choosing an instrument: Use a ruler for ordinary lengths, vernier calipers for small external or internal diameters, and a micrometer screw gauge for very small thicknesses. Before measuring, check the zero reading. If the instrument has a zero error, apply the correction consistently. Read an analogue scale with the eye directly above the mark to reduce parallax.

Uncertainty: For a scale with the smallest division of 1 mm, a single reading is often recorded with an uncertainty of about ±0.5 mm, unless the instrument or question specifies another convention. For a measurement obtained by subtracting two readings, consider the uncertainty in both readings. Repeat measurements help reveal random variation; they do not automatically remove a systematic error.

Derived units: From speed = distance/time, the unit is m/s. From density = mass/volume, the unit is kg/m³. Check dimensions by substituting base units before accepting a formula.

Worked example: A rectangular block is 4.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 1.5 cm and has mass 48 g. Its volume is 12 cm³ and density is 48/12 = 4.0 g cm⁻³. Convert units if the answer is required in kg/m³.

Check your understanding: Explain why repeated readings improve reliability, distinguish accuracy from precision, and state one way to reduce parallax.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A student measures a metal rod as 12.4 cm. Convert this to metres. 12.4 cm ÷ 100 = 0.124 m. Always convert centimetres to metres before using SI equations.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A ruler has 1 mm divisions. State one way to reduce parallax and give a suitable uncertainty for one reading.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Read the scale with the eye vertically above the mark (1); uncertainty about ±0.5 mm for a single reading, if the scale division is 1 mm (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Award the method point for eye-level viewing. Accept an appropriate instrument-based uncertainty if justified.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A student walks 120 m in 30 s, then 80 m in 10 s. Calculate the total average speed for the journey.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total distance = 120 + 80 = 200 m (1); total time = 30 + 10 = 40 s (1); average speed = 200 ÷ 40 = 5.0 m/s (1).

2. Motion: distance, displacement, speed and acceleration

Distance is the total path length travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position in a stated direction. Speed is the rate of change of distance, while velocity is the rate of change of displacement.

average speed = total distance ÷ total time
average velocity = displacement ÷ time
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time

Worked example

A cyclist increases velocity from 4 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹ in 3 s.

Change in velocity = 10 − 4 = 6 m s⁻¹. Acceleration = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m s⁻².

Distance–time and velocity–time graphs

  • The gradient of a distance–time graph gives speed.
  • A horizontal distance–time line means the object is stationary.
  • The gradient of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement (or distance travelled when velocity remains non-negative).
Common mistake: Do not confuse the height of a velocity–time graph (velocity) with its gradient (acceleration).

Further textbook notes: interpreting motion

Distance and displacement: Distance is scalar and depends on the complete route; displacement is the vector from initial to final position. An athlete completing one lap has travelled a non-zero distance but has zero displacement if they finish at the starting point. Speed is scalar; velocity includes direction.

Acceleration: Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. An object can accelerate while slowing down: its acceleration is then opposite to its velocity. Uniform acceleration means velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.

Graph skills: On a displacement–time graph, gradient gives velocity. On a speed–time graph, area under the graph gives distance. On a velocity–time graph, signed area gives displacement; areas below the time axis count as negative. A curved graph has a changing gradient, so instantaneous velocity is found from the tangent.

Worked example: A runner accelerates uniformly from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s. Acceleration = (8−2)/3 = 2 m/s². If the velocity–time graph is a straight line, displacement during this interval is average velocity × time = ((2+8)/2)×3 = 15 m.

Check your understanding: Sketch a velocity–time graph for an object that moves forward, stops, then reverses direction. Explain the sign of its displacement.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A car changes velocity from 6 m/s to 18 m/s in 4 s. a=(18−6)/4=3 m/s².
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A cyclist travels 150 m in 25 s. Calculate average speed.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Speed = distance/time (1) = 150/25 = 6.0 m/s (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Include the correct unit. Do not use displacement unless direction and net change in position are requested.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A car accelerates uniformly from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 6 s. Calculate its acceleration and state what the gradient of a velocity–time graph represents.

Show answer and mark scheme

a = (v − u)/t = (16 − 4)/6 = 2.0 m/s² (2); gradient represents acceleration (1).

3. Forces, mass, weight and Newton’s laws

A force is an interaction that can change an object’s motion or shape. Forces are vectors and are measured in newtons. The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

resultant force F = mass m × acceleration a

For a constant mass, an unbalanced resultant force causes acceleration in the direction of the resultant force. If the resultant force is zero, an object remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity.

weight W = mass m × gravitational field strength g

Mass is measured in kilograms and does not depend on location. Weight is a force measured in newtons and changes with gravitational field strength. Near Earth’s surface, g is approximately 9.8 N kg⁻¹ (some questions may instruct you to use 10 N kg⁻¹).

Newton’s laws

  1. First law: An object remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted on by a resultant external force.
  2. Second law: Resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, F = ma.
  3. Third law: When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. The forces act on different objects.

Worked example

A 1,200 kg car accelerates at 2.0 m s⁻². Resultant force = 1,200 × 2.0 = 2,400 N.

Friction and air resistance oppose relative motion. At terminal velocity, resistive force balances the driving force or weight component, so resultant force and acceleration are zero.

Further textbook notes: force, inertia and momentum

Free-body diagrams: Isolate one object and draw only forces acting on that object. Weight acts vertically downwards; a normal contact force acts perpendicular to a surface; friction acts parallel to the surface and opposes relative motion or its tendency. Tension acts along a taut string. Do not draw an object's own force on another object on the same free-body diagram.

Equilibrium: If forces balance, the resultant force is zero. This means no acceleration, not necessarily no motion. A moving object can continue at constant velocity when the resultant force is zero. In vertical equilibrium on a level surface, normal reaction may equal weight, provided no other vertical forces act.

Inertia: Mass measures inertia—the resistance to a change in velocity. For the same resultant force, a larger mass has a smaller acceleration. Newton’s third-law pairs are equal and opposite but act on different bodies, so they do not cancel on a single-body diagram.

Worked example: A 5 kg trolley is pulled with 18 N while friction is 3 N. Resultant force = 18−3 = 15 N. Acceleration = 15/5 = 3 m/s².

Check your understanding: Draw a free-body diagram for a falling object before and after it reaches terminal velocity. Explain why acceleration becomes zero at terminal velocity.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 900 kg car accelerates at 1.5 m/s². Resultant force = ma = 900×1.5 = 1350 N.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A 2 kg trolley is pulled with 14 N; friction is 4 N. Calculate acceleration.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Resultant force = 14−4 = 10 N (1); use F=ma (1); a=10/2=5 m/s² (1).

Examiner’s explanation: The key mark is subtracting friction before applying Newton’s second law.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A 5 kg trolley experiences a resultant force of 15 N. Calculate its acceleration. State the effect of doubling the resultant force if mass stays constant.

Show answer and mark scheme

F = ma, so a = 15/5 = 3 m/s² (2); doubling force doubles acceleration to 6 m/s² (1).

4. Moments, pressure and density

Moments

The moment of a force is its turning effect about a pivot. The perpendicular distance is measured from the pivot to the force’s line of action.

moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot

For an object in rotational equilibrium, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment about the same pivot.

Pressure

pressure = force ÷ area

Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻². For the same force, reducing the contact area increases pressure.

pressure in a liquid = depth × density × gravitational field strength

Liquid pressure increases with depth and density. At the same depth in the same stationary liquid, pressure is the same in all directions.

Density

density ρ = mass m ÷ volume V

SI unit: kg m⁻³. To find the volume of an irregular solid, use water displacement: measure the initial water volume, fully submerge the object, then subtract the initial reading from the final reading.

Worked example

A block has mass 540 g and volume 200 cm³. Density = 540 ÷ 200 = 2.7 g cm⁻³ (equivalent to 2,700 kg m⁻³).

Further textbook notes: turning effects, pressure and density

Principle of moments: For an object in rotational equilibrium, total clockwise moment about a pivot equals total anticlockwise moment. The perpendicular distance is the shortest distance from the pivot to the force’s line of action—not necessarily the length of the object. A force through the pivot produces zero moment.

Pressure: Pressure is force per unit area, p = F/A. For the same force, reducing contact area increases pressure. In a liquid at rest, pressure increases with depth because a deeper point supports a taller column of liquid. The pressure difference is Δp = ρgΔh.

Density: Density is mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V. For an irregular solid, determine volume by water displacement. Ensure mass and volume units are compatible; 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.

Worked example: A force of 40 N acts perpendicular to a spanner 0.25 m from its pivot. Moment = 40×0.25 = 10 N m. If the force is applied at an angle, use the perpendicular component or perpendicular distance.

Check your understanding: Explain why a door handle is placed far from its hinges and calculate the pressure produced by a 600 N force on an area of 0.03 m².

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 30 N force acts perpendicular to a spanner 0.20 m from its pivot. Moment = 30×0.20 = 6.0 N m.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A 500 N person stands on an area of 0.025 m². Calculate pressure.

Show model answer and mark allocation

p=F/A (1); p=500/0.025=20,000 Pa (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Pressure uses the area perpendicular to the force; state pascals.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A force of 40 N acts perpendicular to a spanner 0.25 m from a pivot. Calculate the moment. Explain how moving the force to 0.50 m changes it.

Show answer and mark scheme

Moment = force × perpendicular distance = 40 × 0.25 = 10 N m (2); at 0.50 m it becomes 20 N m, twice as large (1).

5. Energy stores, transfers, work and power

Energy can be stored in chemical, kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear stores. Energy is transferred between stores mechanically, electrically, by heating or by radiation. Energy is conserved: it is not created or destroyed, although some may be dissipated to less useful thermal stores.

work done = force × distance moved in direction of force
kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed²
gravitational potential energy change = mass × g × height change
power = energy transferred ÷ time = work done ÷ time

Efficiency

efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%

Efficiency has no unit and cannot exceed 100%. In real devices, some energy is transferred to unwanted stores, commonly by heating and sound.

Worked example

A 2 kg object is lifted vertically by 3 m. Taking g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹, gain in gravitational potential energy = 2 × 9.8 × 3 = 58.8 J.

Further textbook notes: energy, work and power

Energy conservation: Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it is transferred between stores. In a real process, some energy is often dissipated to the surroundings, commonly by heating and sound. The total energy remains conserved even when useful energy decreases.

Work: Work is done when a force causes displacement in its direction. For a constant force parallel to displacement, W = Fd. Work done is measured in joules. Lifting an object increases its gravitational potential energy; accelerating it increases kinetic energy.

Power and efficiency: Power is the rate of energy transfer, P = E/t, and for mechanical work P = W/t. Efficiency = useful output energy (or power) ÷ total input energy (or power) × 100%. Efficiency cannot exceed 100% for an ordinary energy transfer system.

Worked example: A 2 kg object is lifted vertically by 3 m. Taking g = 9.8 N/kg, gain in GPE = mgh = 2×9.8×3 = 58.8 J. If this takes 4 s, useful power = 58.8/4 = 14.7 W.

Check your understanding: Describe the energy transfers in a braking car and explain why its brakes become warm.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 3 kg object is lifted 2 m. Taking g=10 N/kg, gain in GPE=mgh=60 J.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A machine transfers 900 J in 15 s. Calculate its power.

Show model answer and mark allocation

P=E/t (1); P=900/15=60 W (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Power is a rate; answer in watts, not joules.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A machine receives 800 J and transfers 600 J usefully. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage and state one cause of wasted energy.

Show answer and mark scheme

Efficiency = useful output/input × 100 = 600/800 × 100 = 75% (2); some energy is dissipated, for example as heat or sound (1).

6. Momentum and collisions

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. It is a vector, so direction matters.

momentum p = mass m × velocity v

In a closed system with no resultant external force, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it. This is the principle of conservation of momentum.

Impulse

change in momentum = resultant force × time

Increasing the time taken to change momentum reduces the average force for the same momentum change. Crumple zones, airbags and padded sports equipment increase collision time and help reduce injury risk.

Worked example

A 0.15 kg ball moves at 20 m s⁻¹. Its momentum is 0.15 × 20 = 3.0 kg m s⁻¹. If it is brought to rest in 0.10 s, average resultant force magnitude = 3.0 ÷ 0.10 = 30 N.

Choose a positive direction before substituting velocities in collision questions. A velocity in the opposite direction must carry a negative sign.

Further textbook notes: momentum and impulse

Momentum: Momentum p = mv is a vector. Its direction is the direction of velocity. In a closed system, total momentum is conserved when the resultant external force or external impulse is negligible. Choose a positive direction before substituting values; objects moving in the opposite direction have negative velocity.

Impulse: Impulse equals change in momentum, Ft = Δp, for a constant resultant force. On a force–time graph, the area under the graph represents impulse. Increasing collision time for the same change in momentum reduces average force. This explains the use of airbags, seat belts, crumple zones and padded sports equipment.

Collision types: Momentum is conserved in both elastic and inelastic collisions in an isolated system. Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision; in an inelastic collision some kinetic energy is transferred to internal energy, sound or deformation.

Worked example: A 0.20 kg ball moving at 5 m/s is brought to rest. Change in momentum = 0−(0.20×5) = −1.0 kg m/s. If stopping takes 0.05 s, average force = −1.0/0.05 = −20 N; the negative sign indicates the force opposes the initial motion.

Check your understanding: Explain why a longer stopping time reduces injury risk and solve a one-dimensional two-object collision using signed velocities.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 0.5 kg ball moves at 8 m/s. Momentum p=mv=4 kg m/s in its direction of travel.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A 0.15 kg ball moving at 10 m/s is stopped in 0.05 s. Calculate average resultant force.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Initial momentum=0.15×10=1.5 kg m/s (1); change in momentum=0−1.5=−1.5 kg m/s (1); F=Δp/Δt=−1.5/0.05=−30 N (1).

Examiner’s explanation: The negative sign means the force acts opposite to the initial motion. Magnitude is 30 N.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A 0.20 kg ball moving at 10 m/s is brought to rest. Calculate its change in momentum and explain why increasing stopping time reduces force.

Show answer and mark scheme

Initial momentum = 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 kg m/s; final = 0, so change = −2.0 kg m/s (2); for the same momentum change, a longer time gives a smaller force (1).

7. Waves and the electromagnetic spectrum

A wave transfers energy and information without an overall transfer of matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to it. Sound in air is longitudinal; electromagnetic waves are transverse.

wave speed v = frequency f × wavelength λ

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz), wavelength in metres (m), and wave speed in m s⁻¹. Period T is the time for one complete oscillation: T = 1 ÷ f.

Reflection changes direction at a boundary; refraction is a change in direction caused by a change in wave speed; diffraction is spreading as waves pass through a gap or around an obstacle. Diffraction is most noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.

Electromagnetic spectrum

In order of increasing frequency: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can damage living tissue; exposure should be controlled.

Further textbook notes: wave behaviour

Wave quantities: Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium. Wavelength λ is the distance between adjacent points in phase. Frequency f is the number of complete waves passing a point each second; period T is the time for one cycle, with T = 1/f. Wave speed v = fλ.

Wave types: In a transverse wave, oscillations are perpendicular to energy transfer; in a longitudinal wave, oscillations are parallel. Sound is longitudinal in air and requires a medium. Electromagnetic waves are transverse and can travel through a vacuum.

Wave interactions: Reflection changes direction at a boundary. Refraction occurs when wave speed changes on entering another medium, causing a change in direction unless it enters normally. Diffraction is spreading around an obstacle or through a gap; it is more noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.

Electromagnetic spectrum: In vacuum all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.0×10⁸ m/s. From radio to gamma, frequency and photon energy increase while wavelength decreases. Uses and hazards depend on frequency and intensity.

Worked example: A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 0.80 m. Speed = fλ = 50×0.80 = 40 m/s.

Check your understanding: Compare transverse and longitudinal waves, and explain why diffraction is greatest when a gap is close to one wavelength wide.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A wave has frequency 25 Hz and wavelength 0.40 m. v=fλ=10 m/s.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A wave travels at 12 m/s with frequency 4 Hz. Calculate wavelength.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Use v=fλ (1); λ=12/4=3.0 m (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Rearrange the wave equation correctly and give metres.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A wave has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength 0.8 m. Calculate its speed. State what happens to wavelength if speed stays constant and frequency doubles.

Show answer and mark scheme

v = fλ = 5 × 0.8 = 4 m/s (2); wavelength halves to 0.4 m (1).

8. Electricity, charge and circuits

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. Conventional current is defined as the direction positive charge would flow; in metal wires, electrons drift in the opposite direction.

charge Q = current I × time t
potential difference V = energy transferred ÷ charge
resistance R = potential difference V ÷ current I

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. A resistor converts electrical energy mainly into thermal energy.

Series and parallel circuits

  • Series: current is the same through every component; supply potential difference is shared between components; total resistance is the sum of resistances.
  • Parallel: potential difference is the same across each branch; current divides between branches; total current equals the sum of branch currents.
electrical power P = potential difference V × current I
electrical energy E = power P × time t

Worked example

A 12 V supply drives a current of 0.5 A. Resistance = 12 ÷ 0.5 = 24 Ω; power = 12 × 0.5 = 6 W.

Further textbook notes: electric circuits

Charge and current: Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t. A current of 1 A means 1 C of charge passes a point each second. In a series circuit, current is the same at every point. At a junction, total current entering equals total current leaving.

Potential difference and resistance: Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V = E/Q. Resistance R = V/I. For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. A filament lamp is non-ohmic because its temperature and resistance rise as current increases.

Series and parallel: In series, supply potential difference is shared and total resistance is the sum of resistances. In parallel, each branch has the same potential difference; adding branches reduces total resistance. Electrical power can be calculated using P = IV, P = I²R or P = V²/R.

Worked example: A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Current = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A. Power = VI = 12×3 = 36 W.

Safety: A fuse melts when current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit. Earthing provides a low-resistance path for fault current. Never work on live mains circuits.

Check your understanding: Explain why household appliances are connected in parallel and calculate the current through a 6 Ω resistor connected to 9 V.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 6 Ω resistor is connected to 12 V. I=V/R=2 A; P=VI=24 W.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A 3 Ω resistor is connected to a 9 V supply. Calculate current and power.

Show model answer and mark allocation

I=V/R (1); I=9/3=3 A (1); P=VI=9×3=27 W (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use the potential difference across that resistor. Power is measured in watts.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Two resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in series to a 20 V supply. Calculate the current and state the current through each resistor.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total resistance = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω (1); I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A (1); series current is 2 A through each resistor (1).

9. Magnetism and electromagnetism

Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract. Magnetic field direction is defined as the direction a north pole would move. Field lines go from north to south outside a magnet; closer lines indicate a stronger field.

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A coil or solenoid produces a field similar to that of a bar magnet. The field strength of an electromagnet can be increased by increasing current, increasing the number of turns per unit length, or inserting a suitable magnetic core.

The motor effect is the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. The direction depends on current and magnetic field direction. Electromagnetic induction occurs when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or the magnetic flux linking a coil changes; the induced potential difference can drive a current in a complete circuit.

For induction questions, state what changes (field strength, motion, angle or number of turns) and connect it to the size of the induced potential difference.

Further textbook notes: magnetic fields and induction

Magnetic fields: A magnetic field is a region where a magnetic pole or moving charge experiences a force. Field lines form continuous loops; outside a bar magnet they run from north to south. The field is stronger where lines are closer together. Magnetic materials include iron, nickel and cobalt.

Electromagnets: A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A solenoid produces a field similar to a bar magnet; inserting a soft iron core strengthens it. Field strength can be increased by increasing current, adding turns per unit length or using a suitable core.

Motor effect: A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force. The direction depends on current and field direction; Fleming’s left-hand rule can help when the syllabus requires it. Reversing either current or magnetic field reverses the force.

Electromagnetic induction: An emf is induced when magnetic flux linkage changes. Faster relative motion, a stronger magnetic field or more coil turns can increase induced emf. A generator uses induction to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy; a transformer changes alternating potential difference using electromagnetic induction.

Worked example: A coil is moved into a magnetic field. If it is moved twice as quickly, the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage increases, so the induced emf is larger (assuming other factors remain constant).

Check your understanding: State two ways to increase the strength of an electromagnet and explain how a generator produces an alternating output.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: An electromagnet becomes stronger if current or the number of turns is increased; a soft iron core also strengthens it.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

State two ways to increase the strength of a solenoid and explain electromagnetic induction.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Increase current (1); increase turns per unit length or add a soft iron core (1); changing magnetic flux linkage induces an emf (1).

10. Radioactivity and nuclear physics

Atoms contain a small, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random; the activity of a source is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq).

RadiationNaturePenetration / ionisation
Alpha (α)Helium nucleusStrongly ionising; stopped by paper or a few cm of air
Beta (β⁻)Fast electronModerately ionising; reduced by thin aluminium
Gamma (γ)Electromagnetic waveWeakly ionising; highly penetrating, reduced by thick lead or concrete

Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei, or activity, to fall to half its initial value. It is not the time for all nuclei to decay.

remaining activity after n half-lives = initial activity × (½)ⁿ

Worked example

A source has activity 800 Bq and a half-life of 5 days. After 15 days, three half-lives have passed: 800 × (½)³ = 100 Bq.

Background radiation comes from natural and human-made sources. Reduce unnecessary exposure by limiting time near a source, increasing distance and using appropriate shielding.

Further textbook notes: nuclear structure and radiation

Atomic nucleus: Proton number Z identifies the element; nucleon number A is the total number of protons and neutrons. Neutron number = A−Z. Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Nuclear notation places A above and Z below the element symbol.

Radioactive emissions: Alpha particles are helium nuclei and are strongly ionising but weakly penetrating. Beta-minus radiation consists of fast electrons and has moderate ionising and penetrating ability. Gamma radiation is electromagnetic, weakly ionising and highly penetrating. The exact shielding and hazard depend on the source and exposure.

Half-life: Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei or activity to fall to half its initial value. Radioactive decay is random for an individual nucleus but predictable for a large sample. After n half-lives, remaining activity is initial activity × (1/2)ⁿ.

Safety: Reduce exposure time, increase distance and use suitable shielding. Do not handle sources directly. Background radiation must be considered when measuring activity.

Worked example: A source has activity 800 Bq and half-life 6 hours. After 18 hours, three half-lives have passed: 800→400→200→100 Bq.

Check your understanding: Compare alpha, beta and gamma by charge, relative ionisation and penetration, and calculate activity after a stated number of half-lives.

11. Thermal physics: particle model and heat transfer

In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions; in liquids, particles remain close but move past one another; in gases, particles are widely spaced and move rapidly and randomly. Temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of particles, while internal energy is the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy.

energy for temperature change: ΔE = m c ΔT
energy for change of state: E = m L

During a change of state, energy changes particle arrangement or separation rather than increasing temperature, so temperature remains constant for a pure substance at constant pressure.

Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions and free electrons in metals. Convection transfers energy by bulk movement of fluids. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and does not require a medium.

Further textbook notes: thermal physics

Particle model: Temperature is related to the average random kinetic energy of particles. Heating a substance may raise its temperature or change its state. During a change of state, energy changes particle arrangement or separation while temperature remains constant for a pure substance at constant pressure.

Specific heat capacity: The energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K is its specific heat capacity c. Use ΔE = mcΔT. Specific latent heat is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg without changing temperature: E = mL.

Heat transfer: Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions and, in metals, mobile electrons. Convection occurs when warmer, less dense fluid rises and cooler fluid sinks, creating circulation. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and can travel through a vacuum. Dark, matt surfaces are generally good absorbers and emitters.

Worked example: Heating 0.50 kg of water by 20°C, using c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹, requires E = mcΔT = 0.50×4200×20 = 42,000 J.

Check your understanding: Explain why temperature stays constant during melting and compare conduction, convection and radiation.

12. Astrophysics and space (where included in the specification)

Stars form when gas and dust collapse under gravity. During the main sequence, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium and energy is released. A star’s later evolution depends mainly on its initial mass. Low- and high-mass stars follow different end stages.

The Solar System contains the Sun, planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets. The apparent brightness of a star depends on its luminosity and distance. Redshift in light from distant galaxies is evidence that many galaxies are moving away; observations support the expansion of the Universe.

Check whether astrophysics is included in your particular Pearson qualification and specification before treating this chapter as examinable content.

Further textbook notes: stars and the expanding Universe

Life cycle of a star: Gravity causes a cloud of gas and dust (nebula) to collapse. As its core heats, nuclear fusion can begin and a stable main-sequence star forms. In the main sequence, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing energy. The later stages depend mainly on the star’s initial mass.

Stellar evolution: A Sun-like star expands into a red giant, sheds outer layers and leaves a white dwarf. A much more massive star can become a red supergiant, undergo a supernova and leave a neutron star or black hole. These are broad pathways; the exact outcome depends on mass and later evolution.

Redshift and expansion: Light from many distant galaxies is redshifted, indicating that their wavelengths are stretched. The general relationship between greater distance and greater recession speed supports the conclusion that the Universe is expanding. The age and scale of the Universe are inferred from astronomical observations and models.

Orbits: A satellite remains in orbit because its velocity is tangential while gravity continually provides centripetal acceleration towards the body it orbits. A stable orbit does not mean gravity is absent.

Worked example: If a galaxy’s spectral line is observed at a longer wavelength than its laboratory wavelength, the light is redshifted. Explain that the wavelength has increased and relate this observation to recession.

Check your understanding: Compare the likely final stages of low-mass and high-mass stars and explain why a satellite does not fly off in a straight line.
