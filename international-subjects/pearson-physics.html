Pearson Edexcel Physics — Topic Notes

Open each chapter to read its lesson on this page. These foundational notes cover common International GCSE Physics concepts; use your current Pearson specification to confirm the exact required depth and options.

How to study: Read the explanation, copy the equations with units, attempt the worked example, then answer the review questions without looking.

1. Physical quantities, units and measurement

Physical quantities and SI units

A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as a numerical value and a unit. The SI base units commonly used in school physics include metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current and kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature. Derived quantities are formed from base quantities.

Accuracy, precision and errors

Accuracy describes closeness to the accepted value; precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A systematic error shifts readings consistently, while a random error causes unpredictable variation. Repeat measurements, identify anomalous readings and calculate a mean where appropriate. Record a sensible number of significant figures consistent with the measuring instrument.

Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only, such as mass, time, energy and speed. A vector has magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration and force. When adding vectors, directions must be considered; perpendicular components can be combined using Pythagoras’ theorem.

Exam habit: Convert units before substituting. For example, 250 cm = 2.50 m and 3 minutes = 180 s.
2. Motion: distance, displacement, speed and acceleration

Distance is the total path length travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position in a stated direction. Speed is the rate of change of distance, while velocity is the rate of change of displacement.

average speed = total distance ÷ total time
average velocity = displacement ÷ time
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time

Worked example

A cyclist increases velocity from 4 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹ in 3 s.

Change in velocity = 10 − 4 = 6 m s⁻¹. Acceleration = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m s⁻².

Distance–time and velocity–time graphs

  • The gradient of a distance–time graph gives speed.
  • A horizontal distance–time line means the object is stationary.
  • The gradient of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement (or distance travelled when velocity remains non-negative).
Common mistake: Do not confuse the height of a velocity–time graph (velocity) with its gradient (acceleration).
3. Forces, mass, weight and Newton’s laws

A force is an interaction that can change an object’s motion or shape. Forces are vectors and are measured in newtons. The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

resultant force F = mass m × acceleration a

For a constant mass, an unbalanced resultant force causes acceleration in the direction of the resultant force. If the resultant force is zero, an object remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity.

weight W = mass m × gravitational field strength g

Mass is measured in kilograms and does not depend on location. Weight is a force measured in newtons and changes with gravitational field strength. Near Earth’s surface, g is approximately 9.8 N kg⁻¹ (some questions may instruct you to use 10 N kg⁻¹).

Newton’s laws

  1. First law: An object remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted on by a resultant external force.
  2. Second law: Resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, F = ma.
  3. Third law: When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. The forces act on different objects.

Worked example

A 1,200 kg car accelerates at 2.0 m s⁻². Resultant force = 1,200 × 2.0 = 2,400 N.

Friction and air resistance oppose relative motion. At terminal velocity, resistive force balances the driving force or weight component, so resultant force and acceleration are zero.

4. Moments, pressure and density

Moments

The moment of a force is its turning effect about a pivot. The perpendicular distance is measured from the pivot to the force’s line of action.

moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot

For an object in rotational equilibrium, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment about the same pivot.

Pressure

pressure = force ÷ area

Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻². For the same force, reducing the contact area increases pressure.

pressure in a liquid = depth × density × gravitational field strength

Liquid pressure increases with depth and density. At the same depth in the same stationary liquid, pressure is the same in all directions.

Density

density ρ = mass m ÷ volume V

SI unit: kg m⁻³. To find the volume of an irregular solid, use water displacement: measure the initial water volume, fully submerge the object, then subtract the initial reading from the final reading.

Worked example

A block has mass 540 g and volume 200 cm³. Density = 540 ÷ 200 = 2.7 g cm⁻³ (equivalent to 2,700 kg m⁻³).

5. Energy stores, transfers, work and power

Energy can be stored in chemical, kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear stores. Energy is transferred between stores mechanically, electrically, by heating or by radiation. Energy is conserved: it is not created or destroyed, although some may be dissipated to less useful thermal stores.

work done = force × distance moved in direction of force
kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed²
gravitational potential energy change = mass × g × height change
power = energy transferred ÷ time = work done ÷ time

Efficiency

efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%

Efficiency has no unit and cannot exceed 100%. In real devices, some energy is transferred to unwanted stores, commonly by heating and sound.

Worked example

A 2 kg object is lifted vertically by 3 m. Taking g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹, gain in gravitational potential energy = 2 × 9.8 × 3 = 58.8 J.

6. Momentum and collisions

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. It is a vector, so direction matters.

momentum p = mass m × velocity v

In a closed system with no resultant external force, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it. This is the principle of conservation of momentum.

Impulse

change in momentum = resultant force × time

Increasing the time taken to change momentum reduces the average force for the same momentum change. Crumple zones, airbags and padded sports equipment increase collision time and help reduce injury risk.

Worked example

A 0.15 kg ball moves at 20 m s⁻¹. Its momentum is 0.15 × 20 = 3.0 kg m s⁻¹. If it is brought to rest in 0.10 s, average resultant force magnitude = 3.0 ÷ 0.10 = 30 N.

Choose a positive direction before substituting velocities in collision questions. A velocity in the opposite direction must carry a negative sign.
7. Waves and the electromagnetic spectrum

A wave transfers energy and information without an overall transfer of matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to it. Sound in air is longitudinal; electromagnetic waves are transverse.

wave speed v = frequency f × wavelength λ

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz), wavelength in metres (m), and wave speed in m s⁻¹. Period T is the time for one complete oscillation: T = 1 ÷ f.

Reflection changes direction at a boundary; refraction is a change in direction caused by a change in wave speed; diffraction is spreading as waves pass through a gap or around an obstacle. Diffraction is most noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.

Electromagnetic spectrum

In order of increasing frequency: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can damage living tissue; exposure should be controlled.

8. Electricity, charge and circuits

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. Conventional current is defined as the direction positive charge would flow; in metal wires, electrons drift in the opposite direction.

charge Q = current I × time t
potential difference V = energy transferred ÷ charge
resistance R = potential difference V ÷ current I

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. A resistor converts electrical energy mainly into thermal energy.

Series and parallel circuits

  • Series: current is the same through every component; supply potential difference is shared between components; total resistance is the sum of resistances.
  • Parallel: potential difference is the same across each branch; current divides between branches; total current equals the sum of branch currents.
electrical power P = potential difference V × current I
electrical energy E = power P × time t

Worked example

A 12 V supply drives a current of 0.5 A. Resistance = 12 ÷ 0.5 = 24 Ω; power = 12 × 0.5 = 6 W.

9. Magnetism and electromagnetism

Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract. Magnetic field direction is defined as the direction a north pole would move. Field lines go from north to south outside a magnet; closer lines indicate a stronger field.

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A coil or solenoid produces a field similar to that of a bar magnet. The field strength of an electromagnet can be increased by increasing current, increasing the number of turns per unit length, or inserting a suitable magnetic core.

The motor effect is the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. The direction depends on current and magnetic field direction. Electromagnetic induction occurs when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or the magnetic flux linking a coil changes; the induced potential difference can drive a current in a complete circuit.

For induction questions, state what changes (field strength, motion, angle or number of turns) and connect it to the size of the induced potential difference.
10. Radioactivity and nuclear physics

Atoms contain a small, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random; the activity of a source is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq).

RadiationNaturePenetration / ionisation
Alpha (α)Helium nucleusStrongly ionising; stopped by paper or a few cm of air
Beta (β⁻)Fast electronModerately ionising; reduced by thin aluminium
Gamma (γ)Electromagnetic waveWeakly ionising; highly penetrating, reduced by thick lead or concrete

Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei, or activity, to fall to half its initial value. It is not the time for all nuclei to decay.

remaining activity after n half-lives = initial activity × (½)ⁿ

Worked example

A source has activity 800 Bq and a half-life of 5 days. After 15 days, three half-lives have passed: 800 × (½)³ = 100 Bq.

Background radiation comes from natural and human-made sources. Reduce unnecessary exposure by limiting time near a source, increasing distance and using appropriate shielding.

11. Thermal physics: particle model and heat transfer

In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions; in liquids, particles remain close but move past one another; in gases, particles are widely spaced and move rapidly and randomly. Temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of particles, while internal energy is the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy.

energy for temperature change: ΔE = m c ΔT
energy for change of state: E = m L

During a change of state, energy changes particle arrangement or separation rather than increasing temperature, so temperature remains constant for a pure substance at constant pressure.

Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions and free electrons in metals. Convection transfers energy by bulk movement of fluids. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and does not require a medium.

12. Astrophysics and space (where included in the specification)

Stars form when gas and dust collapse under gravity. During the main sequence, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium and energy is released. A star’s later evolution depends mainly on its initial mass. Low- and high-mass stars follow different end stages.

The Solar System contains the Sun, planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets. The apparent brightness of a star depends on its luminosity and distance. Redshift in light from distant galaxies is evidence that many galaxies are moving away; observations support the expansion of the Universe.

Check whether astrophysics is included in your particular Pearson qualification and specification before treating this chapter as examinable content.

Self-check questions

  1. State the SI unit of acceleration.
  2. A runner travels 150 m in 12 s. Calculate average speed.
  3. Explain why an object moving at constant velocity has zero resultant force.
  4. Calculate the weight of a 5 kg mass using g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹.
  5. State the equation linking density, mass and volume.
  6. Describe the energy transfer when a falling object speeds up.
  7. Calculate the momentum of a 0.4 kg trolley moving at 3 m s⁻¹.
  8. State two differences between transverse and longitudinal waves.
  9. A 6 V battery drives 2 A through a circuit. Calculate resistance and power.
  10. Explain what is meant by half-life.

Answers: 1) m s⁻². 2) 12.5 m s⁻¹. 3) Forces are balanced; no acceleration. 4) 49 N. 5) ρ = m/V. 6) Gravitational potential store decreases and kinetic store increases, with some energy dissipated. 7) 1.2 kg m s⁻¹. 8) Oscillation perpendicular versus parallel to energy transfer. 9) R = 3 Ω; P = 12 W. 10) Time for undecayed nuclei/activity to halve.
