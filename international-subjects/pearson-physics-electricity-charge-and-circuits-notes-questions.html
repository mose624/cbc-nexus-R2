8. Electricity, charge and circuits

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. Conventional current is defined as the direction positive charge would flow; in metal wires, electrons drift in the opposite direction.

charge Q = current I × time t
potential difference V = energy transferred ÷ charge
resistance R = potential difference V ÷ current I

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. A resistor converts electrical energy mainly into thermal energy.

Series and parallel circuits

  • Series: current is the same through every component; supply potential difference is shared between components; total resistance is the sum of resistances.
  • Parallel: potential difference is the same across each branch; current divides between branches; total current equals the sum of branch currents.
electrical power P = potential difference V × current I
electrical energy E = power P × time t

Worked example

A 12 V supply drives a current of 0.5 A. Resistance = 12 ÷ 0.5 = 24 Ω; power = 12 × 0.5 = 6 W.

Further textbook notes: electric circuits

Charge and current: Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t. A current of 1 A means 1 C of charge passes a point each second. In a series circuit, current is the same at every point. At a junction, total current entering equals total current leaving.

Potential difference and resistance: Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V = E/Q. Resistance R = V/I. For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. A filament lamp is non-ohmic because its temperature and resistance rise as current increases.

Series and parallel: In series, supply potential difference is shared and total resistance is the sum of resistances. In parallel, each branch has the same potential difference; adding branches reduces total resistance. Electrical power can be calculated using P = IV, P = I²R or P = V²/R.

Worked example: A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Current = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A. Power = VI = 12×3 = 36 W.

Safety: A fuse melts when current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit. Earthing provides a low-resistance path for fault current. Never work on live mains circuits.

Check your understanding: Explain why household appliances are connected in parallel and calculate the current through a 6 Ω resistor connected to 9 V.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A 6 Ω resistor is connected to 12 V. I=V/R=2 A; P=VI=24 W.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A 3 Ω resistor is connected to a 9 V supply. Calculate current and power.

Show model answer and mark allocation

I=V/R (1); I=9/3=3 A (1); P=VI=9×3=27 W (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use the potential difference across that resistor. Power is measured in watts.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Two resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in series to a 20 V supply. Calculate the current and state the current through each resistor.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total resistance = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω (1); I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A (1); series current is 2 A through each resistor (1).
