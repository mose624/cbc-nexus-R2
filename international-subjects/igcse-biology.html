TOPIC 1 • CORE + SUPPLEMENT

1. Characteristics and classification of living organisms

Learning outcomes: describe the seven characteristics of living organisms; explain why and how organisms are classified; define species; use binomial names and dichotomous keys; compare the five kingdoms; classify vertebrates, arthropods and major plant groups; and explain how DNA evidence can show evolutionary relationships (Supplement).

1.1 Characteristics of living organisms

Living organisms carry out life processes. The seven characteristics are often remembered using MRS GREN. The terms describe biological processes, not simply visible actions.

M — MovementAn action by an organism or part of it that changes position or place. Plants show movement, for example shoots growing towards light.
R — RespirationChemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism. It is not the same as breathing.
S — SensitivityThe ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment, such as light, temperature or chemicals.
G — GrowthA permanent increase in size and dry mass, resulting from an increase in cell number, cell size or both.
R — ReproductionProcesses that make more organisms of the same kind. Reproduction may be sexual or asexual.
E — ExcretionRemoval of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements, such as carbon dioxide and urea.
N — NutritionTaking in materials for energy, growth and development. Plants make organic nutrients by photosynthesis; animals obtain food by feeding.
Common examination trap: excretion is not egestion. Excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells; egestion removes undigested food from the alimentary canal. Respiration is a chemical process in cells and is not simply the movement of air into and out of lungs.

1.2 Why organisms are classified

Classification is the organisation of organisms into groups using shared features. It makes the huge diversity of living things easier to identify, study and communicate about. A useful classification system groups organisms according to similarities that reflect their relationships.

Species: a group of organisms that can reproduce with one another to produce fertile offspring. Members of the same species share many characteristics, although individuals may show variation.

1.3 The binomial system of naming

Each species is given a two-part scientific name. The first word is the genus and begins with a capital letter; the second is the species name and begins with a lower-case letter. Both words are written in italics when typed, or underlined separately when handwritten.

1.7 Viruses

Viruses are not placed in any of the five kingdoms. A virus consists of genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat. Viruses can reproduce only inside living host cells, using the host cell's machinery. They are not cells and do not carry out all seven life processes independently.

1.8 Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key identifies an organism by offering two contrasting statements at each step. Choose the statement that matches the specimen and follow the instruction until you reach an identification. Each pair should use clear, observable features that do not overlap.

1.9 DNA and evolutionary relationships (Supplement)

DNA contains a sequence of bases. When the DNA base sequences of two organisms are compared, a greater similarity generally indicates a more recent common ancestor and a closer evolutionary relationship. Classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships, not just superficial resemblance. Organisms may look similar because they live in similar environments, even when they are not closely related.

Worked example: using a key

Question: An arthropod has eight legs, no antennae and two main body regions. Which group does it belong to?

Reasoning: Eight legs indicate four pairs. The absence of antennae and the two main body regions support the identification. Answer: arachnid.

Practical and investigation skills

  • Observe specimens, photographs or models carefully and record visible features before naming an organism.
  • Use a hand lens or microscope safely; keep specimens in suitable containers and do not handle unknown organisms.
  • Construct a dichotomous key using observable, mutually exclusive paired statements.
  • Repeat identifications with another learner and resolve disagreements by checking the stated features.
  • When comparing classification using DNA data, use the same gene/region and compare equivalent base positions.

A cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of a living organism. Cells contain structures that perform particular functions. 2.3 Levels of organisation

In multicellular organisms, cells work together in an organised hierarchy:

CellThe basic unit of life, for example a muscle cell.
TissueA group of similar cells working together, for example muscle tissue.
OrganA structure made of different tissues working together, for example the heart.
Organ systemA group of organs working together, for example the circulatory system.
OrganismAn individual living thing, for example a human.

2.4 Size of specimens and magnification

Magnification compares the size of an image with the actual size of the specimen. It has no unit because it is a ratio of two lengths measured in the same unit.

Formula to learn:
Magnification = image size ÷ actual size
Rearrange when needed: image size = magnification × actual size; actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

Unit conversion: 1 millimetre (mm) = 1000 micrometres (µm). To convert mm to µm, multiply by 1000. To convert µm to mm, divide by 1000. Convert both image and actual size to the same unit before calculating.

Worked example 1: calculate magnification

A drawing of a cell is 36 mm long. The actual cell is 0.12 mm long. Calculate the magnification.

Step 1: Write the formula: magnification = image size ÷ actual size.

Step 2: Substitute: 36 mm ÷ 0.12 mm = 300.

Answer: ×300. The mm units cancel, so magnification has no unit.

Worked example 2: convert units first

An image measures 24 mm. The actual specimen measures 80 µm. Calculate magnification.

Convert 24 mm to micrometres: 24 × 1000 = 24 000 µm. Then 24 000 ÷ 80 = ×300.

Worked example 3: calculate actual size

A bacterium is shown at ×1500 magnification. Its image length is 12 mm. Actual size = 12 mm ÷ 1500 = 0.008 mm. Convert to micrometres: 0.008 × 1000 = 8 µm.

Worked example 4: calculate image size

A cell is 25 µm long and is drawn at ×400. Convert actual size to mm: 25 µm = 0.025 mm. Image size = 400 × 0.025 = 10 mm.

2.5 Practical skills: observing cells

  • Prepare a thin specimen so light can pass through it. Use an appropriate stain when needed to increase contrast; stains may kill cells.
  • Place the specimen on a clean slide, add a drop of water or suitable mounting liquid, and lower a coverslip carefully to reduce air bubbles.
  • Start with the low-power objective, focus using the coarse adjustment, then use fine adjustment for a sharp image. Use fine focus at high power.
  • Do not allow the objective lens to press against the slide. Handle glass slides and coverslips carefully.
  • Draw biological diagrams with clear single lines, no shading, and labels that touch the correct structure. Include a title and magnification or a scale bar when required.
  • When measuring a specimen image, measure the longest clearly visible dimension and record the unit.

2.6 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Cell wall and cell membrane are different structures: the wall provides support; the membrane controls exchange.
  • Plant cells also have mitochondria and ribosomes. Chloroplasts are present only in photosynthetic cells, not every plant cell.
  • Not all plant cells have chloroplasts; root cells, for example, are generally not photosynthetic.
  • Bacteria are cells, but they are not eukaryotic and have no nucleus.
  • Do not confuse tissue with organ: an organ contains different tissues working together.
  • In magnification calculations, convert to matching units before dividing. Never attach mm or µm to the final magnification.

3. Movement into and out of cells

Learning outcomes: explain diffusion, osmosis and active transport; relate each process to concentration gradients and cell membranes; explain why these processes matter in living organisms; investigate factors affecting diffusion and the effects of osmosis on plant tissues; and interpret practical results.

3.1 The cell membrane and movement of substances

The cell membrane forms the boundary between a cell and its surroundings. It is selectively permeable: some substances cross it more easily than others. Cells exchange substances with their environment to obtain useful materials and remove waste. Three important processes are diffusion, osmosis and active transport.

Do not confuse the three processes: diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient; osmosis is the net movement of water through a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential; active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration.

3.2 Diffusion (Core)

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement. Molecules and ions have kinetic energy and move randomly. Even though particles move in both directions, there is an overall movement from the region where they are more concentrated to the region where they are less concentrated.

The energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of the random movement of molecules and ions. Diffusion is a passive process: the cell does not use ATP to make particles move down the gradient.

Examples and importance of diffusion

  • Gas exchange in the lungs: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where its concentration is higher, into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.
  • Gas exchange in leaves: carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through stomata for photosynthesis; oxygen can diffuse out.
  • Respiring tissues: oxygen diffuses from blood into cells, while carbon dioxide produced by respiration diffuses from cells into the blood.
  • Digestion and absorption: some digested soluble molecules move across membranes down their concentration gradients.

Factors affecting the rate of diffusion

FactorEffect on rateReason
Concentration gradientA steeper gradient increases the rate.There is a greater difference in concentration, so there is greater net movement per unit time.
TemperatureHigher temperature generally increases the rate.Particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, increasing the rate of spreading.

Exam application: alveoli and many exchange surfaces have a large surface area, thin walls and a good blood supply. These features help maintain a steep concentration gradient and a short diffusion distance.

3.3 Osmosis (Core and Supplement)

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (a more dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (a more concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane. A partially permeable membrane allows water molecules to pass through but restricts at least some dissolved solute particles.

At IGCSE level, remember that water moves in both directions across the membrane. Osmosis describes the net movement. The water potential explanation is Supplement content; the core description focuses on water diffusing through a partially permeable membrane.

Why water is important as a solvent

  • Digestion: many enzymes act on dissolved substances; soluble products can be transported in watery fluids.
  • Transport: blood plasma and plant sap carry dissolved nutrients, ions, hormones and waste products.
  • Excretion: nitrogenous waste such as urea is transported in solution and removed in urine.

Osmosis in plant cells (Supplement)

Surrounding solutionWater movementEffect on plant cell
More dilute than cell sap (higher water potential)Water enters the cell by osmosis.Vacuole expands; cytoplasm presses against the cell wall; the cell becomes turgid. The outward pressure is turgor pressure. The cell wall prevents the cell from bursting.

Investigating osmosis in plant tissue

A common investigation uses equal-sized potato cylinders placed in different sucrose or salt concentrations. Measure their initial mass, immerse them for the same time, remove them, gently blot away surface liquid and measure the final mass.

VariableHow to control or measure it
IndependentConcentration of sucrose solution; use a range of known concentrations.
DependentChange in mass or percentage change in mass of each potato cylinder.
ControlledPotato source, cylinder dimensions, solution volume, immersion time, temperature and blotting method.

Percentage change in mass = ((final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass) × 100. A positive result means mass increased; a negative result means mass decreased. At the concentration where percentage change is approximately zero, there is no net movement of water into or out of the tissue under those conditions.

Worked example: percentage change in mass

A potato cylinder has an initial mass of 5.0 g and a final mass of 5.8 g.

Change in mass = 5.8 − 5.0 = 0.8 g.

Percentage change = (0.8 ÷ 5.0) × 100 = +16%. The positive sign shows that the potato gained mass because water entered its cells by osmosis.

Dialysis tubing investigation

Dialysis tubing can model a partially permeable membrane. Soak and rinse the tubing as instructed, tie one end, add a measured volume of concentrated sucrose solution, remove air, tie the other end and rinse the outside. Record its initial mass, place it in distilled water for a fixed time, blot it consistently and record its final mass. An increase in mass indicates net water entry by osmosis. Use eye protection where required, clean spills promptly and do not taste any laboratory materials.

3.4 Active transport (Core and Supplement)

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from respiration. The energy is supplied by ATP made during respiration. In Supplement content, explain that protein carrier molecules in the membrane bind and move particular ions or molecules across the membrane.

Example — mineral ion uptake by root hair cells: mineral ions in the soil solution may be at a lower concentration than inside a root hair cell. The cell can absorb these ions against the concentration gradient using carrier proteins and energy from respiration. The ions are needed for healthy plant growth, including the synthesis of proteins and chlorophyll.

3.5 Practical planning, data and evaluation

  • Repeat measurements and calculate a mean to improve reliability and identify anomalous results.
  • Use equal-sized tissue pieces and the same immersion time, temperature and solution volume when comparing osmosis conditions.
  • Blot samples in the same way before weighing; surface solution left on a sample can distort its mass.
  • Use a suitable range of concentrations. A graph of percentage change in mass against solution concentration can show the concentration at which there is no net mass change.
  • For diffusion investigations, change one factor at a time and keep other factors constant. For example, compare equal-sized agar blocks at different temperatures or use different surface-area-to-volume ratios.
  • State the independent variable, dependent variable and important control variables clearly. Include units and repeat readings.

3.6 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Diffusion does not stop when concentrations become equal: particles continue random movement, but there is no net movement.
  • Osmosis is specifically the net movement of water, not the movement of any solute.
  • A partially permeable membrane is essential to the definition of osmosis.
  • Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential. A dilute solution has a higher water potential than a concentrated solution.
  • Active transport moves against a concentration gradient and requires energy from respiration; diffusion and osmosis are passive.
  • In an osmosis practical, a mass gain is evidence of net water entry; a mass loss is evidence of net water loss.

4. Biological molecules

Learning outcomes: identify the elements in carbohydrates, fats and proteins; describe the smaller molecules from which selected large molecules are made; carry out and interpret five food tests; and, for Supplement learners, describe the basic structure and complementary base pairing of DNA. This lesson follows section 4.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for 2026–2028.

4.1 Elements in biological molecules

Living organisms contain organic molecules built mainly from carbon. The elements you must know for this syllabus section are:

Biological moleculeElementsKey point
CarbohydratesCarbon, hydrogen and oxygen (C, H, O)Many carbohydrates contain hydrogen and oxygen in an approximate 2:1 ratio, but learn the elements rather than assuming every molecule has that exact ratio.
Fats and oils (lipids)Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (C, H, O)They generally contain proportionally less oxygen than carbohydrates.
ProteinsCarbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen (C, H, O, N)Some proteins also contain sulfur, but the essential syllabus answer is CHON.
Exam precision: if asked to list the elements in carbohydrates or fats, give C, H and O. For proteins, include nitrogen as well. Do not confuse the elements in a molecule with the smaller molecules from which it is built.

4.2 Large molecules are built from smaller molecules

Small molecules can join to form larger molecules. The syllabus limits the examples you need to the following three groups:

CarbohydratesStarch, glycogen and cellulose are large molecules made from many glucose molecules joined together.
ProteinsProteins are made from amino acid molecules joined in long chains. Different sequences produce different proteins.
Fats and oilsA fat or oil molecule is made from one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acid molecules.

Carbohydrates: glucose, starch, glycogen and cellulose

Glucose is a simple sugar. Many glucose molecules can be joined to form larger carbohydrate molecules. Starch is an energy-storage carbohydrate in plants; glycogen is an energy-storage carbohydrate in animals and fungi; cellulose is a structural carbohydrate in plant cell walls. For this syllabus objective, remember that all three are made from glucose, but their structures and functions differ.

Proteins and amino acids

Amino acids are small molecules that join to make protein chains. A protein's amino acid sequence determines how it folds into a particular three-dimensional shape. Proteins have many roles in organisms, including acting as enzymes, forming some cell structures, transporting substances and contributing to growth and repair. The food test detects protein; it does not identify the exact protein present.

Fats and oils are lipids. A typical triglyceride has one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acid molecules. Lipids are energy stores and can also provide thermal insulation and form part of cell membranes. Fats are usually solid at room temperature, while oils are usually liquid, but both belong to the lipid group.

Iodine solution is orange-brown before the test. A negative result means it does not turn blue-black. Use a clean pipette for each sample to avoid contamination.

Test 2 — Benedict’s solution for reducing sugars

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube and add a suitable volume of Benedict’s solution.
  2. Heat the mixture in a hot water bath for several minutes, using the teacher's laboratory instructions.
  3. Record the final colour and whether a precipitate forms.

A positive result may range from green through yellow and orange to brick-red. The colour often gives an approximate indication of the amount of reducing sugar, but it is not a precise concentration measurement unless a calibrated method is used. A negative result remains blue. Heating is required for the standard test.

Test 3 — Biuret test for protein

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube.
  2. Add Biuret reagent, or follow the approved sequence using sodium hydroxide and dilute copper(II) sulfate.
  3. Mix gently and observe the colour.

A lilac or purple colour indicates protein. If the solution remains blue, the test is negative. The test detects peptide bonds in proteins; it is not a test for starch or simple sugars.

Test 4 — ethanol emulsion test for fats and oils

  1. Add ethanol to the food sample and shake carefully to dissolve any lipid present.
  2. Allow solid material to settle if necessary, then pour the ethanol extract into water.
  3. Look for a cloudy white emulsion.

A milky-white emulsion is a positive result. A clear mixture is a negative result. Ethanol is flammable: keep it away from naked flames and use the laboratory's approved safety procedure. Never heat ethanol directly.

Test 5 — DCPIP test for vitamin C

  1. Place a measured volume of blue DCPIP solution in a test tube or spotting well.
  2. Add the food extract drop by drop, mixing consistently.
  3. Record whether the blue colour disappears and, when comparing samples, the volume needed to decolourise the same starting amount of DCPIP.

Vitamin C reduces DCPIP, causing its blue colour to disappear. If equal volumes and concentrations of DCPIP are used, a sample that decolourises it with fewer drops is generally more concentrated in vitamin C. Keep the starting DCPIP volume and concentration the same for a fair comparison.

5. Enzymes

Learning outcomes: define catalysts and enzymes; explain why enzymes are essential to metabolism; describe enzyme action using active sites, substrates, enzyme–substrate complexes and products; explain enzyme specificity; investigate how temperature and pH affect enzyme activity; and explain optimum conditions and denaturation.

5.1 Catalysts and biological catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. It is not used up, so a small amount can catalyse many reaction cycles.

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts. They are involved in metabolic reactions in living cells. Metabolism is the sum of the chemical reactions taking place in an organism, including reactions that build molecules (anabolism) and reactions that break molecules down (catabolism).

Build-up reactionsEnzymes help join smaller molecules to make larger molecules, for example reactions involved in making proteins.
Breakdown reactionsEnzymes help break large molecules into smaller molecules, for example digestion of starch and proteins.
Why they matterAt normal cell temperatures, many reactions would be too slow without enzymes. Enzymes make reaction rates fast enough to sustain life.
Remember: enzymes are not living organisms. They are proteins that catalyse reactions. The enzyme remains chemically unchanged at the end of a reaction and can be used again, provided it has not been denatured or damaged.

5.2 How enzymes work

The molecule on which an enzyme acts is called its substrate. A specific region of the enzyme, called the active site, has a shape that is complementary to the substrate. The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. The reaction occurs and product(s) are released. The enzyme is unchanged and can catalyse another reaction.

  1. The substrate moves into the enzyme's active site.
  2. The substrate binds to the active site because their shapes are complementary.
  3. An enzyme–substrate complex forms.
  4. The reaction converts the substrate into product(s).
  5. The product(s) leave the active site; the enzyme can be reused.

Extended explanation: enzyme specificity means that an enzyme usually catalyses only one type of reaction or acts on a particular substrate. The active site's three-dimensional shape and chemical properties are complementary to the substrate. A substrate with a different shape will not fit correctly and will not form the required enzyme–substrate complex.

5.3 Temperature and enzyme activity

As temperature rises from a low value towards the enzyme's optimum temperature, enzyme activity usually increases. Substrate and enzyme molecules gain kinetic energy, move faster and collide more frequently. More collisions have enough energy and the correct orientation to form enzyme–substrate complexes, so the rate increases.

Above the optimum temperature, the enzyme's structure begins to change. Bonds that help maintain its three-dimensional shape are disrupted. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits properly. The enzyme is denatured and its activity falls rapidly. Denaturation is usually permanent; cooling a denatured enzyme does not restore its original active-site shape.

At low temperatures, enzyme activity is slow because molecules have less kinetic energy and there are fewer successful collisions. Low temperature generally does not denature the enzyme; activity can increase again when the temperature is raised to a suitable value.

Do not confuse: low temperature slows enzyme activity but normally does not denature the enzyme. High temperature can denature it by changing the active site's shape.

5.4 pH and enzyme activity

Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which it works fastest. A change in pH can alter charges and bonds within the protein, changing the shape of the active site. The substrate may no longer fit as well, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate decreases. At an extreme pH, the enzyme may be denatured.

Different enzymes have different optimum pH values because they normally work in different parts of organisms. For example, pepsin in the stomach works best in acidic conditions, while enzymes acting in the small intestine generally work best in neutral to alkaline conditions. The optimum pH is not necessarily pH 7.

5.5 Investigating enzyme activity using amylase and starch

A common school investigation uses amylase to break down starch. Iodine solution is used to test for starch: iodine turns blue-black when starch is present and remains orange-brown when starch is absent. As amylase digests the starch, the time taken for a sample to stop turning iodine blue-black can be measured.

Method

  1. Place drops of iodine solution into separate wells of a spotting tile.
  2. Measure equal volumes of starch solution and amylase solution into labelled test tubes.
  3. Place the solutions in a water bath at the chosen temperature until they reach that temperature.
  4. Mix the amylase and starch and start the stopwatch immediately.
  5. At regular, fixed time intervals, remove a small drop of the reaction mixture and add it to a fresh iodine drop.
  6. Record the time when iodine no longer turns blue-black and remains orange-brown. This indicates that no detectable starch remains.
  7. Repeat the investigation and calculate a mean time. Use fresh solutions and clean equipment to avoid contamination.

Testing the effect of temperature

Repeat the procedure at a range of temperatures, for example 20°C, 30°C, 40°C, 50°C and 60°C. Use the same concentrations and volumes of starch and amylase, the same pH, the same sampling interval and the same endpoint definition. Keep the reaction mixture in the water bath throughout the test.

Testing the effect of pH

Repeat the reaction using buffer solutions of different pH values. Keep temperature, enzyme concentration, starch concentration, volumes and sampling intervals constant. Buffers help maintain the chosen pH during the reaction.

When the endpoint is the time taken for starch to disappear, a shorter time means the reaction is faster. A simple comparative rate can be calculated as:

Relative rate = 1 ÷ time taken
Use the same units for all times. The value is a relative measure for comparing trials, not necessarily the absolute rate in concentration per second.

  • The enzyme–substrate complex is temporary.
  • Specificity is explained by the complementary shape and fit of the active site and substrate.

  • In the starch test, blue-black means starch is present; orange-brown means no starch is detected.

6. Plant nutrition

Learning outcomes: explain photosynthesis and write its word equation; describe chlorophyll's role; explain how plants use and store carbohydrates; explain the importance of nitrate and magnesium ions; plan investigations into the requirements and rate of photosynthesis; interpret hydrogencarbonate indicator results; identify limiting factors (Supplement); and identify leaf structures and explain their adaptations for photosynthesis.

6.1 Photosynthesis: the process

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light. It takes place in chloroplasts, especially in the cells of leaves. The raw materials are carbon dioxide, which enters mainly through stomata, and water, which is absorbed by roots and transported to leaves in xylem vessels.

Word equation:
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
Conditions: light energy and chlorophyll.

Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in chloroplasts. It absorbs light energy and transfers this energy into chemical energy used to synthesise carbohydrates. Oxygen is released as a by-product and diffuses out of the leaf, mainly through stomata.

Balanced chemical equation (Supplement)

For Extended candidates, the balanced equation is:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
light energy and chlorophyll are required. The numbers show that atoms are conserved: there are 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 18 oxygen atoms on each side.

Photosynthesis does not mean that plants obtain their food from soil. The carbon in the carbohydrate comes from carbon dioxide in the air; water supplies hydrogen and some oxygen. Mineral ions from soil are needed for making other substances, but they are not a substitute for the carbon source.

6.2 What happens to the carbohydrates made?

Glucose made during photosynthesis can be used immediately or converted into other substances. The syllabus focuses on these uses:

CelluloseUsed to build plant cell walls, providing strength and support.

Plants may also use glucose to make other organic substances. For example, glucose can be combined with nitrogen-containing compounds to form amino acids, which are joined to make proteins.

6.4 Investigating the requirements for photosynthesis

Experiments can demonstrate that light, chlorophyll and carbon dioxide are required for starch production in a leaf. Starch is used as evidence because some of the glucose produced is converted to starch and stored in leaf cells.

Standard starch test for a leaf

  1. Destarch the plant by keeping it in darkness for about 24–48 hours so that stored starch is used up.
  2. Expose the plant or selected leaf areas to the required experimental conditions.
  3. Remove a leaf and briefly place it in boiling water to kill the cells and stop chemical reactions.
  4. Heat the leaf in ethanol in a hot-water bath to remove chlorophyll. Never heat ethanol directly over a flame; it is highly flammable.
  5. Rinse the leaf in warm water to soften it, then spread it on a white tile.
  6. Add iodine solution. A blue-black colour shows starch is present; yellow-brown/orange-brown shows starch is absent.

Testing the need for light

After destarching, cover part of a leaf on both sides with opaque foil and leave another part uncovered. Place the plant in bright light for several hours. Test the leaf for starch. The exposed region should turn blue-black; the covered region should remain yellow-brown because it did not receive light. Keep the leaf on the same plant so that other conditions are similar.

Testing the need for chlorophyll

Use a destarched variegated leaf with green and non-green regions. Expose it to light, then test it for starch. Green regions containing chlorophyll should turn blue-black; non-green regions should remain yellow-brown. This shows that chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis under the test conditions.

Testing the need for carbon dioxide

Destarch two similar potted plants or use two comparable leaves in a controlled setup. Place one in an airtight transparent enclosure with a carbon dioxide absorber such as soda lime (handled safely under teacher supervision), and the control in an otherwise identical enclosure with normal air. Expose both to the same light, then test leaves for starch. The leaf without access to carbon dioxide should not form detectable starch; the control should. Ensure the absorber does not contact the plant and that the apparatus is properly sealed.

6.6 Limiting factors (Supplement)

A limiting factor is a factor in shortest supply that restricts the rate of a process. Photosynthesis may be limited by light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. Increasing one factor raises the rate only while that factor is limiting; eventually another factor becomes limiting.

  • Low light intensity: light energy is insufficient, so increasing light intensity increases the rate.
  • Low carbon dioxide concentration: not enough carbon dioxide is available for carbohydrate synthesis, so increasing its concentration increases the rate.
  • Temperature below the optimum: enzyme-controlled reactions proceed more slowly; warming increases the rate.
  • Temperature above the optimum: photosynthetic enzymes and cell structures may be damaged; the rate falls.

In a greenhouse, growers may increase light, provide additional carbon dioxide and maintain a suitable temperature. The benefit of changing one factor depends on the levels of the other factors. Increasing light will have little effect if carbon dioxide is already severely limiting.

7.3 Mechanical digestion, teeth and peristalsis

Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces without changing the chemical nature of the food molecules. It increases the surface area available for digestive enzymes. Incisors cut, canines tear, and premolars and molars crush and grind food. Saliva lubricates food and makes a bolus that can be swallowed.

Peristalsis is the wave-like contraction and relaxation of circular and longitudinal muscles in the gut wall. Circular muscles contract behind a food bolus and relax ahead of it, pushing food along the canal. Peristalsis occurs in the oesophagus and other regions of the alimentary canal.

7.5 The roles of hydrochloric acid and bile

Hydrochloric acid is secreted in the stomach. It provides the acidic pH needed by stomach protease (pepsin) and helps destroy many microorganisms swallowed with food. The stomach lining is protected by mucus.

Bile is made by the liver and stored in the gall bladder before release into the duodenum. Bile is alkaline and helps neutralise acidic contents from the stomach, providing a more suitable pH for enzymes in the small intestine. Bile salts emulsify large fat globules into small droplets, increasing the surface area for lipase. Bile is not an enzyme and does not chemically digest fat.

7.6 Absorption in the small intestine

Absorption is the movement of digested food molecules through the wall of the intestine into the blood or lymph. The ileum is adapted for efficient absorption by being long and highly folded, with many villi and microvilli that provide a large surface area. The epithelium is one cell thick, giving a short diffusion distance. Each villus has a dense capillary network to carry away glucose and amino acids and a lacteal for absorption of many products of fat digestion.

7.7 Assimilation and transport of absorbed nutrients

Assimilation is the use of absorbed nutrients by cells and tissues. Glucose may be used in respiration or converted to glycogen for storage, especially in liver and muscle. Amino acids are used to make proteins for growth and repair, including enzymes and antibodies. Fatty acids and glycerol can be used to make lipids for storage and cell membranes. Glucose and amino acids enter blood capillaries in villi and are carried in the hepatic portal vein to the liver. Many lipid digestion products enter epithelial cells, are reassembled into lipids and packaged into particles that enter lacteals and travel in lymph before reaching the blood.

8. Transport in plants

Learning outcomes: explain why plants need transport systems; describe the structure and functions of xylem and phloem; explain water uptake by root hair cells, movement through roots and the transpiration stream; describe factors affecting transpiration; explain translocation; and plan and interpret investigations using a potometer.

8.1 Why plants need transport systems

Small plants can exchange some substances directly with their surroundings by diffusion. In larger plants, diffusion alone is too slow to supply every cell because distances are greater and many cells are far from the leaf or root surface. Plants therefore have specialised vascular tissues:

  • Xylem transports water and dissolved mineral ions from roots towards stems and leaves. It also helps support the plant.
  • Phloem transports dissolved organic substances, especially sucrose and amino acids, between sources and sinks. This movement is called translocation.

Water is needed for photosynthesis, as a solvent and transport medium, to maintain cell turgor and for cooling by evaporation. Mineral ions are needed to make substances such as proteins and chlorophyll.

8.2 Xylem: structure and function

Xylem vessels are formed from cells joined end to end. At maturity, vessel elements are dead and have no cytoplasm; their end walls are lost or perforated, forming a continuous hollow tube. The walls are strengthened with lignin, which reduces collapse under the tension created as water is pulled up the plant. Pits in the walls allow sideways movement of water between vessels and surrounding tissues.

Key link: lignin strengthens the vessel walls; the hollow lumen provides a low-resistance pathway; pits allow lateral movement; and continuous vessels help water move through the plant.

8.3 Phloem: structure and function

Phloem contains living sieve tube elements arranged end to end. Their end walls form sieve plates with pores through which phloem sap can pass. Sieve tube elements have very little cytoplasm and lack a nucleus at maturity. Each is closely associated with a companion cell, which contains a nucleus and many mitochondria and helps maintain the sieve tube and load or unload sucrose.

Phloem transports sucrose, amino acids and other dissolved substances from sources (where they are made or released) to sinks (where they are used or stored). Movement can occur upwards or downwards in different sieve tubes, depending on source and sink locations.

8.4 Water uptake by roots

Root hair cells are specialised epidermal cells that increase the surface area in contact with soil water. Water enters root hair cells by osmosis: net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (the dilute soil solution) to lower water potential (the more concentrated cell contents) through a partially permeable cell membrane.

Mineral ions are absorbed from the soil solution. When ions are taken up against their concentration gradient, active transport is involved and energy from respiration is required. Water can then move across the root cortex towards the xylem by pathways through cell walls and cytoplasm. The endodermis helps regulate entry into the vascular tissue.

8.5 The transpiration stream

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly from the leaves through stomata. Water evaporates from moist mesophyll cell surfaces into leaf air spaces and diffuses out through open stomata. This loss lowers the water potential in leaf cells and draws replacement water from the xylem. Cohesion between water molecules helps maintain a continuous column, while adhesion to xylem walls supports the column. Evaporation from leaves therefore creates tension that pulls water up the xylem from the roots.

Stomata are pores in the epidermis controlled by guard cells. When guard cells take up water and become turgid, the pore generally opens; when they lose water and become flaccid, the pore closes or narrows. Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter for photosynthesis but also provide a route for water vapour loss.

8.7 Investigating transpiration with a potometer

A potometer estimates the rate of water uptake by a cut shoot. It does not measure transpiration directly: some absorbed water is used in photosynthesis, growth or maintaining cell turgor. Under steady conditions, water uptake is often used as an estimate of transpiration rate.

Method:

  1. Fill the apparatus completely with water and check that there are no unwanted air leaks.
  2. Cut a healthy leafy shoot under water to prevent air entering the xylem, then fit it into the apparatus while submerged.
  3. Make every joint airtight. Introduce a small air bubble into the capillary tube.
  4. Record the bubble's starting position and measure its movement over a measured time interval.
  5. Repeat readings and calculate a mean rate. Use a reservoir or tap to return the bubble to its starting position between trials.
  6. Change one environmental factor at a time (for example, fan speed), keeping other factors as constant as possible.
Investigation variableExample
Independent variableWind speed, light intensity, temperature or humidity (change one).
Dependent variableDistance moved by the bubble per unit time, or volume of water taken up per unit time.
Control variablesLeaf area/species, shoot condition, temperature (if not tested), light intensity (if not tested), humidity and measurement time.

Reliability and validity: repeat measurements and calculate a mean; use the same shoot or comparable shoots; allow conditions to stabilise; check for leaks; measure leaf area if comparing shoots of different sizes. A leak or damaged shoot invalidates the estimate.

8.8 Translocation in phloem

Translocation is the movement of assimilates, especially sucrose and amino acids, in phloem from sources to sinks. A source is a region that releases assimilates, such as a photosynthesising leaf or a storage organ when reserves are mobilised. A sink is a region that uses or stores assimilates, such as a growing root, fruit, seed or developing shoot.

Sucrose is loaded into phloem at a source, often using energy-dependent transport involving companion cells. This lowers the water potential in the sieve tube, so water enters from nearby xylem by osmosis. The resulting pressure helps drive phloem sap towards sinks. At a sink, sucrose is unloaded and used in respiration, growth or converted to storage substances; water may move back into the xylem. Different sieve tubes can carry sap in different directions at the same time.

Do not confuse: transpiration is water loss, mainly from leaves; the transpiration stream is water movement in xylem; translocation is movement of assimilates in phloem. Xylem transport is mainly upward from roots, whereas phloem transport between sources and sinks can be in either direction.

8.9 Worked examples and calculations

Example 1: potometer rate

A bubble moves 36 mm in 6 minutes. Mean movement rate = distance ÷ time = 36 ÷ 6 = 6 mm/min.

Example 2: volume uptake

A capillary has a cross-sectional area of 0.5 mm². A bubble moves 40 mm. Volume of water uptake = area × distance = 0.5 × 40 = 20 mm³. If this takes 5 minutes, rate = 20 ÷ 5 = 4 mm³/min.

Example 3: percentage change

Water uptake rises from 12 mm³/min to 18 mm³/min. Percentage increase = ((18 − 12) ÷ 12) × 100 = 50%.

Example 4: interpreting a result

If bubble movement is faster in moving air than in still air, the likely explanation is that wind removes humid air from around the leaf, maintaining a steeper water-vapour concentration gradient and increasing diffusion through stomata. State the result and link it to the mechanism.

8.10 Common misconceptions and examination technique

  • Xylem transports water and mineral ions; phloem transports sucrose and amino acids.
  • Mature xylem vessel elements are dead and lignified; sieve tube elements are living and associated with companion cells.
  • Water enters root hair cells by osmosis; mineral ions may enter by active transport.
  • Transpiration is evaporation and diffusion of water vapour from the plant, mainly through stomata.
  • A potometer measures water uptake, not water vapour loss directly. Describe it as an estimate of transpiration rate.
  • High humidity usually reduces transpiration because it reduces the concentration gradient; wind usually increases it by removing humid air.
  • Translocation is not restricted to downward movement. It follows source-to-sink relationships.
  • In practical questions, identify independent, dependent and control variables and explain how repeats improve reliability.
  • For calculations, show the equation, substitution, units and a sensible conclusion.

9. Transport in animals

Learning outcomes: explain why large animals need transport systems; describe the mammalian circulatory system, heart and blood vessels; explain the cardiac cycle and pulse; describe blood components and their functions; explain tissue fluid formation and return; and interpret simple cardiovascular data.

9.1 Why animals need transport systems

In a small organism, diffusion across the body surface may supply cells with oxygen and nutrients and remove wastes. In a large multicellular animal, the surface-area-to-volume ratio is smaller and many cells are far from the body surface. Diffusion alone would be too slow to meet the demands of metabolism. A mass transport system moves substances rapidly between exchange surfaces and body tissues.

The mammalian circulatory system transports oxygen from the lungs, digested nutrients from the small intestine, hormones from endocrine glands and waste products towards organs that remove them. It also distributes heat and helps defend the body against pathogens.

9.2 The mammalian double circulatory system

Humans have a double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice during one complete circuit. The pulmonary circuit carries blood from the right side of the heart to the lungs and back to the left side. The systemic circuit carries blood from the left side of the heart to the body and back to the right side.

Important definitions: an artery carries blood away from the heart; a vein carries blood towards the heart. These definitions depend on direction, not oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs, while the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood to the heart.

9.3 Structure of the mammalian heart

The heart is a muscular organ with four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle. A septum separates the left and right sides and prevents oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing. Atria receive blood; ventricles pump blood out. The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall than the right ventricle because it pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body. The right ventricle pumps blood only to the nearby lungs.

Blood pathway: vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral (bicuspid) valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

Valves open when pressure behind them is greater than pressure ahead and close when blood attempts to flow backwards. The atrioventricular valves lie between atria and ventricles; semilunar valves are found at the exits of the ventricles.

9.4 The cardiac cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events during one heartbeat. During diastole, the heart muscle relaxes and blood enters the atria and passes into the ventricles. During atrial systole, the atria contract and complete ventricular filling. During ventricular systole, the ventricles contract; the atrioventricular valves close, the semilunar valves open and blood is forced into the pulmonary artery and aorta. The ventricles then relax and the cycle repeats.

The familiar “lub-dub” sounds are mainly caused by closure of valves: the first sound is associated with atrioventricular valve closure and the second with semilunar valve closure.

Heart rate and cardiac output

Heart rate is the number of heartbeats per minute. Stroke volume is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per beat. Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute.

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. If heart rate is 72 beats/min and stroke volume is 70 cm³/beat, cardiac output = 72 × 70 = 5040 cm³/min, or 5.04 dm³/min.

During exercise, muscles respire more rapidly and need more oxygen and glucose, while producing more carbon dioxide. Heart rate and usually stroke volume increase, raising cardiac output and delivery of materials to active tissues.

9.7 Tissue fluid and exchange with cells (Supplement)

At the arterial end of a capillary, blood pressure forces some plasma out through the thin capillary wall. Red blood cells and most plasma proteins remain in the blood. The fluid around cells is called tissue fluid. It supplies cells with dissolved oxygen and nutrients and receives carbon dioxide and other wastes.

At the venous end, blood pressure is lower and water potential effects favour return of much of the tissue fluid into capillaries. Excess tissue fluid enters lymph vessels and is eventually returned to the blood. This fluid movement helps maintain exchange between blood and body cells.

9.8 Blood clotting

When a blood vessel is damaged, platelets gather at the site and initiate a series of reactions that convert soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads. The fibrin forms a mesh that traps blood cells and helps form a clot. The clot reduces blood loss and creates a barrier that helps prevent pathogens entering the body.

9.9 Coronary heart disease and risk factors

The heart muscle receives oxygen and nutrients through the coronary arteries. Coronary heart disease can occur when fatty deposits build up in coronary artery walls, narrowing the lumen and reducing blood flow to cardiac muscle. A complete blockage may cause a heart attack.

Risk factors include smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, physical inactivity, obesity, high blood pressure, diabetes and some inherited factors. Risk can be reduced by not smoking, regular physical activity, a balanced diet and appropriate management of blood pressure and blood glucose.

  • The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.
  • The left ventricle has a thicker wall because it pumps blood around the systemic circulation at higher pressure.
  • Capillaries have walls one cell thick to provide a short diffusion distance.
  • Red blood cells transport oxygen using haemoglobin; plasma transports many dissolved substances.

10. Diseases and immunity

Learning outcomes: distinguish pathogens from vectors; describe how pathogens cause disease and spread; explain physical and chemical barriers to infection; describe phagocytosis and antibody action; explain active and passive immunity, vaccination and herd immunity; and evaluate ways of preventing the spread of communicable diseases.

10.1 Pathogens and communicable disease

A pathogen is a disease-causing organism or agent. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists. A communicable disease can spread from one host to another, directly or indirectly. A vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts; for example, mosquitoes transmit the malaria parasite.

Pathogens may damage cells directly, use host nutrients, or release toxins. Symptoms are effects of infection and may include fever, inflammation, diarrhoea, coughing or tissue damage.

10.2 Transmission of communicable diseases

Pathogens can spread by direct contact, droplets released by coughing or sneezing, contaminated food or water, contaminated surfaces, blood and other body fluids, or vectors. The route of transmission determines which control measures are most effective.

10.3 Body defences against pathogens

The body has several non-specific barriers that reduce the chance of pathogens entering and causing infection:

  • Skin: forms a physical barrier; blood clotting seals cuts.
  • Mucus and cilia: mucus traps particles and pathogens in airways; cilia move mucus towards the throat to be swallowed.
  • Nasal hairs: trap larger particles.
  • Stomach acid: kills many pathogens swallowed with food or mucus.
  • Tears and saliva: contain antimicrobial substances, including lysozyme.

These defences are part of innate immunity and act without needing to recognise a particular pathogen first.

10.4 Phagocytosis

Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens. They recognise foreign material, surround it with their cell membrane and enclose it in a vesicle. Lysosomes release digestive enzymes into the vesicle, breaking down the pathogen. This process is called phagocytosis.

Phagocytosis is a non-specific defence: a phagocyte can engulf a range of pathogens. It does not depend on producing an antibody specific to one antigen.

10.5 Antigens and antibodies

An antigen is a molecule, often a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen, that is recognised as foreign and can stimulate an immune response. Lymphocytes are white blood cells involved in specific immunity. Some lymphocytes produce antibodies.

An antibody is a protein with a binding site whose shape is complementary to a particular antigen. The antibody binds to the antigen, forming an antigen–antibody complex. This may neutralise toxins or viruses, cause pathogens to clump together, or mark them for destruction by phagocytes. Each antibody is specific to its antigen.

In active immunity, the person's lymphocytes respond to an antigen and produce antibodies. Some become memory cells. If the same antigen enters again, memory cells respond more quickly and strongly, often preventing symptoms. In passive immunity, antibodies are transferred from another source; protection is immediate but fades as antibodies are broken down.

10.7 Vaccination and memory cells

A vaccine contains harmless forms of pathogen antigens, such as weakened or inactivated pathogens, parts of pathogens, or genetic instructions that cause cells to make an antigen. The vaccine stimulates an immune response without causing the full disease in the usual way. Lymphocytes produce specific antibodies and memory cells. If the person later encounters the pathogen, the secondary response is faster and produces more antibodies.

Vaccination programmes reduce the number of susceptible people in a population. When enough people are immune, transmission becomes less likely and people who cannot be vaccinated may receive indirect protection. This is called herd immunity. The proportion needed varies with how easily the pathogen spreads and how effective the vaccine is.

10.8 Antibiotics and antibiotic resistance

Antibiotics are medicines used to treat bacterial infections. They work against bacteria, not viruses, because viruses reproduce inside host cells and do not have the same cellular structures and processes targeted by antibiotics. Antibiotics should be used only when appropriate and taken exactly as prescribed.

Antibiotic resistance evolves in bacterial populations by natural selection:

  1. A bacterial population contains genetic variation; a few bacteria may have a mutation that makes them resistant.
  2. An antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria, while resistant bacteria are more likely to survive.
  3. Surviving resistant bacteria reproduce and pass on resistance genes.
  4. The proportion of resistant bacteria increases, making the antibiotic less effective.

Resistance can spread between bacteria when resistance genes are transferred. Overuse and incorrect use of antibiotics increase selection pressure. Measures include prescribing antibiotics only when needed, completing the prescribed course as directed, preventing infections through hygiene and vaccination, and using infection-control procedures in healthcare settings.

10.9 Malaria: a vector-borne disease

Malaria is caused by a protoctist parasite of the genus Plasmodium and transmitted by infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The mosquito is the vector. Parasites enter the human bloodstream during a bite and infect liver cells and then red blood cells. Repeated destruction of red blood cells contributes to fever and anaemia.

Control measures include insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, draining or managing suitable breeding sites, protective clothing, rapid diagnosis and appropriate antimalarial treatment. Vector control reduces transmission but does not replace treatment of infected people.

  • Antigens are recognised as foreign; antibodies are proteins that bind specifically to antigens.

11. Gas exchange in humans

Learning outcomes: explain why gas exchange is needed; describe the human gas exchange system and the features of alveoli; explain ventilation and the roles of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles; describe diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide; and explain how exercise, smoking and lung disease affect gas exchange.

11.1 Why gas exchange is important

Human cells need oxygen for aerobic respiration, which releases energy from glucose. Carbon dioxide is produced as a waste product of respiration and must be removed because its accumulation affects blood pH. The lungs provide a large surface where oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it.

Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the air in the lungs and the blood by diffusion. It is different from ventilation, which is the movement of air into and out of the lungs, and from cellular respiration, which is the chemical process in cells that releases energy.

11.2 The human gas exchange system

Air passes through the nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and finally the alveoli. The trachea divides into two bronchi, one entering each lung. Each bronchus branches into smaller bronchioles that end in clusters of alveoli.

11.3 Adaptations of alveoli for gas exchange

Alveoli are tiny air sacs surrounded by networks of blood capillaries. They provide an efficient exchange surface because they have:

  • Large surface area: millions of alveoli provide a very large total area for diffusion.
  • Thin walls: alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium are each one cell thick, giving a short diffusion distance.
  • Moist lining: gases dissolve before diffusing across cell membranes.
  • Rich blood supply: continuous blood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide to the lungs, maintaining concentration gradients.
  • Ventilation: fresh air continually replaces alveolar air, helping maintain steep concentration gradients.

Oxygen concentration (or partial pressure) is higher in alveolar air than in the deoxygenated blood arriving at the lungs, so oxygen diffuses into the blood. Carbon dioxide concentration is higher in arriving blood than in alveolar air, so carbon dioxide diffuses into the alveolus and is exhaled.

11.4 Ventilation: inhalation and exhalation

Ventilation moves air by changing the volume and pressure of the thorax. Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure.

During quiet exhalation, the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax and elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall helps force air out. Forced exhalation uses internal intercostal muscles and abdominal muscles to reduce thoracic volume more strongly.

11.5 Transport of respiratory gases

Most oxygen is transported from the lungs to body tissues attached to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Oxygen binds reversibly to haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin. In tissues where oxygen concentration is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases oxygen, which diffuses into cells for aerobic respiration.

Carbon dioxide produced by respiring cells diffuses into the blood and is transported to the lungs, mainly in the form of hydrogencarbonate ions in plasma. Some is carried dissolved in plasma and some binds to haemoglobin. In the lungs, carbon dioxide is released from the blood and diffuses into alveoli.

11.6 Effects of exercise on ventilation and gas exchange

During exercise, muscles respire more rapidly and use more oxygen while producing more carbon dioxide. The breathing rate and depth increase, raising the rate of ventilation. Heart rate and cardiac output also increase, bringing more blood to the lungs and active muscles. These changes help maintain steep diffusion gradients and supply oxygen for aerobic respiration.

After strenuous exercise, breathing and heart rates remain elevated for a period as the body returns towards resting conditions and deals with changes associated with exercise.

11.7 Smoking and respiratory disease

Tobacco smoke contains harmful substances including tar, nicotine and carbon monoxide. Tar damages cilia and irritates the airways; mucus accumulates and increases the risk of infection. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Nicotine is addictive and increases heart rate and blood pressure.

  • Chronic bronchitis: inflammation of bronchi, excess mucus and persistent coughing; airflow and clearance of pathogens are impaired.
  • Emphysema: alveolar walls are damaged and air spaces merge; surface area for gas exchange decreases and elastic recoil is reduced, making ventilation difficult.
  • Lung cancer: abnormal cell division can form a tumour; smoking greatly increases risk.
  • Asthma: airways become inflamed and narrow, often with excess mucus; this restricts airflow and can cause wheezing and breathlessness.

To investigate the effect of exercise, record a resting rate, perform a standardised activity for a set time, then measure immediately after exercise and at regular recovery intervals.

  • Keep the exercise type, duration and intensity as similar as possible for each trial.
  • Use the same method and time interval when counting breaths.
  • Repeat readings and calculate a mean; identify anomalous results rather than automatically including them.
  • Allow adequate recovery and consider safety, fitness and health differences between participants.

A simple model of lung ventilation can be made with a bottle, balloons and a flexible membrane. Pulling the membrane down increases the volume inside the bottle and inflates the balloons; pushing it up decreases volume and deflates them. The model demonstrates pressure and volume changes but does not fully reproduce ribs, muscles, pleural membranes or the structure of real lungs.

11.9 Worked examples and calculations

Example 1: breathing rate

A learner counts 18 breaths in 30 seconds. Breathing rate per minute = 18 × 2 = 36 breaths min⁻¹.

Example 2: percentage increase in breathing rate

Resting rate is 15 breaths min⁻¹ and post-exercise rate is 30 breaths min⁻¹. Percentage increase = ((30 − 15) ÷ 15) × 100 = 100%.

Example 3: surface area and diffusion

If damage reduces the effective exchange surface from 70 m² to 42 m², percentage decrease = ((70 − 42) ÷ 70) × 100 = 40%. A smaller surface area reduces the potential rate of diffusion, all other factors being equal.

11.10 Common misconceptions and examination technique

  • Ventilation is movement of air; gas exchange is diffusion of gases; respiration is a chemical process in cells.
  • Oxygen moves from alveoli into blood, while carbon dioxide moves from blood into alveoli.
  • Do not say the diaphragm “moves down” because air pulls it; it contracts and flattens during inhalation.
  • During inhalation, thoracic volume increases and pressure inside the lungs falls.
  • Alveoli have a large surface area, thin moist walls, a good blood supply and ventilation to maintain gradients.
  • Carbon monoxide reduces oxygen transport by binding strongly to haemoglobin.
  • Emphysema reduces gas-exchange surface area because alveolar walls are damaged.
  • When asked to explain an effect of exercise, link increased muscle respiration to greater oxygen demand and carbon dioxide production, then to increased ventilation.

12. Respiration

Learning outcomes: define respiration; distinguish aerobic and anaerobic respiration; write word and balanced symbol equations; explain energy transfer to ATP; describe anaerobic respiration in muscles and yeast; explain oxygen debt; and plan investigations into respiration.

12.1 What is respiration?

Respiration is the enzyme-controlled chemical process in cells that releases energy from nutrient molecules, especially glucose. Energy is transferred to ATP (adenosine triphosphate), which provides usable energy for muscle contraction, active transport, protein synthesis, cell division and, in mammals, maintaining body temperature. Respiration is not the same as breathing: breathing moves air, while respiration is a cellular process.

12.2 Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down glucose completely, releasing a relatively large amount of energy.

Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy transferred to ATP)

Balanced symbol equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

In eukaryotic cells, glycolysis begins in the cytoplasm. Later stages occur in mitochondria, where oxygen is used in the final stages. Carbon dioxide is a waste product transported to the lungs in humans.

12.3 Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without using oxygen. Glucose is only partially broken down, so less energy is released per glucose molecule than in aerobic respiration.

In mammalian muscle cells during vigorous activity:

glucose → lactic acid (lactate) (+ energy transferred to ATP)

In yeast and some plant cells, anaerobic respiration (alcoholic fermentation) produces ethanol and carbon dioxide:

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (+ energy transferred to ATP)

Balanced symbol equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

12.5 Exercise and oxygen debt

During intense exercise, muscles may need ATP faster than oxygen can be delivered. Anaerobic respiration supplements ATP production and lactate accumulates. After exercise, breathing rate and heart rate remain elevated, supplying extra oxygen and helping return the body to resting conditions. At this level, the extra oxygen required during recovery is called oxygen debt. Oxygen helps process and remove lactate, including its conversion in the liver. Recovery also includes restoring ATP and phosphocreatine stores.

12.6 Fermentation in yeast

Yeast ferments sugars anaerobically, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide. In bread-making, carbon dioxide bubbles become trapped in dough and make it rise; ethanol evaporates during baking. Brewing uses the ethanol produced. Temperature, pH and sugar concentration affect the rate. Low temperature slows enzyme-controlled reactions; excessive heat denatures enzymes and kills yeast.

12.7 Practical: effect of temperature on yeast respiration

Use equal volumes and concentrations of yeast suspension and glucose solution in identical flasks. Place each in a water bath at a different temperature, allow it to equilibrate, then collect carbon dioxide with a gas syringe and record volume at fixed time intervals.

  • Independent variable: temperature.
  • Dependent variable: carbon dioxide volume per unit time.
  • Control variables: yeast concentration, glucose concentration and volume, pH, total volume, apparatus and measurement time.
  • Repeat each temperature, calculate a mean rate, keep apparatus airtight and use warm—not boiling—water baths.

12.8 Practical: respirometer

A respirometer can compare oxygen uptake by germinating seeds. Seeds are placed in a sealed chamber; soda lime or potassium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide. As oxygen is consumed, gas volume and pressure fall, moving a coloured liquid marker in a capillary tube. A control containing glass beads or non-germinating/boiled seeds helps account for temperature and pressure changes unrelated to respiration. Soda lime and potassium hydroxide are corrosive: avoid contact and follow laboratory safety instructions.

Example 1 — rate: yeast produces 24 cm³ CO₂ in 8 minutes. Rate = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 cm³ min⁻¹.

  • Anaerobic respiration releases energy, but less than aerobic respiration.
  • Muscle cells produce lactate; yeast produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
  • Do not state that carbon dioxide is produced by anaerobic respiration in mammalian muscles.
  • ATP transfers usable energy to cellular processes.
  • When explaining oxygen debt, connect anaerobic activity and lactate accumulation with extra oxygen use during recovery.
  • For practical questions, state variables, repeats, controls, measurement intervals and how rate is calculated.

13. Excretion in Humans

Learning outcomes: define excretion; distinguish excretion from egestion; identify metabolic wastes; explain the roles of the liver and kidneys; describe the urinary system; explain urine formation and the role of the nephron; and compare kidney dialysis with a healthy kidney.

13.1 Meaning and importance of excretion

Excretion is the removal from the body of toxic materials, the waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements. Metabolism is the sum of chemical reactions in cells. Excretion prevents harmful substances accumulating and helps maintain a stable internal environment.

Do not confuse excretion with egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food from the alimentary canal as faeces. Faeces are not metabolic waste produced by cells.

13.2 The liver and the formation of urea

Proteins are digested into amino acids. Amino acids are used to build new proteins, but the body cannot store excess amino acids for later use. In the liver, excess amino acids are deaminated: the amino group is removed. This produces ammonia, which is toxic. The liver converts ammonia into the less toxic compound urea, which is transported in the blood plasma to the kidneys for excretion.

The remaining carbon-containing part of the amino acid can be used in respiration or converted into other substances. The liver also processes toxins, produces bile and carries out many metabolic functions.

13.3 The human urinary system

The urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder and the urethra. The kidneys filter blood and regulate the amounts of water and mineral ions in the body. Urine passes from each kidney down a ureter to the bladder, where it is stored. It leaves the body through the urethra.

13.4 Structure of a kidney

Each kidney has an outer cortex, an inner medulla and a central renal pelvis, which collects urine before it enters the ureter. Blood enters through the renal artery and leaves through the renal vein. The kidney contains many nephrons, the microscopic functional units where filtration and selective reabsorption occur.

13.5 The nephron and urine formation

A nephron contains a glomerulus inside Bowman's capsule, a proximal convoluted tubule, a loop of Henle, a distal convoluted tubule and a collecting duct. The glomerulus is a knot of capillaries supplied by an afferent arteriole and drained by an efferent arteriole.

Ultrafiltration (Supplement)

Blood enters the glomerulus at relatively high pressure. Water and small dissolved substances are forced through the capillary wall and filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule. These include glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, urea and water. Blood cells and most plasma proteins are too large to pass through and remain in the blood. The fluid entering the capsule is called glomerular filtrate.

Selective reabsorption

Useful substances are returned from the filtrate to the blood. Under normal conditions, all glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule by active transport, together with the necessary transport of other useful solutes. Water follows by osmosis. Some mineral ions are also reabsorbed. The amount of water reabsorbed is adjusted according to the body's needs. Urea remains in the filtrate and is excreted in urine.

The loop of Henle and collecting duct help establish and use a concentration gradient in the kidney medulla, allowing water reabsorption to be regulated. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), covered in the coordination topic, changes the collecting duct's permeability to water.

13.6 What is in urine?

Normal urine contains water, urea and variable amounts of mineral ions. It normally contains no blood cells and no significant glucose or plasma proteins. The volume and concentration of urine vary with water intake, temperature, sweating and hormone control. If glucose is detected in urine, this may indicate that blood glucose is above the kidney's reabsorption capacity, as can occur in diabetes mellitus.

13.7 Kidney failure and dialysis

If kidneys fail, urea and other wastes can accumulate in the blood and the body's water and ion balance may become dangerous. One treatment is haemodialysis. Blood is taken from the patient and passed through a dialysis machine, separated from dialysis fluid by a partially permeable membrane. The dialysis fluid has a normal concentration of useful substances such as glucose and appropriate mineral ions, but contains no urea. Urea diffuses from the blood into the fluid down its concentration gradient. Excess salts and water can also be removed by controlling the dialysis fluid and treatment conditions.

Blood cells and plasma proteins remain in the blood because they cannot cross the membrane. The cleaned blood is returned to the patient. Dialysis must be repeated regularly. A kidney transplant may provide a longer-term alternative, but requires a suitable donor and management of the risk of rejection.

13.8 Maintaining water balance

The kidneys adjust the amount of water reabsorbed, helping keep the water potential of the blood within suitable limits. When the body is short of water, more ADH is released from the pituitary gland. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced. When the body has excess water, less ADH is released; less water is reabsorbed and a larger volume of more dilute urine is produced. ADH is studied in more detail in Topic 14.

13.9 Worked examples and data interpretation

Example 1 — daily urea excretion: A person produces 1.5 dm³ of urine containing 20 g of urea per dm³. Urea excreted = concentration × volume = 20 × 1.5 = 30 g.

Example 2 — percentage change in urine volume: Urine volume falls from 1.8 dm³ to 0.9 dm³. Percentage decrease = ((1.8 − 0.9) ÷ 1.8) × 100 = 50%.

Example 3 — dialysis concentration gradient: If blood contains urea and the dialysis fluid contains none, urea diffuses from blood into dialysis fluid. Replacing the fluid maintains a steep concentration gradient and allows continued diffusion.

  • Excretion removes metabolic wastes; egestion removes undigested food.
  • Urea is made in the liver, not in the kidneys. The kidneys remove it from the blood.
  • Deamination removes the amino group from excess amino acids; ammonia is converted to urea in the liver.
  • Ultrafiltration is driven by high pressure in the glomerulus. Selective reabsorption returns useful substances to the blood.
  • Do not say that all substances filtered into Bowman's capsule are excreted; useful substances are reabsorbed.
  • Dialysis fluid contains no urea and has suitable concentrations of useful solutes to reduce their net loss from blood.
  • When comparing dialysis and kidney function, refer to the partially permeable membrane, diffusion gradient and removal of urea and excess water/ions.

14. Coordination and Response

14.1 Coordination and response

Organisms detect changes in their internal and external environments. A change that causes a response is called a stimulus. A receptor detects a stimulus. An effector (a muscle or gland) produces a response. Coordination ensures that different parts of the body work together.

In humans, coordination is achieved mainly by the nervous system and the endocrine (hormonal) system. Nervous responses use electrical impulses along neurones and are usually rapid and short-lived. Hormones are chemical messengers carried in the blood; their effects are often slower to begin and longer-lasting.

14.2 The human nervous system

The nervous system consists of the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system. The CNS is the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system contains nerves that carry impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body.

A neurone is a specialised cell that carries electrical impulses. A sensory neurone carries impulses from a receptor towards the CNS. A relay neurone carries impulses within the CNS. A motor neurone carries impulses from the CNS to an effector.

14.3 The reflex arc

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus. It helps protect the body from harm and does not require conscious thought before the response occurs. In a typical withdrawal reflex, the pathway is:

  1. A receptor in the skin detects a stimulus, such as intense heat.
  2. An impulse travels along a sensory neurone to the spinal cord.
  3. In the CNS, the impulse passes through a relay neurone.
  4. The impulse travels along a motor neurone to an effector, such as a muscle.
  5. The muscle contracts and withdraws the hand.

14.4 Synapses (Supplement)

A synapse is a tiny gap between two neurones. When an impulse reaches the end of the first neurone, vesicles release a chemical neurotransmitter. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic gap and binds to receptors on the membrane of the next neurone. This starts a new electrical impulse if the threshold is reached. The neurotransmitter is then broken down or removed.

Transmission across a synapse is one-way because neurotransmitter is released from the presynaptic ending and receptors are located on the postsynaptic membrane. Synapses allow information to be integrated and help prevent continuous, uncontrolled transmission.

14.5 The eye as a sense organ

The eye detects light. The cornea refracts light as it enters; the iris controls pupil size; the pupil is the opening through which light enters; the lens focuses light onto the retina; and the retina contains light-sensitive receptor cells. The optic nerve carries impulses from the retina to the brain.

Pupil reflex

In bright light, circular muscles in the iris contract and radial muscles relax. The pupil becomes smaller, reducing the amount of light entering the eye and protecting the retina. In dim light, radial muscles contract and circular muscles relax, making the pupil larger so more light enters.

Accommodation (Supplement)

Accommodation is the adjustment of lens shape to focus near or distant objects on the retina. For a near object, the ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments slacken and the lens becomes thicker and more convex, increasing refraction. For a distant object, ciliary muscles relax, suspensory ligaments become taut and the lens becomes thinner, reducing refraction.

Adrenaline is released by the adrenal glands during fear, stress or excitement. It prepares the body for vigorous activity: heart rate and breathing rate increase, blood glucose concentration rises, and more blood is directed to skeletal muscles. These changes help supply muscles with oxygen and glucose for increased respiration.

14.8 Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment. It keeps conditions such as blood glucose concentration, body temperature and water balance within limits so that cells and enzymes can function effectively. Homeostasis commonly uses negative feedback: a change away from the normal level is detected, corrective responses act against the change, and the condition moves back towards its normal range.

Control of blood glucose concentration

The pancreas monitors blood glucose concentration. When it rises, beta cells in the pancreas release insulin. Insulin increases uptake of glucose by cells and stimulates the liver and muscles to convert glucose into glycogen for storage. Blood glucose concentration falls towards normal.

When blood glucose concentration falls, alpha cells in the pancreas release glucagon. Glucagon stimulates the liver to convert glycogen into glucose and release it into the blood. Blood glucose concentration rises towards normal. Insulin and glucagon therefore have opposing effects.

Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a condition in which blood glucose concentration is not properly controlled. In type 1 diabetes, the pancreas produces little or no insulin because insulin-producing beta cells are destroyed; treatment usually involves insulin. In type 2 diabetes, body cells become less responsive to insulin and insulin production may also become insufficient. Management may include diet, exercise, medication and sometimes insulin. Persistent high blood glucose can damage blood vessels and organs.

14.9 Regulation of body temperature

14.11 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • A receptor detects a stimulus; an effector produces the response.
  • Sensory neurones carry impulses towards the CNS; motor neurones carry impulses away from the CNS to effectors.
  • A reflex is rapid and automatic, but the brain can still receive information about the stimulus.
  • Hormones travel in blood and affect target cells with suitable receptors; they are not electrical impulses.
  • Insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it. Do not reverse their actions.
  • Negative feedback reverses a change away from the normal level; it does not mean that the response is always negative or harmful.
  • Vasodilation increases skin blood flow and heat loss; vasoconstriction reduces it.
  • For accommodation, remember: near = ciliary muscles contract, ligaments slacken, lens thickens; far = ciliary muscles relax, ligaments tighten, lens thins.

15. Drugs

Learning outcomes: define a drug; distinguish medicinal drugs from recreational drugs; explain how antibiotics treat bacterial infections and why they do not treat viruses; describe antibiotic resistance and ways to slow its spread; and explain the effects of tobacco smoke, alcohol and heroin on the body and society.

15.1 What is a drug?

A drug is a substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body. Drugs may be used as medicines to prevent or treat disease, or they may be taken recreationally. A drug can have beneficial effects as well as unwanted side effects. The effect depends on the substance, dose, route of administration and individual.

Recreational drugTaken for its effects on mood, perception or behaviour rather than to treat illnessAlcohol, nicotine and heroin

15.2 Medicinal drugs and antibiotics

Medicinal drugs can relieve symptoms or act on the cause of a disease. Analgesics reduce pain but do not necessarily remove the cause of the pain. Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria or prevent them reproducing. They are used to treat bacterial infections, not viral infections.

Viruses reproduce inside host cells and use the host cell's machinery. Antibiotics act on bacterial structures or processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell-wall formation or bacterial ribosomes. Therefore, antibiotics do not cure illnesses such as colds and influenza, which are caused by viruses.

Antibiotics should be used only when appropriate and according to qualified medical advice. Taking them when they are not needed exposes bacteria to selection pressure without providing benefit to the patient. Do not share antibiotics or use leftover medicines.

15.3 Antibiotic resistance

A bacterial population may contain variation: a few bacteria may have a mutation that makes them less affected by an antibiotic. When the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria are killed or inhibited, while resistant bacteria survive. The resistant bacteria reproduce and pass the resistance allele to their offspring. Over generations, the proportion of resistant bacteria increases. This is natural selection; bacteria do not develop resistance because they deliberately need it.

Antibiotic resistance can spread when resistant bacteria multiply and resistance genes are transferred between bacteria. It makes infections harder to treat and may require different medicines, longer treatment or hospital care.

Ways to slow resistance:

  • Use antibiotics only when prescribed and appropriate.
  • Follow the healthcare professional's instructions; do not self-prescribe, share or save antibiotics for later.
  • Use infection prevention measures such as handwashing, vaccination where available and good hygiene.
  • Reduce unnecessary antibiotic use in human healthcare and animal production.
  • Use surveillance and infection-control measures to limit the spread of resistant strains.

15.4 Tobacco smoking

Tobacco smoke contains many harmful substances. Three important examples are nicotine, carbon monoxide and tar.

SubstanceEffect

Damage to cilia reduces removal of mucus and trapped particles. Mucus can build up, contributing to coughing and infection. Smoking damages alveoli and can lead to chronic bronchitis and emphysema. In emphysema, alveolar walls are damaged, reducing the surface area available for gas exchange and making breathing difficult.

Smoking also increases the risk of coronary heart disease and stroke. Carbon monoxide reduces oxygen transport, while nicotine increases cardiovascular strain. Passive smoking exposes non-smokers to harmful substances in other people's smoke.

15.5 Alcohol

Alcohol (ethanol) is a depressant that affects the central nervous system. It slows reaction time, reduces judgement and coordination, and can impair memory and decision-making. This increases the risk of road accidents, falls and other injuries. Alcohol can cause dependence. Long-term heavy consumption can damage the liver, including causing fatty liver, hepatitis and cirrhosis, and can increase the risk of some cancers.

Alcohol is absorbed from the digestive system into the blood and is mainly broken down in the liver. The liver can process alcohol only at a limited rate. Drinking large amounts in a short time can cause alcohol poisoning, which is a medical emergency.

15.6 Heroin and other opioids

Heroin is a highly addictive opioid drug. It depresses the central nervous system and reduces the activity of brain centres controlling breathing. It can cause drowsiness, impaired judgement and dangerously slow breathing. An overdose may stop breathing and can be fatal. Dependence can lead to withdrawal symptoms when use stops.

Injecting heroin carries additional risks, especially when needles or other equipment are shared. Blood-borne infections such as HIV and hepatitis B or C can spread through contaminated equipment. Illicit drugs may contain unknown substances and unpredictable doses, increasing the risk of poisoning and overdose.

15.7 Social and economic effects of drug misuse

Drug misuse can affect individuals, families and communities. Possible consequences include illness, dependence, accidents, reduced ability to work or study, family stress, healthcare costs, crime associated with drug markets and transmission of infections through shared injecting equipment. These effects vary with the drug, pattern of use and circumstances. Prevention, education, treatment and support services can reduce harm.

16. Reproduction

Learning outcomes: distinguish sexual and asexual reproduction; describe reproduction in flowering plants, including pollination, fertilisation, seed formation and germination; identify the human reproductive organs and their functions; explain fertilisation, implantation, development of the fetus and birth; and describe the menstrual cycle and the roles of reproductive hormones.

16.1 Sexual and asexual reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which organisms produce offspring. In asexual reproduction, one parent produces genetically identical offspring (clones), except where mutations occur. It does not involve fusion of gamete nuclei. In sexual reproduction, male and female gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote. The offspring show genetic variation because they inherit combinations of alleles from two parents.

Asexual reproductionSexual reproduction
Usually one parentUsually two parents, or two gametes from different reproductive structures
No fusion of gamete nucleiFusion of nuclei of gametes at fertilisation
Offspring genetically identical to parent, apart from mutationsOffspring genetically varied

16.2 Reproduction in flowering plants

A flower is the reproductive structure of many flowering plants. The male reproductive part is the stamen, made of anther and filament. The anther produces pollen grains containing the male nucleus. The female reproductive part is the carpel (pistil), consisting of stigma, style and ovary. The ovary contains ovules, each with a female nucleus. Petals may attract pollinators; sepals protect the flower bud.

16.3 Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma. Self-pollination occurs when pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination occurs between flowers on different plants of the same species. Cross-pollination usually increases genetic variation.

16.4 Fertilisation and seed formation in plants

After a compatible pollen grain lands on the stigma, it germinates and grows a pollen tube down the style towards an ovule in the ovary. The male nucleus travels down the pollen tube and fuses with the female nucleus in the ovule. This fusion is fertilisation and produces a diploid zygote, which develops into an embryo.

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary develops into a fruit. The seed contains an embryo and a food store, protected by a seed coat. The fruit helps protect and disperse seeds.

16.5 Seed germination

Germination is the resumption of growth of the embryo in a seed. Most seeds need water, oxygen and a suitable temperature. Water enters the seed and activates enzymes; stored food is broken down and used in respiration and growth. Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration to release energy. A suitable temperature allows enzyme-controlled reactions to occur at an appropriate rate.

Seeds do not normally require light for germination, although light may be needed for later photosynthesis in the developing seedling. A simple investigation can compare germination under different conditions while changing one factor at a time and keeping other variables controlled.

16.6 Human reproductive system

The male reproductive system produces sperm and the hormone testosterone. The testes produce sperm in the seminiferous tubules and testosterone. The scrotum holds the testes outside the body, helping keep them slightly cooler than core body temperature. Sperm mature and are stored in the epididymis, travel along the sperm ducts and pass into the urethra. Glands add fluid to form semen. The penis transfers semen into the female reproductive tract during sexual intercourse.

The female reproductive system produces egg cells and supports development of an embryo and fetus. The ovaries produce egg cells and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. An egg is released into an oviduct (fallopian tube), where fertilisation usually occurs. The uterus has a muscular wall and a lining in which an embryo implants. The cervix is the narrow opening to the uterus and the vagina receives the penis and acts as the birth canal.

16.7 Gametes and fertilisation in humans

Sperm cells are adapted for swimming to the egg: they have a flagellum, many mitochondria to release energy and an acrosome containing enzymes that help the sperm penetrate the egg's outer layers. The egg cell is large, contains nutrient-rich cytoplasm and has a membrane that changes after fertilisation to prevent additional sperm entering.

Fertilisation usually occurs in the oviduct. The nucleus of one sperm fuses with the nucleus of the egg, restoring the diploid chromosome number and forming a zygote. The zygote divides by mitosis as it moves towards the uterus. It develops into an embryo, which implants in the uterine lining.

16.8 The menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle prepares the uterus for possible pregnancy. The uterine lining breaks down and leaves the body during menstruation if pregnancy has not occurred. The lining then repairs and thickens. Ovulation is the release of an egg from an ovary, usually around the middle of a 28-day cycle, although cycle length and ovulation timing vary between individuals.

FSH stimulates a follicle to develop. The developing follicle produces oestrogen, which rebuilds the uterine lining. A rise in oestrogen causes a surge in LH, which triggers ovulation. After ovulation, the corpus luteum produces progesterone, maintaining the uterine lining. If pregnancy does not occur, progesterone and oestrogen levels fall, the lining breaks down and menstruation begins.

16.9 Pregnancy, placenta and birth

After implantation, the embryo develops in the uterus. The placenta forms at the interface between the mother's uterine lining and fetal tissues. It allows exchange of substances between maternal and fetal blood without the two blood supplies normally mixing directly. Oxygen and nutrients such as glucose and amino acids diffuse or are transported from maternal blood to fetal blood. Carbon dioxide and urea pass from fetal blood to maternal blood for removal.

The umbilical cord connects the fetus to the placenta. The amniotic sac and amniotic fluid surround the fetus, cushioning it against mechanical shock and allowing movement. The placenta also acts as a selective barrier, but not all harmful substances are blocked; for example, alcohol, nicotine and some pathogens can cross and harm fetal development.

At birth, contractions of the muscular uterus help push the baby through the cervix and vagina. The umbilical cord is clamped and cut after delivery. The placenta is then delivered. Labour is a coordinated physiological process and the timing and details vary.

16.10 Contraception and sexually transmitted infections

Contraception reduces the chance of pregnancy. Methods include barrier methods such as condoms, hormonal methods that may prevent ovulation, intrauterine devices and surgical methods. Condoms also reduce the risk of transmission of many sexually transmitted infections (STIs), although no method gives absolute protection. Correct and consistent use matters.

STIs are infections transmitted mainly through sexual contact. Examples include HIV, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and syphilis. Some STIs can be treated with appropriate medicines; viral infections may be managed rather than cured. Testing, treatment, vaccination where available, and barrier protection help reduce transmission. Avoid stigma: people with STIs need confidential healthcare and support.

17. Inheritance

Learning outcomes: explain how genetic information is carried on chromosomes; use the terms gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, dominant, recessive, homozygous and heterozygous; interpret simple genetic diagrams; explain sex determination in humans; and describe mutation as a source of genetic variation.

Exam focus: Use correct genetic notation throughout. State the alleles carried by each parent, show the gametes, complete the genetic diagram and give both genotype and phenotype ratios when asked.

17.1 DNA, genes and chromosomes

The nucleus of a eukaryotic cell contains chromosomes. A chromosome is a long, coiled molecule of DNA associated with proteins. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that stores inherited information. A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein or functional product and can influence a characteristic. Different forms of the same gene are called alleles.

In a body cell, chromosomes occur in homologous pairs: one chromosome of each pair was inherited from the mother and the other from the father. The two chromosomes carry genes for the same characteristics at corresponding positions, although the alleles may differ. Gametes contain one chromosome from each homologous pair.

17.3 Monohybrid inheritance

A monohybrid cross investigates the inheritance of one characteristic. During meiosis, alleles separate so that each gamete receives one allele of the pair. At fertilisation, two gametes join and restore the pair.

17.4 Sex determination in humans

In the usual human chromosome pattern, females are XX and males are XY. An egg carries an X chromosome. Sperm carry either X or Y. If an X-bearing sperm fertilises the egg, the usual chromosome combination is XX; if a Y-bearing sperm fertilises it, the combination is XY. The chance of either outcome is approximately 50% for each pregnancy.

17.5 Mutation and inherited variation

A mutation is a change in genetic material. A gene mutation changes the sequence of DNA bases in a gene; chromosome mutations involve changes to chromosome structure or number. Mutations occur spontaneously, and their frequency can be increased by mutagens such as ionising radiation and some chemicals. Many mutations have no noticeable effect; some are harmful and a few may be beneficial in a particular environment.

A mutation in a cell that gives rise to gametes may be passed to offspring. A mutation in an ordinary body cell is generally not inherited by offspring. Mutations create new alleles and therefore contribute to genetic variation within populations.

17.6 Meiosis and inheritance

Meiosis is the type of cell division that produces haploid gametes. The chromosome number is halved, so fertilisation restores the diploid number. Homologous chromosomes separate during meiosis, causing alleles of a gene to be distributed into different gametes. This helps explain why each gamete carries only one allele from each pair.

17.7 Exam technique and common errors

  • Do not confuse genotype (allele combination) with phenotype (observable characteristic).
  • Do not describe a dominant allele as necessarily common, stronger or better; dominance only describes its expression in a heterozygote.
  • Write the gametes separately before completing the genetic diagram.
  • Give ratios in simplest whole-number form and identify whether a question asks for genotype or phenotype.
  • Do not state that every mutation is harmful; effects vary.

18. Variation and selection

Learning outcomes: describe variation within a species; distinguish continuous and discontinuous variation; explain genetic and environmental causes of variation; describe how mutation creates variation; explain natural selection and evolution; and describe selective breeding by humans.

18.1 Variation within a species

Variation means differences between individuals of the same species. Individuals vary in measurable features and in characteristics that fall into distinct groups. Variation is important because it provides differences on which natural selection can act.

TypeDescriptionExamples
Continuous variationValues show a range with many intermediate forms; measurements can be recorded on a scale.Human height, body mass, leaf length.
Discontinuous variationIndividuals fall into distinct categories with no intermediate values for that feature.Human ABO blood group, ability to taste a particular substance (as a simplified classroom example).

Continuous variation is often influenced by many genes and environmental factors. Discontinuous variation is often controlled by one or a few genes and has limited environmental influence, although the exact cause depends on the characteristic.

Illustrative distributions of variation

18.2 Genetic and environmental causes

Genetic variation results from differences in alleles inherited from parents. Sexual reproduction produces genetically different offspring because meiosis distributes chromosomes into gametes and fertilisation combines alleles from two parents. Mutation can create new alleles.

Environmental variation results from differences in conditions or experiences, such as diet, climate, light, water availability, exercise and disease. Many characteristics are influenced by both genes and the environment. For example, a person's height is affected by inherited genes and nutrition during growth.

18.3 Natural selection

Natural selection is the process by which individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics tend to survive and reproduce more successfully in a particular environment. The frequency of alleles associated with those characteristics can increase over generations.

  1. Individuals in a population show genetic variation.
  2. More offspring are produced than can all survive; resources may be limited and there may be competition.
  3. Individuals with characteristics that give an advantage in the current environment are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  4. They pass alleles associated with the advantageous characteristic to their offspring.
  5. Over many generations, the advantageous alleles may become more common in the population.
Exam wording: Avoid saying that organisms develop a useful feature because they need it. Variation already exists (or arises through mutation); individuals with advantageous inherited variation leave more offspring.

18.4 Example: antibiotic resistance

A bacterial population may contain a few bacteria with a mutation that gives resistance to an antibiotic. When the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria are more likely to die, while resistant bacteria survive and reproduce. The proportion of resistant bacteria can increase. Antibiotics do not deliberately cause bacteria to adapt, and resistant bacteria can pass resistance alleles to their offspring.

18.5 Adaptation and evolution

An adaptation is an inherited feature that makes an organism better suited to its environment and increases its chances of survival and reproduction. Examples include thick fur in cold environments, a streamlined body in aquatic animals and water-storage features in desert plants.

Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population over many generations. Natural selection is an important mechanism of evolution. Populations, rather than individual organisms, evolve across generations.

18.6 Selective breeding (artificial selection)

Humans carry out selective breeding to increase the frequency of desirable characteristics in domesticated plants and animals. The usual process is to select parents with the desired features, breed them together, select the best offspring and repeat over many generations.

ApplicationSelected characteristic
Crop plantsHigh yield, disease resistance, larger fruits or improved nutritional content.
Dairy cattleHigh milk production.
Meat animalsRapid growth and greater muscle mass.
Ornamental plantsFlower colour, size or shape.

Selective breeding can reduce genetic variation if a small number of parents are repeatedly used. This can increase inbreeding and the chance that harmful recessive alleles are inherited in homozygous form.
