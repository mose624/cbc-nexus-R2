TOPIC 1 • CORE + SUPPLEMENT

1. Characteristics and classification of living organisms

Learning outcomes: describe the seven characteristics of living organisms; explain why and how organisms are classified; define species; use binomial names and dichotomous keys; compare the five kingdoms; classify vertebrates, arthropods and major plant groups; and explain how DNA evidence can show evolutionary relationships (Supplement).

1.1 Characteristics of living organisms

Living organisms carry out life processes. The seven characteristics are often remembered using MRS GREN. The terms describe biological processes, not simply visible actions.

M — MovementAn action by an organism or part of it that changes position or place. Plants show movement, for example shoots growing towards light.
R — RespirationChemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism. It is not the same as breathing.
S — SensitivityThe ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment, such as light, temperature or chemicals.
G — GrowthA permanent increase in size and dry mass, resulting from an increase in cell number, cell size or both.
R — ReproductionProcesses that make more organisms of the same kind. Reproduction may be sexual or asexual.
E — ExcretionRemoval of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements, such as carbon dioxide and urea.
N — NutritionTaking in materials for energy, growth and development. Plants make organic nutrients by photosynthesis; animals obtain food by feeding.
Common examination trap: excretion is not egestion. Excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells; egestion removes undigested food from the alimentary canal. Respiration is a chemical process in cells and is not simply the movement of air into and out of lungs.

1.2 Why organisms are classified

Classification is the organisation of organisms into groups using shared features. It makes the huge diversity of living things easier to identify, study and communicate about. A useful classification system groups organisms according to similarities that reflect their relationships.

Species: a group of organisms that can reproduce with one another to produce fertile offspring. Members of the same species share many characteristics, although individuals may show variation.

1.3 The binomial system of naming

Each species is given a two-part scientific name. The first word is the genus and begins with a capital letter; the second is the species name and begins with a lower-case letter. Both words are written in italics when typed, or underlined separately when handwritten.

ExampleGenusSpecies nameCorrect presentation
HumanHomosapiensHomo sapiens

The binomial system is internationally agreed, reducing confusion caused by different local or common names for the same organism.

1.7 Viruses

Viruses are not placed in any of the five kingdoms. A virus consists of genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat. Viruses can reproduce only inside living host cells, using the host cell's machinery. They are not cells and do not carry out all seven life processes independently.

1.8 Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key identifies an organism by offering two contrasting statements at each step. Choose the statement that matches the specimen and follow the instruction until you reach an identification. Each pair should use clear, observable features that do not overlap.

1.9 DNA and evolutionary relationships (Supplement)

DNA contains a sequence of bases. When the DNA base sequences of two organisms are compared, a greater similarity generally indicates a more recent common ancestor and a closer evolutionary relationship. Classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships, not just superficial resemblance. Organisms may look similar because they live in similar environments, even when they are not closely related.

Worked example: using a key

Question: An arthropod has eight legs, no antennae and two main body regions. Which group does it belong to?

Reasoning: Eight legs indicate four pairs. The absence of antennae and the two main body regions support the identification. Answer: arachnid.

Practical and investigation skills

  • Observe specimens, photographs or models carefully and record visible features before naming an organism.
  • Use a hand lens or microscope safely; keep specimens in suitable containers and do not handle unknown organisms.
  • Construct a dichotomous key using observable, mutually exclusive paired statements.
  • Repeat identifications with another learner and resolve disagreements by checking the stated features.
  • When comparing classification using DNA data, use the same gene/region and compare equivalent base positions.

2. Organisation of the organism

Learning outcomes: compare plant, animal and bacterial cells; identify and explain the functions of cell structures; explain cell specialisation using examples; describe levels of organisation; and calculate magnification, image size and actual size using consistent units.

2.1 Cell structure: the basic unit of life

A cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of a living organism. Cells contain structures that perform particular functions. Plant and animal cells are eukaryotic: they have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Bacterial cells are prokaryotic: they do not have a nucleus or other membrane-bound organelles.

2.3 Levels of organisation

In multicellular organisms, cells work together in an organised hierarchy:

CellThe basic unit of life, for example a muscle cell.
TissueA group of similar cells working together, for example muscle tissue.
OrganA structure made of different tissues working together, for example the heart.
Organ systemA group of organs working together, for example the circulatory system.
OrganismAn individual living thing, for example a human.

2.4 Size of specimens and magnification

Magnification compares the size of an image with the actual size of the specimen. It has no unit because it is a ratio of two lengths measured in the same unit.

Formula to learn:
Magnification = image size ÷ actual size
Rearrange when needed: image size = magnification × actual size; actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

Unit conversion: 1 millimetre (mm) = 1000 micrometres (µm). To convert mm to µm, multiply by 1000. To convert µm to mm, divide by 1000. Convert both image and actual size to the same unit before calculating.

Worked example 1: calculate magnification

A drawing of a cell is 36 mm long. The actual cell is 0.12 mm long. Calculate the magnification.

Step 1: Write the formula: magnification = image size ÷ actual size.

Step 2: Substitute: 36 mm ÷ 0.12 mm = 300.

Answer: ×300. The mm units cancel, so magnification has no unit.

Worked example 2: convert units first

An image measures 24 mm. The actual specimen measures 80 µm. Calculate magnification.

Convert 24 mm to micrometres: 24 × 1000 = 24 000 µm. Then 24 000 ÷ 80 = ×300.

Worked example 3: calculate actual size

A bacterium is shown at ×1500 magnification. Its image length is 12 mm. Actual size = 12 mm ÷ 1500 = 0.008 mm. Convert to micrometres: 0.008 × 1000 = 8 µm.

Worked example 4: calculate image size

A cell is 25 µm long and is drawn at ×400. Convert actual size to mm: 25 µm = 0.025 mm. Image size = 400 × 0.025 = 10 mm.

2.5 Practical skills: observing cells

  • Prepare a thin specimen so light can pass through it. Use an appropriate stain when needed to increase contrast; stains may kill cells.
  • Place the specimen on a clean slide, add a drop of water or suitable mounting liquid, and lower a coverslip carefully to reduce air bubbles.
  • Start with the low-power objective, focus using the coarse adjustment, then use fine adjustment for a sharp image. Use fine focus at high power.
  • Do not allow the objective lens to press against the slide. Handle glass slides and coverslips carefully.
  • Draw biological diagrams with clear single lines, no shading, and labels that touch the correct structure. Include a title and magnification or a scale bar when required.
  • When measuring a specimen image, measure the longest clearly visible dimension and record the unit.

2.6 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Cell wall and cell membrane are different structures: the wall provides support; the membrane controls exchange.
  • Plant cells also have mitochondria and ribosomes. Chloroplasts are present only in photosynthetic cells, not every plant cell.
  • Not all plant cells have chloroplasts; root cells, for example, are generally not photosynthetic.
  • Bacteria are cells, but they are not eukaryotic and have no nucleus.
  • Do not confuse tissue with organ: an organ contains different tissues working together.
  • In magnification calculations, convert to matching units before dividing. Never attach mm or µm to the final magnification.

TOPIC 3 • CORE + SUPPLEMENT

3. Movement into and out of cells

Learning outcomes: explain diffusion, osmosis and active transport; relate each process to concentration gradients and cell membranes; explain why these processes matter in living organisms; investigate factors affecting diffusion and the effects of osmosis on plant tissues; and interpret practical results.

3.1 The cell membrane and movement of substances

The cell membrane forms the boundary between a cell and its surroundings. It is selectively permeable: some substances cross it more easily than others. Cells exchange substances with their environment to obtain useful materials and remove waste. Three important processes are diffusion, osmosis and active transport.

Do not confuse the three processes: diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient; osmosis is the net movement of water through a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential; active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration.

3.2 Diffusion (Core)

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement. Molecules and ions have kinetic energy and move randomly. Even though particles move in both directions, there is an overall movement from the region where they are more concentrated to the region where they are less concentrated.

The energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of the random movement of molecules and ions. Diffusion is a passive process: the cell does not use ATP to make particles move down the gradient.

Examples and importance of diffusion

  • Gas exchange in the lungs: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where its concentration is higher, into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.
  • Gas exchange in leaves: carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through stomata for photosynthesis; oxygen can diffuse out.
  • Respiring tissues: oxygen diffuses from blood into cells, while carbon dioxide produced by respiration diffuses from cells into the blood.
  • Digestion and absorption: some digested soluble molecules move across membranes down their concentration gradients.

Factors affecting the rate of diffusion

FactorEffect on rateReason
Concentration gradientA steeper gradient increases the rate.There is a greater difference in concentration, so there is greater net movement per unit time.
TemperatureHigher temperature generally increases the rate.Particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, increasing the rate of spreading.
Surface areaA larger surface area increases the rate.More particles can cross the exchange surface at the same time.
Diffusion distanceA shorter distance increases the rate.Particles have a shorter pathway to cross.
Exam application: alveoli and many exchange surfaces have a large surface area, thin walls and a good blood supply. These features help maintain a steep concentration gradient and a short diffusion distance.

3.3 Osmosis (Core and Supplement)

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (a more dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (a more concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane. A partially permeable membrane allows water molecules to pass through but restricts at least some dissolved solute particles.

At IGCSE level, remember that water moves in both directions across the membrane. Osmosis describes the net movement. The water potential explanation is Supplement content; the core description focuses on water diffusing through a partially permeable membrane.

Why water is important as a solvent

  • Digestion: many enzymes act on dissolved substances; soluble products can be transported in watery fluids.
  • Transport: blood plasma and plant sap carry dissolved nutrients, ions, hormones and waste products.
  • Excretion: nitrogenous waste such as urea is transported in solution and removed in urine.

Osmosis in plant cells (Supplement)

Surrounding solutionWater movementEffect on plant cell
More dilute than cell sap (higher water potential)Water enters the cell by osmosis.Vacuole expands; cytoplasm presses against the cell wall; the cell becomes turgid. The outward pressure is turgor pressure. The cell wall prevents the cell from bursting.
Similar water potential to cell sapNo net movement of water.The cell may be flaccid if it has lost turgor; it is not strongly supported by internal pressure.
More concentrated than cell sap (lower water potential)Water leaves the cell by osmosis.The vacuole and cytoplasm shrink. The cell becomes flaccid; in severe water loss the cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall, called plasmolysis.

Importance to organisms: water uptake by root hair cells helps replace water lost from leaves; turgor pressure supports non-woody plant tissues. Loss of water can make leaves and young stems wilt. Osmosis also affects water balance in animal cells, which do not have a rigid cell wall.

Investigating osmosis in plant tissue

A common investigation uses equal-sized potato cylinders placed in different sucrose or salt concentrations. Measure their initial mass, immerse them for the same time, remove them, gently blot away surface liquid and measure the final mass.

VariableHow to control or measure it
IndependentConcentration of sucrose solution; use a range of known concentrations.
DependentChange in mass or percentage change in mass of each potato cylinder.
ControlledPotato source, cylinder dimensions, solution volume, immersion time, temperature and blotting method.

Percentage change in mass = ((final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass) × 100. A positive result means mass increased; a negative result means mass decreased. At the concentration where percentage change is approximately zero, there is no net movement of water into or out of the tissue under those conditions.

Worked example: percentage change in mass

A potato cylinder has an initial mass of 5.0 g and a final mass of 5.8 g.

Change in mass = 5.8 − 5.0 = 0.8 g.

Percentage change = (0.8 ÷ 5.0) × 100 = +16%. The positive sign shows that the potato gained mass because water entered its cells by osmosis.

Dialysis tubing investigation

Dialysis tubing can model a partially permeable membrane. Soak and rinse the tubing as instructed, tie one end, add a measured volume of concentrated sucrose solution, remove air, tie the other end and rinse the outside. Record its initial mass, place it in distilled water for a fixed time, blot it consistently and record its final mass. An increase in mass indicates net water entry by osmosis. Use eye protection where required, clean spills promptly and do not taste any laboratory materials.

3.4 Active transport (Core and Supplement)

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from respiration. The energy is supplied by ATP made during respiration. In Supplement content, explain that protein carrier molecules in the membrane bind and move particular ions or molecules across the membrane.

Example — mineral ion uptake by root hair cells: mineral ions in the soil solution may be at a lower concentration than inside a root hair cell. The cell can absorb these ions against the concentration gradient using carrier proteins and energy from respiration. The ions are needed for healthy plant growth, including the synthesis of proteins and chlorophyll.

ProcessDirection of net movementMembrane required?Energy from respiration?
DiffusionHigher to lower concentrationNot always; across a membrane in cell exchangeNo
OsmosisHigher to lower water potentialYes, partially permeable membraneNo
Active transportLower to higher concentrationYes, with carrier proteinsYes

3.5 Practical planning, data and evaluation

  • Repeat measurements and calculate a mean to improve reliability and identify anomalous results.
  • Use equal-sized tissue pieces and the same immersion time, temperature and solution volume when comparing osmosis conditions.
  • Blot samples in the same way before weighing; surface solution left on a sample can distort its mass.
  • Use a suitable range of concentrations. A graph of percentage change in mass against solution concentration can show the concentration at which there is no net mass change.
  • For diffusion investigations, change one factor at a time and keep other factors constant. For example, compare equal-sized agar blocks at different temperatures or use different surface-area-to-volume ratios.
  • State the independent variable, dependent variable and important control variables clearly. Include units and repeat readings.

3.6 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Diffusion does not stop when concentrations become equal: particles continue random movement, but there is no net movement.
  • Osmosis is specifically the net movement of water, not the movement of any solute.
  • A partially permeable membrane is essential to the definition of osmosis.
  • Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential. A dilute solution has a higher water potential than a concentrated solution.
  • Active transport moves against a concentration gradient and requires energy from respiration; diffusion and osmosis are passive.
  • In an osmosis practical, a mass gain is evidence of net water entry; a mass loss is evidence of net water loss.

4. Biological molecules

Learning outcomes: identify the elements in carbohydrates, fats and proteins; describe the smaller molecules from which selected large molecules are made; carry out and interpret five food tests; and, for Supplement learners, describe the basic structure and complementary base pairing of DNA. This lesson follows section 4.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for 2026–2028.

4.1 Elements in biological molecules

Living organisms contain organic molecules built mainly from carbon. The elements you must know for this syllabus section are:

Biological moleculeElementsKey point
CarbohydratesCarbon, hydrogen and oxygen (C, H, O)Many carbohydrates contain hydrogen and oxygen in an approximate 2:1 ratio, but learn the elements rather than assuming every molecule has that exact ratio.
Fats and oils (lipids)Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (C, H, O)They generally contain proportionally less oxygen than carbohydrates.
ProteinsCarbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen (C, H, O, N)Some proteins also contain sulfur, but the essential syllabus answer is CHON.
Exam precision: if asked to list the elements in carbohydrates or fats, give C, H and O. For proteins, include nitrogen as well. Do not confuse the elements in a molecule with the smaller molecules from which it is built.

4.2 Large molecules are built from smaller molecules

Small molecules can join to form larger molecules. The syllabus limits the examples you need to the following three groups:

CarbohydratesStarch, glycogen and cellulose are large molecules made from many glucose molecules joined together.
ProteinsProteins are made from amino acid molecules joined in long chains. Different sequences produce different proteins.
Fats and oilsA fat or oil molecule is made from one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acid molecules.

Carbohydrates: glucose, starch, glycogen and cellulose

Glucose is a simple sugar. Many glucose molecules can be joined to form larger carbohydrate molecules. Starch is an energy-storage carbohydrate in plants; glycogen is an energy-storage carbohydrate in animals and fungi; cellulose is a structural carbohydrate in plant cell walls. For this syllabus objective, remember that all three are made from glucose, but their structures and functions differ.

Large carbohydrateMade fromMain role / where found
StarchGlucose unitsEnergy store in plants, for example in seeds and storage organs.
GlycogenGlucose unitsEnergy store in animals, especially in liver and muscle cells.
CelluloseGlucose unitsForms strong plant cell walls and provides structural support.

Proteins and amino acids

Amino acids are small molecules that join to make protein chains. A protein's amino acid sequence determines how it folds into a particular three-dimensional shape. Proteins have many roles in organisms, including acting as enzymes, forming some cell structures, transporting substances and contributing to growth and repair. The food test detects protein; it does not identify the exact protein present.

Fats and oils: glycerol and fatty acids

Fats and oils are lipids. A typical triglyceride has one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acid molecules. Lipids are energy stores and can also provide thermal insulation and form part of cell membranes. Fats are usually solid at room temperature, while oils are usually liquid, but both belong to the lipid group.

4.3 Food tests: method, observation and conclusion

Food tests use chemical reagents to detect particular substances. Use a small, labelled sample, add the correct reagent and record the observation before drawing a conclusion. If testing a solid food, crush a small amount with distilled water and use the resulting mixture or filtrate as appropriate.

Remember the colour changes: iodine: orange-brown → blue-black (starch); Benedict’s: blue → green/yellow/orange/brick-red after heating (reducing sugar); Biuret: blue → lilac/purple (protein); ethanol emulsion: clear → milky white (lipid); DCPIP: blue → colourless (vitamin C).

Test 1 — iodine solution for starch

  1. Place a small amount of the food sample on a spotting tile or in a test tube.
  2. Add a few drops of iodine solution.
  3. Observe the colour. A blue-black colour is a positive result for starch.

Iodine solution is orange-brown before the test. A negative result means it does not turn blue-black. Use a clean pipette for each sample to avoid contamination.

Test 2 — Benedict’s solution for reducing sugars

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube and add a suitable volume of Benedict’s solution.
  2. Heat the mixture in a hot water bath for several minutes, using the teacher's laboratory instructions.
  3. Record the final colour and whether a precipitate forms.

A positive result may range from green through yellow and orange to brick-red. The colour often gives an approximate indication of the amount of reducing sugar, but it is not a precise concentration measurement unless a calibrated method is used. A negative result remains blue. Heating is required for the standard test.

Test 3 — Biuret test for protein

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube.
  2. Add Biuret reagent, or follow the approved sequence using sodium hydroxide and dilute copper(II) sulfate.
  3. Mix gently and observe the colour.

A lilac or purple colour indicates protein. If the solution remains blue, the test is negative. The test detects peptide bonds in proteins; it is not a test for starch or simple sugars.

Test 4 — ethanol emulsion test for fats and oils

  1. Add ethanol to the food sample and shake carefully to dissolve any lipid present.
  2. Allow solid material to settle if necessary, then pour the ethanol extract into water.
  3. Look for a cloudy white emulsion.

A milky-white emulsion is a positive result. A clear mixture is a negative result. Ethanol is flammable: keep it away from naked flames and use the laboratory's approved safety procedure. Never heat ethanol directly.

Test 5 — DCPIP test for vitamin C

  1. Place a measured volume of blue DCPIP solution in a test tube or spotting well.
  2. Add the food extract drop by drop, mixing consistently.
  3. Record whether the blue colour disappears and, when comparing samples, the volume needed to decolourise the same starting amount of DCPIP.

Vitamin C reduces DCPIP, causing its blue colour to disappear. If equal volumes and concentrations of DCPIP are used, a sample that decolourises it with fewer drops is generally more concentrated in vitamin C. Keep the starting DCPIP volume and concentration the same for a fair comparison.

4.4 Planning reliable food-test investigations

When comparing foods or estimating relative nutrient content, make the test fair. Use equal sample masses or volumes, the same reagent volumes and concentrations, the same heating time and temperature where heating is required, and the same observation method. Repeat tests and record results in a table.

Investigation featureExample for comparing reducing sugar in food samples
Independent variableType of food sample, or its known dilution.
Dependent variableColour / precipitate after Benedict’s test; for a quantitative method, a calibrated measurement.
Control variablesMass of food, volume of extract, volume and concentration of Benedict’s solution, water-bath temperature and heating time.
ReliabilityRepeat each test and compare consistent results.

For vitamin C comparisons using DCPIP, keep the initial DCPIP volume and concentration constant and add each food extract in the same way. A known vitamin C solution can be used as a positive comparison. Record the volume needed to remove the blue colour; do not compare results if the starting conditions differ.

Laboratory safety

  • Wear eye protection and follow the teacher's instructions for all reagents.
  • Use a hot water bath and a test-tube holder for Benedict’s test; point test tubes away from people.
  • Ethanol is flammable. Keep it away from flames, sparks and hot surfaces; never heat it directly.
  • Do not taste samples or reagents. Wash hands and clean work areas after testing.
  • Use small quantities, label tubes and dispose of chemicals as instructed.

4.5 DNA structure (Supplement)

DNA is a molecule that carries genetic information. It consists of two strands coiled together to form a double helix. Each strand contains chemical units called bases. Bonds between pairs of bases hold the two strands together. The bases pair in a specific complementary way: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G. Full names of the bases are not required for this syllabus objective.

How to describe DNA in an examination

  • State that DNA has two strands.
  • Describe the strands as coiled together in a double helix.
  • State that each strand contains bases.
  • Explain that bonds between paired bases hold the strands together.
  • Give the complementary pairs accurately: A with T; C with G.
Core versus Supplement: elements, the listed building blocks and all five food tests are Core. The DNA double-helix and base-pairing description is Supplement (Extended) content.

4.6 Worked examples

Worked example 1 — identify an unknown food substance

Question: A food extract is blue before heating with Benedict’s solution. After heating in a water bath, it forms an orange precipitate. What can be concluded?

Answer: The sample contains a reducing sugar. The evidence is the change from blue to orange after heating. The result does not identify which reducing sugar is present.

Worked example 2 — interpret a set of food tests

Results: iodine remains orange-brown; Biuret turns lilac; ethanol emulsion becomes milky; DCPIP becomes colourless.

Interpretation: no starch detected; protein detected; lipid detected; vitamin C detected. A negative iodine test does not mean the food contains no carbohydrate at all, because it may contain sugars rather than starch.

Worked example 3 — complementary DNA bases

Question: One DNA strand has the base sequence A–C–T–G–G. Write the complementary sequence.

Method: replace A with T, T with A, C with G and G with C.

Answer: T–G–A–C–C.

4.7 Common misconceptions and examination technique

  • Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made from glucose, but they are not interchangeable: they have different structures and roles.
  • Proteins are made from amino acids, not glucose.
  • Fats and oils are made from glycerol and fatty acids, not amino acids.
  • Benedict’s solution must be heated in the standard reducing-sugar test; iodine does not require heating.
  • A positive iodine result is blue-black, not blue alone; a positive Biuret result is lilac / purple.
  • The ethanol emulsion test is read after the ethanol extract is added to water. A cloudy white emulsion indicates lipid.
  • DCPIP is blue before the test and becomes colourless when reduced by vitamin C.
  • A negative starch test does not prove that a food contains no carbohydrate.
  • For DNA, do not reverse the pairings: A–T and C–G. The strands are coiled into a double helix.

5. Enzymes

Learning outcomes: define catalysts and enzymes; explain why enzymes are essential to metabolism; describe enzyme action using active sites, substrates, enzyme–substrate complexes and products; explain enzyme specificity; investigate how temperature and pH affect enzyme activity; and explain optimum conditions and denaturation.

5.1 Catalysts and biological catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. It is not used up, so a small amount can catalyse many reaction cycles.

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts. They are involved in metabolic reactions in living cells. Metabolism is the sum of the chemical reactions taking place in an organism, including reactions that build molecules (anabolism) and reactions that break molecules down (catabolism).

Build-up reactionsEnzymes help join smaller molecules to make larger molecules, for example reactions involved in making proteins.
Breakdown reactionsEnzymes help break large molecules into smaller molecules, for example digestion of starch and proteins.
Why they matterAt normal cell temperatures, many reactions would be too slow without enzymes. Enzymes make reaction rates fast enough to sustain life.
Remember: enzymes are not living organisms. They are proteins that catalyse reactions. The enzyme remains chemically unchanged at the end of a reaction and can be used again, provided it has not been denatured or damaged.

5.2 How enzymes work

The molecule on which an enzyme acts is called its substrate. A specific region of the enzyme, called the active site, has a shape that is complementary to the substrate. The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. The reaction occurs and product(s) are released. The enzyme is unchanged and can catalyse another reaction.

  1. The substrate moves into the enzyme's active site.
  2. The substrate binds to the active site because their shapes are complementary.
  3. An enzyme–substrate complex forms.
  4. The reaction converts the substrate into product(s).
  5. The product(s) leave the active site; the enzyme can be reused.

Extended explanation: enzyme specificity means that an enzyme usually catalyses only one type of reaction or acts on a particular substrate. The active site's three-dimensional shape and chemical properties are complementary to the substrate. A substrate with a different shape will not fit correctly and will not form the required enzyme–substrate complex.

5.3 Temperature and enzyme activity

As temperature rises from a low value towards the enzyme's optimum temperature, enzyme activity usually increases. Substrate and enzyme molecules gain kinetic energy, move faster and collide more frequently. More collisions have enough energy and the correct orientation to form enzyme–substrate complexes, so the rate increases.

Above the optimum temperature, the enzyme's structure begins to change. Bonds that help maintain its three-dimensional shape are disrupted. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits properly. The enzyme is denatured and its activity falls rapidly. Denaturation is usually permanent; cooling a denatured enzyme does not restore its original active-site shape.

At low temperatures, enzyme activity is slow because molecules have less kinetic energy and there are fewer successful collisions. Low temperature generally does not denature the enzyme; activity can increase again when the temperature is raised to a suitable value.

Do not confuse: low temperature slows enzyme activity but normally does not denature the enzyme. High temperature can denature it by changing the active site's shape.

5.4 pH and enzyme activity

Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which it works fastest. A change in pH can alter charges and bonds within the protein, changing the shape of the active site. The substrate may no longer fit as well, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate decreases. At an extreme pH, the enzyme may be denatured.

Different enzymes have different optimum pH values because they normally work in different parts of organisms. For example, pepsin in the stomach works best in acidic conditions, while enzymes acting in the small intestine generally work best in neutral to alkaline conditions. The optimum pH is not necessarily pH 7.

5.5 Investigating enzyme activity using amylase and starch

A common school investigation uses amylase to break down starch. Iodine solution is used to test for starch: iodine turns blue-black when starch is present and remains orange-brown when starch is absent. As amylase digests the starch, the time taken for a sample to stop turning iodine blue-black can be measured.

Method

  1. Place drops of iodine solution into separate wells of a spotting tile.
  2. Measure equal volumes of starch solution and amylase solution into labelled test tubes.
  3. Place the solutions in a water bath at the chosen temperature until they reach that temperature.
  4. Mix the amylase and starch and start the stopwatch immediately.
  5. At regular, fixed time intervals, remove a small drop of the reaction mixture and add it to a fresh iodine drop.
  6. Record the time when iodine no longer turns blue-black and remains orange-brown. This indicates that no detectable starch remains.
  7. Repeat the investigation and calculate a mean time. Use fresh solutions and clean equipment to avoid contamination.

Testing the effect of temperature

Repeat the procedure at a range of temperatures, for example 20°C, 30°C, 40°C, 50°C and 60°C. Use the same concentrations and volumes of starch and amylase, the same pH, the same sampling interval and the same endpoint definition. Keep the reaction mixture in the water bath throughout the test.

Testing the effect of pH

Repeat the reaction using buffer solutions of different pH values. Keep temperature, enzyme concentration, starch concentration, volumes and sampling intervals constant. Buffers help maintain the chosen pH during the reaction.

Improving reliability: repeat each condition at least three times, identify anomalous results, calculate a mean from consistent repeats and use the same endpoint judgement each time. Use a thermostatically controlled water bath and allow solutions to equilibrate before mixing.

Limitations: the iodine endpoint is judged by eye and may be subjective; sampling intervals limit the precision of the recorded time; temperature may change during sampling; and contamination or inaccurate volumes may affect the rate. Use a colorimeter or a more precise sampling method if available and appropriate.

5.6 Processing results and calculating rate

When the endpoint is the time taken for starch to disappear, a shorter time means the reaction is faster. A simple comparative rate can be calculated as:

Relative rate = 1 ÷ time taken
Use the same units for all times. The value is a relative measure for comparing trials, not necessarily the absolute rate in concentration per second.

Worked example

Question: At 30°C, starch disappears in 80 seconds. At 40°C, it disappears in 40 seconds. Calculate the relative rate at each temperature and compare them.

TemperatureTime (s)Relative rate (s⁻¹)
30°C801 ÷ 80 = 0.0125
40°C401 ÷ 40 = 0.0250

Conclusion: the relative rate at 40°C is twice the rate at 30°C because the time taken is half as long. This comparison is valid only if all other conditions were kept the same.

5.7 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Enzymes are proteins and biological catalysts; they increase reaction rate and are not used up by the reaction.
  • The substrate binds to the active site; the product is released after the reaction.
  • The enzyme–substrate complex is temporary.
  • Specificity is explained by the complementary shape and fit of the active site and substrate.
  • Increasing temperature raises activity only up to the optimum; above it, denaturation causes activity to fall.
  • Low temperature usually slows activity without denaturing the enzyme.
  • Each enzyme has its own optimum pH; do not state that all enzymes work best at pH 7.
  • Denaturation changes the enzyme's three-dimensional shape, especially the active site, so the substrate no longer fits properly.
  • In the starch test, blue-black means starch is present; orange-brown means no starch is detected.
  • For a fair test, change one independent variable at a time and control the other conditions.
