1. Principles of chemistry: particles, atoms and formulae

Matter is made of particles. In a solid, particles are close together and vibrate about fixed positions; in a liquid, particles remain close but move past one another; in a gas, particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly. Changes of state are physical changes: the substance's chemical identity remains the same.

Atomic structure

An atom contains protons and neutrons in a tiny nucleus, with electrons occupying shells or energy levels. A proton has relative charge +1 and relative mass 1; a neutron has charge 0 and relative mass 1; an electron has charge −1 and a very small relative mass. Atomic number is the number of protons. Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Ions form when atoms or groups of atoms gain or lose electrons. Losing electrons forms positive ions; gaining electrons forms negative ions.

Formulae and equations

A chemical formula shows the elements and relative numbers of atoms or ions in a substance. A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides; balance by changing coefficients, never by changing subscripts in formulae.

relative formula mass (Mᵣ) = sum of the relative atomic masses in the formula
Exam tip: Check that ionic compounds have an overall charge of zero and that every atom is balanced in a symbol equation.

Further textbook notes: states, atoms and formulae

In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions; in liquids they remain close but move past one another; in gases they are widely spaced and move randomly. Changes of state are physical changes because the particles themselves do not become different substances. During melting or boiling, energy is used to overcome attractions between particles.

An atom contains protons and neutrons in a tiny nucleus and electrons in shells or energy levels. Proton number identifies an element; a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Relative atomic mass is a weighted mean based on isotope abundance.

Worked example: An atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. Its proton number is 11 and nucleon number is 23. It is a neutral sodium-23 atom.

Practise: Explain why isotopes have similar chemical properties and distinguish an element, compound and mixture.

2. Bonding and structure

Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, usually formed when electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal. Ionic compounds form giant lattices. They usually have high melting points and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved because ions are mobile, but not when solid because ions are fixed.

Covalent bonding involves a shared pair of electrons between atoms. Simple molecular substances often have low melting and boiling points because intermolecular forces are relatively weak; covalent bonds within molecules are strong. Giant covalent structures, such as diamond and silicon dioxide, have many strong covalent bonds and high melting points. Graphite has layers and delocalised electrons, so it conducts electricity.

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons. Metals conduct electricity and heat; layers of ions can slide, explaining malleability.

Further textbook notes: bonding and properties

Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice. Metals lose electrons to form cations; non-metals gain electrons to form anions. Ionic compounds conduct electricity when molten or dissolved because ions can move, but not when solid because ions are fixed in the lattice.

Covalent bonding involves shared pairs of electrons. Simple molecular substances often have low melting and boiling points because intermolecular forces are relatively weak, even though covalent bonds within molecules are strong. Giant covalent structures such as diamond and silicon dioxide have many strong covalent bonds and high melting points. Graphite has layers and delocalised electrons, allowing electrical conduction.

Metallic bonding is attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons. The mobile electrons explain electrical and thermal conductivity; layers of ions can slide, explaining malleability.

3. Formulae, equations and the mole

The mole is the amount of substance containing the Avogadro number of specified particles. Relative formula mass is found by adding the relative atomic masses in a formula.

moles n = mass m ÷ molar mass M
mass = moles × molar mass

Use grams for mass and g mol⁻¹ for molar mass in the common school calculation. The balanced equation gives the mole ratio between reactants and products.

Worked example

Calculate the amount of substance in 10.0 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. Mᵣ = 40.1 + 12.0 + (3 × 16.0) = 100.1. Moles = 10.0 ÷ 100.1 = 0.0999 mol (3 significant figures).

For a limiting-reactant question, calculate the available moles of each reactant, divide by its equation coefficient, and identify the smallest result. That reactant is used up first.

Further textbook notes: reacting masses and concentration

A balanced equation gives the mole ratio in which substances react. Convert mass to moles using n = m/Mr, use the equation ratio, then convert the required moles back to mass or volume. The limiting reactant is used up first and determines the maximum product formed. Percentage yield = actual yield/theoretical yield × 100%.

For solutions, concentration in mol/dm³ = moles/volume in dm³. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. In titration calculations, use the balanced equation ratio rather than assuming a 1:1 reaction.

Worked example: 0.20 mol of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. The equation gives 1 mol Mg to 1 mol H₂, so 0.20 mol Mg produces 0.20 mol H₂ theoretically.

Practise: Calculate the mass of product from a balanced equation and identify the limiting reactant when two reactant amounts are supplied.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: For 2Mg+O₂→2MgO, 2 mol Mg produces 2 mol MgO. If 0.5 mol Mg reacts fully, it forms 0.5 mol MgO.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Show model answer and mark allocation

4. Acids, alkalis, salts and pH

An acid produces hydrogen ions in aqueous solution. An alkali is a soluble base that produces hydroxide ions in water. The pH scale indicates acidity or alkalinity: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is alkaline at the stated conditions. Universal indicator gives a range of colours; a pH meter gives a more precise reading.

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
acid + base → salt + water
acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Neutralisation is the reaction of hydrogen ions with hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). A salt is named from the metal or ammonium ion and the acid: hydrochloric acid forms chlorides, sulfuric acid forms sulfates, and nitric acid forms nitrates.

Preparing a soluble salt

For an acid and an insoluble base, warm dilute acid, add excess base until no more reacts, filter off excess solid, then evaporate some water and allow crystals to form. Wash and dry the crystals.

In titration, use a suitable indicator, record initial and final burette readings, repeat to obtain concordant titres, and use the mean of concordant results.

Further textbook notes: acids, bases and salt preparation

Acids donate H⁺ ions in aqueous solution. Alkalis are soluble bases that produce OH⁻ ions. Neutralisation is the reaction of H⁺ and OH⁻ to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Acids react with metals to form a salt and hydrogen, with carbonates to form a salt, water and carbon dioxide, and with bases to form a salt and water.

To prepare a soluble salt from an insoluble base, warm dilute acid, add excess base until no more reacts, filter off the excess solid, evaporate some water and allow crystals to form. To prepare a salt from an acid and an alkali, use titration to find exact neutralisation volumes, then repeat without indicator to obtain a pure solution for crystallisation.

Practise: Write balanced symbol equations for acid reactions and explain why excess insoluble base is filtered off before crystallisation.

Worked example: Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water. Net ionic equation: H⁺+OH⁻→H₂O.

5. Rates of reaction and collision theory

Particles must collide for a reaction to occur. A collision is successful only if particles have sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy and have a suitable orientation. Increasing temperature increases average kinetic energy and the proportion of particles with energy at or above activation energy.

Increasing concentration or gas pressure increases collision frequency. Increasing the surface area of a solid exposes more particles for collisions. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy and is not used up overall.

Rates can be investigated by measuring gas volume produced, mass lost, or time taken for a visible change such as a precipitate to obscure a mark. Keep control variables constant and repeat measurements.

mean rate = change in measured quantity ÷ time taken

Further textbook notes: rate experiments

Rate of reaction can be measured by tracking loss of mass when a gas escapes, gas volume produced, or change in concentration. A graph of product formed against time is steepest when the reaction is fastest; the gradient at a particular point gives instantaneous rate. Keep the method, apparatus and measurement intervals consistent.

Increasing temperature raises the particles’ average kinetic energy and the proportion with energy at least equal to activation energy. Increasing concentration or gas pressure increases collision frequency. Increasing surface area exposes more particles for collisions. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is not used up overall.

Fair test: Change one independent variable, measure the dependent variable and control other factors such as mass, volume, temperature and particle size. Repeat readings and identify anomalies.

Practise: Explain collision theory using particle language and interpret two rate curves, including the effect of a catalyst.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Increasing temperature raises particle kinetic energy and the fraction of collisions with energy equal to or greater than activation energy, so more collisions are successful each second.

6. Energetics: exothermic and endothermic reactions

An exothermic reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, which usually rise in temperature. An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings, which usually cool. Combustion, many neutralisations and oxidation reactions are exothermic; thermal decomposition is commonly endothermic.

Breaking bonds requires energy; making bonds releases energy. Overall energy change depends on the difference between energy absorbed to break bonds and energy released when new bonds form.

energy change = energy absorbed − energy released

Reaction profile diagrams show reactant and product energy levels, activation energy and overall energy change. For an exothermic reaction, products are at lower energy than reactants; for an endothermic reaction, products are at higher energy.

Further textbook notes: energy profiles

In an exothermic reaction, energy is transferred to the surroundings and the products have lower chemical energy than the reactants. In an endothermic reaction, energy is taken in and products have higher chemical energy. The enthalpy change is negative for exothermic reactions and positive for endothermic reactions.

Activation energy is the minimum energy particles need for a successful reaction. A catalyst lowers activation energy by providing an alternative reaction pathway; it does not change the energy difference between reactants and products. On an energy profile, label reactants, products, activation energy and overall energy change.

Practise: Draw and label an energy profile for an exothermic reaction and explain why a catalyst increases rate without changing the final equilibrium position in a reversible system.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: An exothermic energy profile has products at lower energy than reactants. The vertical difference is negative ΔH; a catalyst lowers the activation-energy peak.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Show model answer and mark allocation

Examiner’s explanation: A catalyst changes activation energy, not the overall enthalpy change.

7. Electrolysis and redox

Electrolysis uses direct current to decompose an ionic substance when molten or in aqueous solution. Positive ions (cations) move to the negative cathode and gain electrons (reduction). Negative ions (anions) move to the positive anode and lose electrons (oxidation).

reduction = gain of electrons
oxidation = loss of electrons

In molten ionic compounds, the metal is produced at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode. In aqueous solutions, products depend on the ions present and the relative ease of discharge; learn the specified product rules for your course.

Half-equations show electron transfer and must balance both atoms and charge. For example: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.

Further textbook notes: electrolysis and electron transfer

Electrolysis requires mobile ions in a molten ionic compound or aqueous electrolyte. Positive ions move to the negative cathode and are reduced by gaining electrons. Negative ions move to the positive anode and are oxidised by losing electrons. Remember: OIL RIG—oxidation is loss, reduction is gain of electrons.

In molten compounds, the ions present determine the products. In aqueous solutions, water also contributes ions, so product selection depends on the relative reactivity of the ions and the electrode material. In electroplating, the object to be coated is the cathode; the coating metal may be the anode and the electrolyte contains ions of that metal.

Practise: Predict products for a stated molten electrolyte, write half-equations and identify oxidation and reduction.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: At the cathode, Cu²⁺+2e⁻→Cu. Copper ions gain electrons, so reduction occurs.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Write the cathode half-equation for molten lead(II) bromide and identify the process.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Pb²⁺+2e⁻→Pb (1); reduction/gain of electrons (1).

Examiner’s explanation: At the cathode positive ions gain electrons.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Show answer and mark scheme

8. The periodic table and chemical trends

Elements are arranged in order of increasing proton number. Elements in the same group have similar outer-shell electron arrangements and often similar chemical properties. Across a period, proton number increases and elements change from metals towards non-metals.

Group 1 metals form +1 ions and become more reactive down the group because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. Group 7 halogens form −1 ions; their reactivity decreases down the group because gaining an electron becomes less favourable. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution.

Noble gases have full outer electron shells and are generally unreactive. Transition elements often form coloured compounds, have variable oxidation states and can act as catalysts.

Further textbook notes: periodic trends and group chemistry

Elements in the same group have the same number of outer-shell electrons and therefore similar chemical properties. Across a period, proton number increases and electrons are added to the same main shell. Group 1 metals become more reactive down the group because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.

Group 7 halogens become less reactive down the group because atoms become larger and shielding increases, making it harder to attract an additional electron. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution. Noble gases are very unreactive because their outer electron shells are full.

Practise: Predict a displacement reaction and explain a trend using electron shells, shielding and attraction to the nucleus.

9. Extraction and uses of metals

Metals differ in reactivity. A metal more reactive than carbon is generally extracted from its compound by electrolysis; a less reactive metal may be extracted by reduction with carbon or carbon monoxide. Very unreactive metals can occur native in the Earth's crust.

Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain. In extraction, metal ions are reduced to metal atoms. The reactivity series helps predict displacement reactions and suitable extraction methods.

Alloys are mixtures containing a metal and one or more other elements. Different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers in a pure metal, making it harder for layers to slide; alloys are often harder than the pure metal.

Further textbook notes: metal extraction and corrosion

The method of extraction depends on a metal’s position in the reactivity series. Metals below carbon can often be extracted by reduction of their oxides with carbon or carbon monoxide. Metals above carbon are generally extracted by electrolysis of molten compounds, which requires substantial electrical energy.

In the blast furnace, carbon monoxide reduces iron oxide to iron. Limestone decomposes to calcium oxide, which reacts with acidic impurities such as silicon dioxide to form slag. Aluminium extraction uses electrolysis of aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite; cryolite lowers the operating temperature and energy demand.

Rusting requires both oxygen and water. Prevent rusting by excluding them with paint, oil or plastic, by galvanising with zinc, or by sacrificial protection. Recycling metals conserves finite ores and often uses less energy than extraction.

Practise: Compare extraction routes for iron and aluminium and explain how sacrificial protection works.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Iron is extracted by reduction of iron oxide with carbon monoxide in the blast furnace. Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis because it is more reactive than carbon.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Show model answer and mark allocation

Examiner’s explanation: Base the comparison on relative reactivity.

10. Organic chemistry: crude oil, alkanes and alkenes

Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation separates it into fractions according to differences in boiling point. Smaller hydrocarbon molecules generally have lower boiling points, lower viscosity and greater volatility than larger molecules.

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only single carbon–carbon bonds; their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for open-chain molecules. Alkenes contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond and are unsaturated; for a simple open-chain alkene with one double bond the general formula is CₙH₂ₙ.

Alkenes decolourise bromine water from orange/brown to colourless in the standard test. Cracking breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter alkanes and alkenes using heat and a catalyst or high temperature. Complete combustion in excess oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water; incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot).

Further textbook notes: hydrocarbons and polymers

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation separates fractions by boiling range: the column is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Shorter-chain hydrocarbons generally have lower boiling points, are more volatile and more flammable, and flow more easily than long-chain fractions.

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons with only carbon–carbon single bonds and general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Alkenes contain a carbon–carbon double bond and undergo addition reactions; bromine water is decolourised by an alkene. Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water; incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide or soot.

Addition polymerisation joins many alkene monomers as the double bond opens. The polymer repeat unit should be drawn with bonds continuing through the brackets.

Practise: Name a simple alkane and alkene, write a balanced combustion equation and draw a polymer repeat unit.

11. Chemical analysis and practical skills

Pure substances have fixed melting and boiling points at a stated pressure; impurities usually change and broaden melting ranges. Chromatography separates substances because they have different solubilities in the solvent and attractions to the stationary phase.

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front

For paper chromatography, draw the baseline in pencil, place a small concentrated spot, keep the solvent below the baseline, allow the solvent to rise, mark the solvent front promptly and calculate Rf values. Rf values are only comparable under the same conditions.

Common gas tests: hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper. Follow the specified safe procedures and do not directly inhale gases.

Further textbook notes: purity and analytical methods

A pure substance has a sharp melting point and boiling point at a specified pressure. Impurities usually lower and broaden a melting range. Paper chromatography separates soluble substances because they distribute differently between the mobile solvent and stationary paper. A substance that travels farther relative to the solvent has a larger Rf value.

Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent front. Both distances are measured from the baseline. Keep the baseline above the solvent level and use pencil because ink may dissolve. A chromatogram can compare samples with known standards, but matching Rf values alone do not prove identity.

Gas tests include hydrogen giving a squeaky pop with a lit splint, oxygen relighting a glowing splint, and carbon dioxide turning limewater milky. Use the test safely and record observations before conclusions.

Practise: Calculate an Rf value and describe how chromatography can reveal that an apparently pure sample is a mixture.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A chromatogram spot travels 3.2 cm while solvent front travels 8.0 cm. Rf=3.2/8.0=0.40.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Show model answer and mark allocation

Examiner’s explanation: Rf has no unit and must be between 0 and 1 when measured correctly.

Pencil graphite is insoluble and does not travel with solvent (1); Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front (1); measure both from the baseline (1).

12. Reversible reactions, equilibrium and industrial chemistry

A reversible reaction can proceed in both directions. In a closed system at dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are equal and concentrations remain constant, although reactions continue at particle level.

Changing concentration, pressure (for gaseous equilibria) or temperature can shift the equilibrium position. A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally and helps equilibrium be reached faster, but does not change the equilibrium position.

For an exothermic forward reaction, increasing temperature favours the reverse direction; for an endothermic forward reaction, increasing temperature favours the forward direction. Increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas, where the number of gas particles differs.

Further textbook notes: equilibrium and industrial conditions

Dynamic equilibrium is established in a closed system when forward and reverse reaction rates are equal. Concentrations remain constant, but reactions continue. A change in concentration, pressure or temperature can shift the equilibrium position to oppose the change (Le Chatelier’s principle).

For a gaseous equilibrium, increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas; decreasing pressure favours the side with more gas. Increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction. A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally and helps equilibrium establish faster, but does not change its position or yield at equilibrium.

Industrial conditions balance yield, rate, cost, safety and equipment. The Haber process uses nitrogen and hydrogen to make ammonia; the forward reaction is exothermic and produces fewer gas molecules, so lower temperature and higher pressure favour yield, while a moderate temperature and iron catalyst give a practical rate.

Practise: Predict the effect of changing temperature and pressure on a stated equilibrium and explain why industrial conditions are compromises.
