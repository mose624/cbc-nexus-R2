Pearson Edexcel Chemistry — Topic Notes

Expand a chapter to read its lesson here. These original foundation notes cover common International GCSE Chemistry concepts. Topic depth, options and practical requirements must be checked against your current Pearson specification.

1. Principles of chemistry: particles, atoms and formulae

Matter is made of particles. In a solid, particles are close together and vibrate about fixed positions; in a liquid, particles remain close but move past one another; in a gas, particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly. Changes of state are physical changes: the substance's chemical identity remains the same.

Atomic structure

An atom contains protons and neutrons in a tiny nucleus, with electrons occupying shells or energy levels. A proton has relative charge +1 and relative mass 1; a neutron has charge 0 and relative mass 1; an electron has charge −1 and a very small relative mass. Atomic number is the number of protons. Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Ions form when atoms or groups of atoms gain or lose electrons. Losing electrons forms positive ions; gaining electrons forms negative ions.

Formulae and equations

A chemical formula shows the elements and relative numbers of atoms or ions in a substance. A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides; balance by changing coefficients, never by changing subscripts in formulae.

relative formula mass (Mᵣ) = sum of the relative atomic masses in the formula
Exam tip: Check that ionic compounds have an overall charge of zero and that every atom is balanced in a symbol equation.
2. Bonding and structure

Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, usually formed when electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal. Ionic compounds form giant lattices. They usually have high melting points and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved because ions are mobile, but not when solid because ions are fixed.

Covalent bonding involves a shared pair of electrons between atoms. Simple molecular substances often have low melting and boiling points because intermolecular forces are relatively weak; covalent bonds within molecules are strong. Giant covalent structures, such as diamond and silicon dioxide, have many strong covalent bonds and high melting points. Graphite has layers and delocalised electrons, so it conducts electricity.

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons. Metals conduct electricity and heat; layers of ions can slide, explaining malleability.

3. Formulae, equations and the mole

The mole is the amount of substance containing the Avogadro number of specified particles. Relative formula mass is found by adding the relative atomic masses in a formula.

moles n = mass m ÷ molar mass M
mass = moles × molar mass

Use grams for mass and g mol⁻¹ for molar mass in the common school calculation. The balanced equation gives the mole ratio between reactants and products.

Worked example

Calculate the amount of substance in 10.0 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. Mᵣ = 40.1 + 12.0 + (3 × 16.0) = 100.1. Moles = 10.0 ÷ 100.1 = 0.0999 mol (3 significant figures).

For a limiting-reactant question, calculate the available moles of each reactant, divide by its equation coefficient, and identify the smallest result. That reactant is used up first.

4. Acids, alkalis, salts and pH

An acid produces hydrogen ions in aqueous solution. An alkali is a soluble base that produces hydroxide ions in water. The pH scale indicates acidity or alkalinity: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is alkaline at the stated conditions. Universal indicator gives a range of colours; a pH meter gives a more precise reading.

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
acid + base → salt + water
acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Neutralisation is the reaction of hydrogen ions with hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). A salt is named from the metal or ammonium ion and the acid: hydrochloric acid forms chlorides, sulfuric acid forms sulfates, and nitric acid forms nitrates.

Preparing a soluble salt

For an acid and an insoluble base, warm dilute acid, add excess base until no more reacts, filter off excess solid, then evaporate some water and allow crystals to form. Wash and dry the crystals.

In titration, use a suitable indicator, record initial and final burette readings, repeat to obtain concordant titres, and use the mean of concordant results.
5. Rates of reaction and collision theory

Particles must collide for a reaction to occur. A collision is successful only if particles have sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy and have a suitable orientation. Increasing temperature increases average kinetic energy and the proportion of particles with energy at or above activation energy.

Increasing concentration or gas pressure increases collision frequency. Increasing the surface area of a solid exposes more particles for collisions. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy and is not used up overall.

Rates can be investigated by measuring gas volume produced, mass lost, or time taken for a visible change such as a precipitate to obscure a mark. Keep control variables constant and repeat measurements.

mean rate = change in measured quantity ÷ time taken
6. Energetics: exothermic and endothermic reactions

An exothermic reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, which usually rise in temperature. An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings, which usually cool. Combustion, many neutralisations and oxidation reactions are exothermic; thermal decomposition is commonly endothermic.

Breaking bonds requires energy; making bonds releases energy. Overall energy change depends on the difference between energy absorbed to break bonds and energy released when new bonds form.

energy change = energy absorbed − energy released

Reaction profile diagrams show reactant and product energy levels, activation energy and overall energy change. For an exothermic reaction, products are at lower energy than reactants; for an endothermic reaction, products are at higher energy.

7. Electrolysis and redox

Electrolysis uses direct current to decompose an ionic substance when molten or in aqueous solution. Positive ions (cations) move to the negative cathode and gain electrons (reduction). Negative ions (anions) move to the positive anode and lose electrons (oxidation).

reduction = gain of electrons
oxidation = loss of electrons

In molten ionic compounds, the metal is produced at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode. In aqueous solutions, products depend on the ions present and the relative ease of discharge; learn the specified product rules for your course.

Half-equations show electron transfer and must balance both atoms and charge. For example: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.

8. The periodic table and chemical trends

Elements are arranged in order of increasing proton number. Elements in the same group have similar outer-shell electron arrangements and often similar chemical properties. Across a period, proton number increases and elements change from metals towards non-metals.

Group 1 metals form +1 ions and become more reactive down the group because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. Group 7 halogens form −1 ions; their reactivity decreases down the group because gaining an electron becomes less favourable. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution.

Noble gases have full outer electron shells and are generally unreactive. Transition elements often form coloured compounds, have variable oxidation states and can act as catalysts.

9. Extraction and uses of metals

Metals differ in reactivity. A metal more reactive than carbon is generally extracted from its compound by electrolysis; a less reactive metal may be extracted by reduction with carbon or carbon monoxide. Very unreactive metals can occur native in the Earth's crust.

Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain. In extraction, metal ions are reduced to metal atoms. The reactivity series helps predict displacement reactions and suitable extraction methods.

Alloys are mixtures containing a metal and one or more other elements. Different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers in a pure metal, making it harder for layers to slide; alloys are often harder than the pure metal.

10. Organic chemistry: crude oil, alkanes and alkenes

Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation separates it into fractions according to differences in boiling point. Smaller hydrocarbon molecules generally have lower boiling points, lower viscosity and greater volatility than larger molecules.

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only single carbon–carbon bonds; their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for open-chain molecules. Alkenes contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond and are unsaturated; for a simple open-chain alkene with one double bond the general formula is CₙH₂ₙ.

Alkenes decolourise bromine water from orange/brown to colourless in the standard test. Cracking breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter alkanes and alkenes using heat and a catalyst or high temperature. Complete combustion in excess oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water; incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot).

11. Chemical analysis and practical skills

Pure substances have fixed melting and boiling points at a stated pressure; impurities usually change and broaden melting ranges. Chromatography separates substances because they have different solubilities in the solvent and attractions to the stationary phase.

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front

For paper chromatography, draw the baseline in pencil, place a small concentrated spot, keep the solvent below the baseline, allow the solvent to rise, mark the solvent front promptly and calculate Rf values. Rf values are only comparable under the same conditions.

Common gas tests: hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper. Follow the specified safe procedures and do not directly inhale gases.

12. Reversible reactions, equilibrium and industrial chemistry

A reversible reaction can proceed in both directions. In a closed system at dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are equal and concentrations remain constant, although reactions continue at particle level.

Changing concentration, pressure (for gaseous equilibria) or temperature can shift the equilibrium position. A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally and helps equilibrium be reached faster, but does not change the equilibrium position.

For an exothermic forward reaction, increasing temperature favours the reverse direction; for an endothermic forward reaction, increasing temperature favours the forward direction. Increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas, where the number of gas particles differs.

Chemistry self-check

  1. State the relative charge and location of a proton.
  2. Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but molten sodium chloride does.
  3. Calculate moles in 5.0 g of Mg (Aᵣ = 24.3).
  4. Write the word equation for an acid reacting with a carbonate.
  5. Explain how a catalyst changes reaction rate.
  6. State whether bond breaking is endothermic or exothermic.
  7. Identify the electrode where reduction occurs.
  8. Explain why Group 1 reactivity increases down the group.
  9. Describe the test for an alkene.
  10. State the condition for dynamic equilibrium.

Answers: 1) +1, nucleus. 2) Ions are fixed in a solid but mobile when molten. 3) 5.0 ÷ 24.3 = 0.206 mol. 4) Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. 5) Provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. 6) Endothermic. 7) Cathode. 8) Outer electron is farther away and more shielded. 9) Bromine water changes orange/brown to colourless. 10) Forward and reverse rates are equal in a closed system.
