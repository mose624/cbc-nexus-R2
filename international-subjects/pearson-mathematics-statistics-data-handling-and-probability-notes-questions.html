Chapter 9: Statistics, data handling and probability

Data may be discrete or continuous, and may be collected from a population or a sample. A frequency table organises observations. Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values; median is the middle value when ordered; mode is the most frequent value; range = maximum − minimum.

Core explanation

Choose a suitable diagram: bar charts for categories or discrete data, histograms for continuous grouped data, pie charts for proportions, and scatter graphs for relationships. In a histogram, frequency density = frequency ÷ class width.

Worked example / application

Probability lies between 0 and 1. For equally likely outcomes, probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. The sum of probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes is 1. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Further textbook notes: statistics and probability

Mean uses all values but is affected by extreme values; median is the middle value after ordering; mode is the most frequent value. For grouped data, estimate the mean using class midpoints multiplied by frequencies. Cumulative frequency can estimate median, quartiles and interquartile range. Probability lies between 0 and 1. For mutually exclusive outcomes, probabilities add; for independent events, probabilities multiply.

Practise: Compare two data sets using averages and spread, draw a cumulative frequency graph and solve a two-stage probability problem.

Three additional worked examples — different approaches

Study each method, compare the reasoning and use the final check to verify your answer. These are original teaching examples designed to build flexible problem-solving skills.

Worked example 1: Method 1 — mean from raw data

Question: Find the mean of 4, 7, 8, 9 and 12.

Step-by-step solution: Add the values: 4 + 7 + 8 + 9 + 12 = 40. Divide by 5 values: mean = 8.

Check / exam insight: The mean uses every data value.

Worked example 2: Method 2 — weighted mean from a frequency table

Question: Values 1, 2 and 3 have frequencies 2, 4 and 3. Find the mean.

Step-by-step solution: Multiply each value by its frequency: 1×2 + 2×4 + 3×3 = 19. Total frequency = 2 + 4 + 3 = 9. Mean = 19/9 ≈ 2.11.

Check / exam insight: Do not divide by the number of distinct values; divide by the total frequency.

Worked example 3: Method 3 — probability using a complement

Question: The probability that a bus is late is 0.18. Find the probability that it is not late.

Step-by-step solution: Complementary probabilities sum to 1. Therefore P(not late) = 1 − 0.18 = 0.82.

Check / exam insight: The two events 'late' and 'not late' cover all possibilities.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Data 2, 3, 3, 6, 11 has mean=25/5=5 and median=3. The range is 11−2=9.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A fair die is rolled once. Find the probability of getting a prime number.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Prime outcomes are 2, 3 and 5 (1); probability=3/6=1/2 (1).

Examiner’s explanation: List the favourable outcomes and divide by all equally likely outcomes.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

The values 2, 3, 3, 5, 7 have frequencies 1, 2, 1, 1, 1 respectively. Find the mean and state the mode.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total = 2 + 6 + 5 + 7 = 20 (1); total frequency = 6, mean = 20/6 = 3.33… (1); mode = 3 (1).
