10. Radioactivity and nuclear physics

Atoms contain a small, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random; the activity of a source is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq).

RadiationNaturePenetration / ionisation
Alpha (α)Helium nucleusStrongly ionising; stopped by paper or a few cm of air
Beta (β⁻)Fast electronModerately ionising; reduced by thin aluminium
Gamma (γ)Electromagnetic waveWeakly ionising; highly penetrating, reduced by thick lead or concrete

Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei, or activity, to fall to half its initial value. It is not the time for all nuclei to decay.

remaining activity after n half-lives = initial activity × (½)ⁿ

Worked example

A source has activity 800 Bq and a half-life of 5 days. After 15 days, three half-lives have passed: 800 × (½)³ = 100 Bq.

Background radiation comes from natural and human-made sources. Reduce unnecessary exposure by limiting time near a source, increasing distance and using appropriate shielding.

Further textbook notes: nuclear structure and radiation

Atomic nucleus: Proton number Z identifies the element; nucleon number A is the total number of protons and neutrons. Neutron number = A−Z. Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Nuclear notation places A above and Z below the element symbol.

Radioactive emissions: Alpha particles are helium nuclei and are strongly ionising but weakly penetrating. Beta-minus radiation consists of fast electrons and has moderate ionising and penetrating ability. Gamma radiation is electromagnetic, weakly ionising and highly penetrating. The exact shielding and hazard depend on the source and exposure.

Half-life: Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei or activity to fall to half its initial value. Radioactive decay is random for an individual nucleus but predictable for a large sample. After n half-lives, remaining activity is initial activity × (1/2)ⁿ.

Safety: Reduce exposure time, increase distance and use suitable shielding. Do not handle sources directly. Background radiation must be considered when measuring activity.

Worked example: A source has activity 800 Bq and half-life 6 hours. After 18 hours, three half-lives have passed: 800→400→200→100 Bq.

Check your understanding: Compare alpha, beta and gamma by charge, relative ionisation and penetration, and calculate activity after a stated number of half-lives.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A radioactive sample has activity 640 Bq and half-life 5 days. After 15 days, activity is 640→320→160→80 Bq.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A source has activity 400 Bq and half-life 2 hours. Calculate activity after 6 hours.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Three half-lives pass (1); 400→200→100→50 Bq (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Show the number of half-lives. Do not subtract half-life intervals linearly from activity.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A radioactive source has a count rate of 800 counts/min. Its half-life is 3 hours. Calculate the count rate after 9 hours, ignoring background radiation.

Show answer and mark scheme

9 hours is three half-lives (1); 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 counts/min (2).
