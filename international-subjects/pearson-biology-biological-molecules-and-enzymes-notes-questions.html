Chapter 3: Biological molecules and enzymes

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins are major biological molecules. Carbohydrates provide energy and can form structural materials; lipids are energy stores and components of membranes; proteins form enzymes, antibodies, transport molecules and structural tissues.

Core explanation

Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that lower activation energy. A substrate binds to an enzyme's active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex. The active site is complementary to the substrate; changes in temperature or pH can alter its shape (denaturation).

Worked example / application

Increasing temperature initially increases collision frequency and reaction rate until an optimum is reached. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme structure are disrupted. Extreme pH can also denature an enzyme. Enzyme activity can be investigated by measuring product formed or substrate used per unit time.

Further textbook notes: biological molecules and enzyme action

Carbohydrates are built from sugar units; starch and glycogen are storage polysaccharides, while cellulose forms plant cell walls. Lipids contain glycerol and fatty acids and are useful long-term energy stores and membrane components. Proteins are polymers of amino acids and have structural, transport, defence and catalytic roles.

Enzymes are biological catalysts with active sites complementary to substrates. Substrate binding forms an enzyme–substrate complex. High temperature can denature an enzyme by changing the active-site shape; extreme pH can also disrupt bonds maintaining its structure. Rate depends on substrate concentration until active sites become saturated.

Practise: Explain enzyme specificity and interpret a graph showing enzyme activity against temperature or pH.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: At high temperature an enzyme's active-site shape changes due to denaturation, so the substrate is no longer complementary and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Explain why an enzyme's rate falls sharply above its optimum temperature.

Show model answer and mark allocation

High temperature disrupts bonds maintaining enzyme structure (1); active site changes shape/denatures (1); substrate no longer fits so fewer complexes form (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Distinguish denaturation from simply slowing molecular movement.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

An enzyme works fastest at 37 °C but activity falls sharply at 70 °C. Explain why.

Show answer and mark scheme

At high temperature bonds maintaining the enzyme's shape are disrupted (1); active site changes shape (1); substrate no longer fits, so enzyme is denatured (1).
