Chapter 1: Number, place value and numerical methods

The number system includes integers, terminating and recurring decimals, fractions, rational numbers and irrational numbers. Use place value to compare numbers and round to a stated number of decimal places or significant figures. Estimation is useful for checking whether an answer is reasonable.

Core explanation

The order of operations is brackets, indices, division and multiplication, addition and subtraction. When working with negative numbers, take care with signs: subtracting a negative is equivalent to adding. Standard form writes a number as A × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ A < 10.

Worked example / application

Worked example: 0.000572 = 5.72 × 10⁻⁴. To estimate 49.8 × 0.203, use 50 × 0.2 = 10. The exact result should be close to 10.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Round 0.006847 to 2 significant figures. Write 7,350,000 in standard form. Evaluate −3² and (−3)² and explain the difference.

Further textbook notes: number skills

Use place value to compare, order and round numbers. Convert fractions, decimals and percentages by using equivalent forms. Standard form is A×10ⁿ where 1≤A<10. Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit; decimal places count digits after the decimal point. Estimate first to check whether an answer is sensible, retain accuracy in intermediate steps and round only at the end unless instructed otherwise.

Worked example: 0.00072 = 7.2×10⁻⁴ in standard form. To round 48,763 to 2 significant figures, give 49,000.

Practise: Convert recurring decimals to fractions, calculate with standard form and determine bounds for rounded measurements.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Write 0.00056 in standard form: 5.6×10⁻⁴. The first number must be at least 1 and less than 10.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Round 0.006784 to 2 significant figures.

Show model answer and mark allocation

First two significant digits are 6 and 7; next digit is 8, so round up to 0.0068 (2).

Examiner’s explanation: Leading zeros are not significant; keep the place value.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Evaluate 3.6 × 0.45 and give your answer to 2 significant figures.

Show answer and mark scheme

3.6 × 0.45 = 1.62 (2); to 2 significant figures = 1.6 (1).

Chapter 2: Fractions, ratio, proportion and percentages

A fraction represents part of a whole or a quotient. To add or subtract fractions, use a common denominator. To multiply fractions, multiply numerators and denominators; to divide, multiply by the reciprocal.

Core explanation

A ratio compares quantities in the same units. Simplify by dividing each part by the highest common factor. Direct proportion has the form y = kx; inverse proportion has the form y = k/x. Percentages are fractions out of 100.

Worked example / application

Worked example: share £84 in the ratio 3:4. There are 7 parts, so one part is £12; the shares are £36 and £48. A 15% increase multiplies by 1.15; a 15% decrease multiplies by 0.85.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Increase 240 by 12%. Share 96 in the ratio 5:3. If y is directly proportional to x and y = 18 when x = 6, find y when x = 11.

Further textbook notes: ratio, proportion and percentages

Simplify a ratio by dividing every part by the same common factor. To share an amount in a ratio, add the parts, divide the total by this sum, then multiply by each part. Direct proportion has form y=kx; inverse proportion has form y=k/x. A percentage increase multiplies by 1+r/100; a decrease multiplies by 1−r/100. Repeated percentage change uses a multiplier repeatedly.

Worked example: Share 840 in the ratio 3:4. There are 7 parts, each worth 120, so the shares are 360 and 480.

Practise: Solve a reverse percentage problem and explain why a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the starting value.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Share 720 in ratio 2:3:4. Total parts=9; one part=80; shares are 160, 240 and 320.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A jacket costs 240 after a 20% discount. Find its original price.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Multiplier after discount=0.80 (1); original=240/0.80 (1); original price=300 (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Reverse percentages require division by the multiplier, not adding 20% of the discounted price.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A quantity of 84 is shared in the ratio 3:4. Find the two shares.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total parts = 3 + 4 = 7 (1); one part = 84/7 = 12 (1); shares = 36 and 48 (1).

Chapter 3: Algebraic expressions, equations and inequalities

Algebra uses letters to represent unknown or variable quantities. Collect like terms, expand brackets using the distributive law and factorise by taking out common factors or using identities. Substitution replaces variables with given values.

Core explanation

Solve a linear equation by applying inverse operations to both sides while maintaining equality. Inequalities are solved similarly, but multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. A formula can be rearranged to make a different variable the subject.

Worked example / application

Worked example: 3(2x − 1) = 21 gives 6x − 3 = 21, so 6x = 24 and x = 4. Factorise x² + 5x + 6 as (x + 2)(x + 3).

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Expand 4(2x − 3). Solve 5x + 7 = 32. Solve −2x > 8 and explain why the sign changes.

Further textbook notes: algebraic manipulation

Collect like terms only when variables and powers match. Expand brackets using the distributive law; factorising reverses expansion. Solve a linear equation by performing the same operation on both sides. For inequalities, reverse the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number. For simultaneous equations, use elimination or substitution.

Worked example: Solve 3(2x−1)=15. Expand: 6x−3=15; add 3: 6x=18; divide by 6: x=3.

Practise: Expand and factorise expressions, solve simultaneous equations and represent a linear inequality on a number line.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Solve 4(2x−3)=20: 8x−12=20; 8x=32; x=4.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Solve simultaneously 2x+y=11 and x−y=1.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Add equations: 3x=12 (1); x=4 (1); substitute to get y=3 (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Show elimination or substitution steps so method marks are clear.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Solve 5(2x − 3) = 3x + 20. Show your working.

Show answer and mark scheme

10x − 15 = 3x + 20 (1); 7x = 35 (1); x = 5 (1).

Chapter 4: Sequences, graphs and functions

A sequence is an ordered list following a rule. For an arithmetic sequence, the difference between consecutive terms is constant. The nth term of an arithmetic sequence is a + (n − 1)d, where a is the first term and d is the common difference.

Core explanation

Coordinates locate points using an x-coordinate and y-coordinate. A straight-line graph has equation y = mx + c, where m is gradient and c is the y-intercept. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. Parallel lines have equal gradients; perpendicular gradients multiply to −1 (when both are defined).

Worked example / application

Worked example: 5, 8, 11, 14,... has common difference 3. Its nth term is 3n + 2. For y = 2x − 4, the gradient is 2 and the y-intercept is −4.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Find the nth term of 7, 12, 17, 22,... Find the gradient between (2,3) and (6,11). Draw y = −x + 3.

Further textbook notes: sequences and functions

An arithmetic sequence has a constant first difference; its nth term is a+(n−1)d. A geometric sequence has a constant multiplier. A function maps each allowed input to exactly one output; the domain is the set of allowed inputs and the range is the set of outputs. For a straight-line graph y=mx+c, m is the gradient and c is the vertical intercept. Parallel lines have equal gradients.

Practise: Find an nth-term rule, calculate a gradient from two points and interpret graph intercepts in context.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: For 5, 8, 11, 14, ... the common difference is 3 and nth term is 3n+2.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Find the nth term of 7, 11, 15, 19, ...

Show model answer and mark allocation

Common difference=4 (1); nth term begins 4n (1); first term requires −? 4(1)+3=7, so 4n+3 (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Check the rule against at least the first two terms.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

The sequence is 7, 11, 15, 19, … Find the nth term and the 20th term.

Show answer and mark scheme

Common difference = 4 (1); nth term = 4n + 3 (1); 20th term = 83 (1).

Chapter 5: Coordinate geometry and transformations

The midpoint of a line segment is found by averaging the x-coordinates and the y-coordinates. The distance between two points follows Pythagoras' theorem. A transformation maps every point of a shape to a new position.

Core explanation

Translations move every point by the same vector. Reflections use a mirror line. Rotations require a centre, angle and direction. Enlargements require a centre and scale factor; a negative scale factor places the image on the opposite side of the centre.

Worked example / application

For A(2,1) and B(8,9), midpoint = (5,5) and distance = √(6² + 8²) = 10 units. Describe a rotation fully using centre, angle and direction.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Find the midpoint and distance between (−1,2) and (5,10). Describe the transformation mapping a triangle to its image.

Further textbook notes: coordinate geometry and transformations

The midpoint of A(x₁,y₁) and B(x₂,y₂) is ((x₁+x₂)/2,(y₁+y₂)/2). Distance is √((x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²). Gradient is (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁). A translation moves every point by the same vector; a reflection uses a mirror line; a rotation needs a centre, angle and direction; an enlargement needs a centre and scale factor.

Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional lengths; area scale factor is the square of the length scale factor and volume scale factor is its cube.

Practise: Find a midpoint, gradient and distance, then describe a transformation fully.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: For A(2,3) and B(8,11), midpoint=(5,7); gradient=(11−3)/(8−2)=8/6=4/3.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

Find the distance between (1,2) and (4,6).

Show model answer and mark allocation

Differences are 3 and 4 (1); use Pythagoras/distance formula (1); distance=√(9+16)=5 units (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Square both coordinate differences before adding and taking the square root.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Find the gradient of the line through (2, 5) and (6, 13).

Show answer and mark scheme

Gradient = (13 − 5)/(6 − 2) (1); = 8/4 (1); = 2 (1).

Chapter 6: Geometry, angles and mensuration

Angle facts include angles on a straight line sum to 180°, angles around a point sum to 360°, vertically opposite angles are equal, and angles in a triangle sum to 180°. Parallel lines create corresponding and alternate equal angles and co-interior angles that sum to 180°.

Core explanation

Perimeter measures boundary length; area measures surface coverage; volume measures space occupied. For a prism, volume = cross-sectional area × length. Circle formulae include circumference C = 2πr and area A = πr². Surface area is the total area of the outside faces.

Worked example / application

Worked example: a cylinder of radius 3 cm and height 8 cm has volume π × 3² × 8 = 72π cm³. Keep units consistent and give exact answers in terms of π when requested.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Find the area of a trapezium with parallel sides 8 cm and 14 cm and height 5 cm. Find the circumference of a circle of radius 4 cm.

Further textbook notes: angle facts and mensuration

Angles on a straight line total 180°; around a point total 360°; vertically opposite angles are equal. For parallel lines, corresponding and alternate angles are equal, while co-interior angles sum to 180°. Interior angles of an n-sided polygon sum to (n−2)×180°. Area of a triangle = ½bh; trapezium = ½(a+b)h; circle circumference = 2πr and area = πr². Volume of a prism = cross-sectional area × length.

Practise: Solve a multi-step problem combining parallel lines, polygons and area, stating square or cubic units correctly.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A polygon's interior angle sum is (n−2)×180°. For a hexagon this is 4×180°=720°.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

Find each exterior angle of a regular 12-sided polygon.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Exterior angles total 360° (1); 360/12=30° (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Use the exterior-angle sum, not the interior-angle sum.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Calculate its volume in terms of π.

Show answer and mark scheme

V = πr²h (1); = π × 3² × 10 (1); = 90π cm³ (1).

Chapter 7: Pythagoras and trigonometry

In a right-angled triangle, Pythagoras' theorem states that a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse. Trigonometric ratios relate an acute angle to side lengths: sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite/adjacent.

Core explanation

Choose the ratio using the sides known and required. Ensure the calculator is in degree mode when angles are in degrees. The sine rule and cosine rule apply to non-right-angled triangles where included information is known.

Worked example / application

Worked example: a right triangle has opposite side 6 cm and hypotenuse 10 cm. sin θ = 6/10 = 0.6, so θ ≈ 36.9°. A 3–4–5 triangle is a useful Pythagorean triple.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Find the hypotenuse when the shorter sides are 9 cm and 12 cm. Find θ when opposite = 7 and adjacent = 10.

Further textbook notes: Pythagoras and trigonometry

In a right-angled triangle, the hypotenuse is opposite the right angle. Pythagoras states a²+b²=c². The trigonometric ratios are sinθ=opposite/hypotenuse, cosθ=adjacent/hypotenuse and tanθ=opposite/adjacent. Choose the ratio containing the known and required sides. Check calculator mode is degrees when angles are in degrees.

Worked example: If opposite=6 cm and hypotenuse=10 cm, sinθ=0.6, so θ=sin⁻¹(0.6)≈36.9°.

Practise: Solve an elevation or bearing problem and include a labelled sketch.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: If opposite=5 and adjacent=12, tanθ=5/12; θ=tan⁻¹(5/12)≈22.6°.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A ladder 5 m long reaches 4 m up a wall. Find its distance from the wall.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Use Pythagoras (1); √(5²−4²)=√9=3 m (1).

Examiner’s explanation: The ladder is the hypotenuse; identify the right angle.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A right-angled triangle has opposite side 8 cm and hypotenuse 10 cm. Find the angle opposite the 8 cm side.

Show answer and mark scheme

sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse = 8/10 (1); θ = sin⁻¹(0.8) (1); θ ≈ 53.1° (1).

Chapter 8: Vectors and mathematical modelling

A vector has magnitude and direction. In two dimensions, a vector may be written as a column vector. Add vectors by adding corresponding components; subtract by subtracting components. A position vector describes a point relative to an origin.

Core explanation

A mathematical model represents a real situation using variables, equations, graphs or diagrams. State assumptions, define variables and interpret the result in context. Units and sensible rounding are essential when a mathematical answer describes a measurement.

Worked example / application

If a = (3,−2) and b = (−1,5), then a + b = (2,3). The magnitude of (6,8) is √(36 + 64) = 10.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Calculate 2a − b for a = (2,3), b = (−1,4). Find the magnitude of (5,12).

Further textbook notes: vectors and modelling

A vector has magnitude and direction; a scalar has magnitude only. Column vectors can be added by adding corresponding components. The magnitude of vector (a,b) is √(a²+b²). A position vector describes a point relative to an origin; vector AB is the position vector of B minus that of A. In modelling, define variables, state assumptions and check units and realism.

Worked example: Add vectors (3,−2) and (−1,5): result (2,3), with magnitude √13.

Practise: Find a displacement vector between two points and explain why direction matters.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Add vectors (2,−1) and (−3,4) to obtain (−1,3). Magnitude=√10.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A displacement is 6 m east and 8 m north. Find its magnitude.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Use Pythagoras (1); √(6²+8²)=10 m (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Magnitude is positive; direction is a separate part of a vector answer.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

Given vector a = (3, 2) and b = (−1, 4), find 2a + b.

Show answer and mark scheme

2a = (6, 4) (1); add b component-wise (1); result = (5, 8) (1).

Chapter 9: Statistics, data handling and probability

Data may be discrete or continuous, and may be collected from a population or a sample. A frequency table organises observations. Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values; median is the middle value when ordered; mode is the most frequent value; range = maximum − minimum.

Core explanation

Choose a suitable diagram: bar charts for categories or discrete data, histograms for continuous grouped data, pie charts for proportions, and scatter graphs for relationships. In a histogram, frequency density = frequency ÷ class width.

Worked example / application

Probability lies between 0 and 1. For equally likely outcomes, probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. The sum of probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes is 1. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

Find the mean of 4, 7, 7, 8, 14. A fair six-sided die is rolled: find P(even) and P(number greater than 4).

Further textbook notes: statistics and probability

Mean uses all values but is affected by extreme values; median is the middle value after ordering; mode is the most frequent value. For grouped data, estimate the mean using class midpoints multiplied by frequencies. Cumulative frequency can estimate median, quartiles and interquartile range. Probability lies between 0 and 1. For mutually exclusive outcomes, probabilities add; for independent events, probabilities multiply.

Practise: Compare two data sets using averages and spread, draw a cumulative frequency graph and solve a two-stage probability problem.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: Data 2, 3, 3, 6, 11 has mean=25/5=5 and median=3. The range is 11−2=9.
Exam-style question (2 marks)

A fair die is rolled once. Find the probability of getting a prime number.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Prime outcomes are 2, 3 and 5 (1); probability=3/6=1/2 (1).

Examiner’s explanation: List the favourable outcomes and divide by all equally likely outcomes.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

The values 2, 3, 3, 5, 7 have frequencies 1, 2, 1, 1, 1 respectively. Find the mean and state the mode.

Show answer and mark scheme

Total = 2 + 6 + 5 + 7 = 20 (1); total frequency = 6, mean = 20/6 = 3.33… (1); mode = 3 (1).

Chapter 10: Exam problem-solving and mathematical communication

A complete solution shows a clear method, correct notation and a final answer with appropriate units. Read command words carefully: calculate, solve, show that, prove, estimate and explain require different forms of response.

Core explanation

For multi-step problems, identify known information, select a suitable formula, substitute values, calculate carefully and check the result. Use diagrams for geometry, tables for organised data and graphs for relationships. Give exact values when requested and round only at the end.

Worked example / application

A good checking routine includes estimating the size of the answer, substituting a solution back into an equation, checking units and verifying that a probability is between 0 and 1.

Key learning point

Use precise scientific or mathematical vocabulary, show the reasoning behind each step, and connect the concept to the relevant principle rather than memorising isolated facts.

Chapter self-check

A rectangle has perimeter 34 cm and length 10 cm. Find its width and area. Show each step and include units.

Further textbook notes: mathematical communication

Read command words carefully. “Show that” requires a clear chain of reasoning; “prove” requires a logically valid argument; “estimate” expects an approximate value; “solve” requires all relevant solutions. Define variables and show substitutions so method marks can be awarded. Draw a diagram for multi-step problems, use consistent units and check the result in context. Retain exact values such as π or surds during working when appropriate, then round the final answer as instructed.

Practise: Review a solution containing an incorrect unit conversion and early rounding error. Identify both mistakes and rewrite the solution clearly.

Worked example, exam-style question and marking guidance

Worked example: A strong solution defines variables, selects a valid method, shows substitutions, uses units and checks whether the result is reasonable.
Exam-style question (3 marks)

A learner calculates 3.6 km ÷ 2 h and gives 1.8 m/s. Identify the error and give the correct speed in m/s.

Show model answer and mark allocation

Convert 3.6 km to 3600 m (1); convert 2 h to 7200 s (1); speed=3600/7200=0.5 m/s (1).

Examiner’s explanation: Both quantities must be converted to compatible SI units before division.

Exam technique: Show each formula, substitution, calculation and unit. For “explain” questions, connect the scientific idea to the outcome; for “describe” questions, give the relevant sequence or features.

Extra chapter practice question (3 marks)

A student writes that 0.4 × 0.3 = 1.2. Explain the error and give the correct answer.

Show answer and mark scheme

Multiplying decimals gives 12 hundredths, not 12 tenths (1); 4 × 3 = 12 and there are two decimal places (1); answer = 0.12 (1).
