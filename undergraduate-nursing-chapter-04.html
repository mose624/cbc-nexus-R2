Chapter overview and learning outcomes

This chapter develops a structured understanding of fundamentals of nursing skills and patient safety. Learners should connect scientific knowledge with person-centred nursing, communicate clearly, use accurate terminology and recognise the limits of simplified models. Clinical examples are educational; patient assessment and care require qualified supervision, institutional policy and current local guidance.

  • Define and use the chapter's core terminology accurately.
  • Explain relevant structures, mechanisms, relationships and regulatory processes.
  • Integrate knowledge across biological, behavioural and nursing sciences.
  • Apply concepts to safe, respectful and evidence-informed nursing learning.
  • Recognise when to seek supervision and escalate a concern.

1. Foundations of safe nursing care

Safe care begins with respect, identification, assessment, communication, infection prevention and recognition of risk. Before any intervention, confirm the person's identity using the approved identifiers, explain the proposed activity, obtain appropriate consent, check relevant precautions and ensure equipment is suitable. Never rely on bed or room number as an identifier.

Use a structured approach: prepare, introduce yourself, identify the patient, explain, obtain consent, perform hand hygiene, provide privacy, complete the task within competence, reassess comfort and response, dispose of materials safely and document accurately. Stop and seek help if the situation differs from what was expected or the task is outside your training.

2. Hand hygiene and standard precautions

Hand hygiene is a central measure for preventing transmission of microorganisms. Use an alcohol-based hand rub when hands are not visibly soiled and the product is appropriate; use soap and water when hands are visibly dirty and in other situations specified by local infection-prevention policy. Follow the recognised hand-hygiene moments used by the institution, including before patient contact, before aseptic tasks, after body-fluid exposure risk, after patient contact and after contact with patient surroundings.

Standard precautions apply to every patient and include hand hygiene, risk-based personal protective equipment, respiratory hygiene, safe injection practice, sharps safety, environmental cleaning, equipment reprocessing and appropriate handling of linen and waste. Additional transmission-based precautions may be required for specific suspected or confirmed infections.

3. Personal protective equipment and aseptic principles

Select gloves, gowns, masks, respirators and eye protection according to anticipated exposure and the organism or procedure involved. PPE does not replace hand hygiene. Put on and remove PPE in the sequence taught by the facility, avoiding contact between contaminated surfaces and skin or clothing. Respirator fit testing and use follow local occupational-safety requirements.

Aseptic technique aims to prevent contamination of key sites and equipment. Distinguish clean technique from sterile technique and use the level required by the procedure and policy. Maintain the integrity of sterile fields, check packaging and expiry information, avoid reaching over sterile areas and replace any item whose sterility is uncertain.

4. Measuring and interpreting vital signs

Common observations include temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, pain and level of consciousness. Record the method, time, position, oxygen support where relevant and any factors affecting measurement. Use correctly sized and maintained equipment. Compare with the person's baseline and assess trends rather than relying on a single number.

Pulse assessment may include rate, rhythm, volume and symmetry. Respiratory assessment includes rate, depth, effort, pattern and associated symptoms; count without unnecessarily altering the person's breathing. Blood pressure requires suitable cuff size and positioning. Pulse oximetry estimates peripheral oxygen saturation and can be affected by perfusion, movement, nail products, skin pigmentation, device quality and other factors. Escalate abnormal observations according to local early-warning systems.

5. Pain assessment and comfort

Pain is a personal sensory and emotional experience. Ask about location, onset, character, radiation, severity, timing, aggravating and relieving factors and impact on function. Use an appropriate validated scale, including behavioural tools where self-report is not possible. Reassess after interventions and document the person's response.

Comfort measures may include positioning, reassurance, relaxation, environmental adjustments, heat or cold only when appropriate and prescribed or authorised analgesia. Pain assessment must not be dismissed because behaviour or vital signs appear normal. New severe pain, chest pain, sudden neurological symptoms or other red flags require prompt assessment and escalation.

6. Hygiene, skin care and pressure injury prevention

Personal hygiene supports comfort, dignity, skin integrity and infection prevention. Assess ability, preferences, cultural needs, mobility, pain, fatigue and risks before assisting with bathing, oral care, grooming or toileting. Encourage independence where safe and protect privacy. Inspect skin during care and report new breakdown, non-blanching changes, wounds or signs of infection.

Pressure injury risk increases with immobility, impaired sensation, poor perfusion, moisture, friction, shear and inadequate nutrition. Prevention is individualised and may include repositioning, mobilisation, pressure-redistributing surfaces, heel offloading, moisture management and nutrition support. Do not massage persistently reddened bony prominences. Follow local risk-assessment and wound-care protocols.

7. Mobility, positioning and falls prevention

Mobility supports respiratory function, circulation, muscle strength, bowel function, independence and psychological wellbeing. Assess baseline mobility, strength, balance, cognition, pain, footwear, equipment and assistance required. Use safe moving-and-handling techniques, mechanical aids and adequate staff assistance according to training and policy. Do not manually lift beyond safe limits.

Falls prevention includes identifying individual risk factors, orienting the patient, keeping the call bell and needed items accessible, maintaining clear pathways, ensuring suitable footwear and using prescribed mobility aids. Avoid restraints as a routine substitute for observation or safe care. After a fall, follow emergency assessment, observation, documentation and reporting procedures.

8. Nutrition, hydration and elimination

Nutrition assessment considers intake, weight trends, swallowing, appetite, oral health, cultural preferences, food access and conditions affecting absorption or metabolism. Hydration assessment considers intake, output, losses, clinical signs, medicines and prescribed fluid restrictions. Support safe positioning and swallowing precautions where indicated; refer concerns to appropriate clinicians.

Monitor bowel and urinary patterns respectfully. Document relevant intake and output accurately when indicated. Constipation, diarrhoea, urinary retention, incontinence and dehydration have multiple causes. Protect privacy, maintain skin care and report acute changes, blood, severe pain, inability to pass urine or other warning signs.

9. Medication safety and calculations

Medication administration is governed by law, professional scope, institutional policy and prescriber orders. Common safety checks include the right patient, medicine, dose, route, time, documentation, indication and response, with additional checks for allergies, interactions, expiry, formulation and required monitoring. Clarify incomplete, illegible, unusual or potentially unsafe orders before proceeding.

Calculations require correct units and independent checking where policy requires it. Never guess a dose or use an unfamiliar preparation without qualified guidance. High-alert medicines and paediatric calculations require additional safeguards. Students must not administer medicines independently unless authorised within their supervised training arrangement.

10. Documentation, handover and escalation

Clinical records should be accurate, timely, factual, legible, attributable and completed in the approved system. Record assessment findings, care provided, patient response, education, communication, referrals and escalation. Correct errors according to policy; never erase, backdate or falsify records. Protect passwords and log out of shared devices.

Structured handover tools such as SBAR organise communication into Situation, Background, Assessment and Recommendation. Include current concerns, relevant history, observations, actions taken and what needs follow-up. Escalate deterioration promptly using local pathways; documentation does not replace direct communication when urgent action is needed.

Integrated case-based learning

Case: A student nurse is assigned to observe an adult patient during a supervised learning placement. The patient has a change from their usual condition and expresses concern about the proposed care. The student must gather relevant information, listen respectfully, check identity and consent, consider immediate safety, communicate findings to the supervising nurse and document objectively.

Discussion: Identify the information that is subjective and objective. Which findings require prompt escalation? What additional context would help interpretation? How can the student preserve dignity and confidentiality? Which actions are within the student's authorised competence, and which require direct supervision? Explain how the chapter's concepts support safe decisions without attempting to diagnose or prescribe independently.

Learning point: Sound nursing practice combines scientific knowledge, careful observation, communication, ethical conduct, teamwork and timely escalation. A textbook scenario is not a substitute for local protocols or direct clinical supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For longer questions, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and relate the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define nursing and identify four major purposes of nursing practice.

    Show answer guidance

    Nursing is a professional discipline that promotes health, prevents illness, supports recovery, relieves suffering and protects dignity. Examples include health promotion, prevention, care coordination and rehabilitation.

  2. Outline the five stages of the nursing process.

    Show answer guidance

    Assessment; analysis or nursing diagnosis; planning; implementation; evaluation. The process is iterative and should be revised when new information emerges.

  3. Differentiate autonomy and beneficence.

    Show answer guidance

    Autonomy concerns respect for a person's informed choices; beneficence concerns acting to promote wellbeing. Ethical decisions may require balancing these and other principles.

  4. State three features of effective therapeutic communication.

    Show answer guidance

    Active listening, open questions, clarification, empathy, summarising and checking understanding are examples.

  5. Explain why accurate documentation is important.

    Show answer guidance

    It supports continuity, communication, accountability, evaluation, legal record-keeping and patient safety.

  6. Differentiate anatomy from physiology.

    Show answer guidance

    Anatomy studies body structures and their relationships; physiology studies how those structures function.

  7. Name the four primary tissue classes.

    Show answer guidance

    Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

  8. Describe the role of the plasma membrane.

    Show answer guidance

    It forms a selectively permeable boundary, regulates transport, supports signalling and contributes to cell recognition.

  9. State two functions of the integumentary system.

    Show answer guidance

    Barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, limiting water loss and vitamin D synthesis.

  10. Explain the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems.

    Show answer guidance

    The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS.

  11. Trace the normal flow of blood through the heart.

    Show answer guidance

    Venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  12. What is cardiac output?

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute; it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.

  13. State the main function of alveoli.

    Show answer guidance

    Alveoli provide a large, thin surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

  14. Name the three basic processes of urine formation.

    Show answer guidance

    Glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion.

  15. List the links in the chain of infection.

    Show answer guidance

    Infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host.

Further study and evidence

Use current nursing textbooks, anatomy and physiology references, peer-reviewed literature, national nursing regulator guidance, institutional skills manuals and current infection-prevention or patient-safety policies. Check publication dates and jurisdiction. Clinical recommendations, drug information, immunisation schedules and professional rules can change; verify them against authoritative local sources before use.
