Research and Dissertation Resources

Comprehensive academic guidance for diploma, undergraduate, master's and doctoral learners. Learn how to formulate research problems, review scholarship, select methods, collect and analyse evidence, write a dissertation and defend your work.

1. Foundations of academic research

Research is a systematic, transparent and ethical process of developing or testing knowledge through questions, evidence, analysis and reasoned interpretation. It is not simply an internet search, a collection of quotations or a statement of personal opinion. A credible investigation explains why the problem matters, where evidence came from, how it was selected, how it was analysed, what conclusions are justified and what remains uncertain.

1.1 Purposes and forms of research

Basic research advances conceptual understanding. Applied research addresses a practical problem. Exploratory research investigates an issue that is not yet well understood. Descriptive research documents characteristics or patterns. Explanatory research examines relationships and possible mechanisms. Evaluative research judges a programme or policy against stated criteria. Action research uses systematic inquiry to improve a local practice. These categories may overlap; the researcher should explain the main purpose rather than attach labels without justification.

1.2 Topic, problem, aim, objectives and questions

A topic identifies a broad area. A research problem identifies a specific uncertainty, contradiction, unmet need or practical difficulty. The aim states the overall purpose. Objectives divide the aim into achievable tasks. Research questions state what the study must answer. Hypotheses express testable expectations where appropriate. A title communicates the focus concisely.

Illustrative example: Broad topic: student achievement. More focused problem: a department uses digital feedback but has limited evidence about how students interpret comments and use them to revise later assignments. Aim: examine how undergraduate students interpret and use digital feedback in a defined programme. Objectives: identify feedback formats, explore understanding of comments, examine revision practices and identify perceived barriers.

1.3 Writing a defensible problem statement

A problem statement should move from context to evidence, consequence, knowledge gap and study response. Use credible statistics, policy documents and peer-reviewed research where appropriate. Identify the population, location, period and definition behind important claims. A local observation may justify an institutional study, but it should not be presented as proof of a national or global trend.

  1. Context: what is happening in the field or setting?
  2. Evidence: what reliable sources demonstrate the issue?
  3. Consequence: why does the issue matter?
  4. Existing knowledge: what have previous studies established?
  5. Gap: what is missing, contested or poorly understood?
  6. Response: what will this study investigate?

1.4 Variables, constructs and hypotheses

A construct is an abstract concept such as motivation, resilience or institutional quality. A variable is a characteristic that can take different values. An operational definition explains exactly how a construct will be observed or measured. Academic performance might mean a final examination score, grade point average, course completion or a standardised test; these measures are not interchangeable.

An independent variable is a predictor or explanatory factor in a particular model; a dependent variable is the outcome. A confounder may be associated with both and distort an observed relationship. A mediator may help explain a pathway; a moderator may change the strength or direction of a relationship. A hypothesis is a testable proposition about an expected relationship or difference. Not every qualitative or exploratory study needs a formal hypothesis.

1.5 Research philosophy and reasoning

Ontology concerns assumptions about reality; epistemology concerns how knowledge can be justified; axiology considers the role of values. Positivist approaches commonly emphasise measurement and testing propositions. Interpretivist approaches explore meaning and experience in context. Critical approaches examine power and inequality. Pragmatism selects methods that best address the research problem while recognising that different forms of evidence may be useful. Deductive reasoning tests expectations derived from theory; inductive reasoning develops ideas from patterns in evidence; abductive reasoning moves between observations and plausible explanations.

1.6 Quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods

Quantitative research analyses numerical evidence to describe patterns, estimate relationships or test hypotheses. Qualitative research examines meanings, experiences, processes and context through interviews, observations, texts or other non-numerical evidence. Mixed methods deliberately integrates both strands. It is not simply collecting a survey and a few interviews without explaining how their results connect.

1.7 Academic expectations by level

A diploma project usually demonstrates a focused applied problem, basic academic reading, a suitable method and a coherent report. An undergraduate dissertation expects greater independence, critical reading, justified design and appropriate analysis. A master's dissertation normally requires deeper synthesis, explicit theoretical or conceptual reasoning and a defensible contribution to professional or scholarly understanding. A doctoral thesis usually requires a substantial original contribution, advanced command of the literature, sophisticated methods and the ability to defend the work. Originality may involve new evidence, interpretation, method, application or theory. Institutional requirements differ, so the current handbook and supervisor guidance take precedence.

2. Literature searching and critical literature reviews

A literature review is a structured and critical account of knowledge relevant to a research problem. It explains what is known, how knowledge was produced, where scholars agree or disagree, what evidence is weak or missing and how the proposed study may contribute. It is both a process of searching and evaluating sources and the written synthesis of that work.

2.1 Search strategy

Start with the research question and identify its central concepts. Generate synonyms, spelling variants and related terms. Combine concepts using Boolean operators: AND narrows a search, OR includes synonyms and NOT excludes a term but should be used cautiously. Quotation marks may search an exact phrase; truncation may retrieve word variants depending on the database. Keep a search log recording database, date, exact search string, filters and screening decisions.

Choose databases suitable for the discipline. Examples include ERIC for education, PubMed for biomedical and life sciences, EconLit for economics, PsycINFO for psychology and IEEE Xplore for computing and engineering. University catalogues, government repositories, professional bodies and specialist research institutes may also be relevant. Google Scholar is a discovery tool, not a guarantee of quality. Check the original publication.

2.2 Evaluating academic sources

  • Authority: who wrote the work and what relevant expertise do they have?
  • Publication quality: is the journal or publisher credible and transparent about review?
  • Currency: is the date appropriate for the topic?
  • Relevance: does the population, context, concept or outcome match the question?
  • Methodological quality: are the design, sample, measures and analysis explained?
  • Evidence: are claims supported by traceable data and references?
  • Bias: are funding, conflicts and alternative explanations considered?
  • Transferability: what contextual differences limit application elsewhere?

Peer review is valuable but does not make a paper flawless. Government reports may provide official statistics but may have a different purpose from empirical studies. Preprints may provide emerging evidence but have not necessarily completed peer review. Match each source to the claim it can support.

2.3 Critical synthesis rather than author-by-author summary

A descriptive review says what each author reported. A critical review compares the question, theory, design, evidence, limitations and contribution of different sources. Organise the review thematically, chronologically, methodologically or theoretically according to the research question. A strong paragraph states an analytical point, presents evidence from several sources, compares findings, evaluates quality and explains the implication for the current study.

2.4 Research gaps

Gaps may be evidential, population-based, contextual, methodological, theoretical, measurement-related, implementation-related, temporal or arise from contradictory findings. A gap must be demonstrated through the search and appraisal process. Avoid claiming that no research exists after a superficial search. State what the review covered, what remains uncertain and why that uncertainty matters.

2.5 Theoretical and conceptual frameworks

A theoretical framework applies an established theory to explain a phenomenon and guide inquiry. A conceptual framework is a study-specific model of concepts and proposed relationships. Each concept should be defined and each arrow or relationship justified by theory or evidence. A diagram without an explanatory narrative is not a framework. In exploratory research, a framework may guide questions without forcing all evidence into predetermined categories.

2.6 Referencing and AI-assisted research

Follow the required institutional style, such as APA, Harvard, Chicago, Vancouver or IEEE. Record complete bibliographic details while reading. Reference managers can organise sources but their output must be checked. Paraphrasing requires understanding the source, writing in your own structure and citing it; changing a few words is not sufficient.

AI tools may suggest search terms, organise notes or explain concepts, but they can fabricate authors, DOI numbers, quotations and findings. Verify every generated citation against the publisher, DOI record, repository or library catalogue. Do not upload confidential participant data or restricted documents to unapproved systems. Follow institutional rules and disclose substantive AI assistance where required.

3. Research design, sampling and methodology

Research design is the overall plan connecting a research question to the evidence needed to answer it. The methodology explains the reasoning behind the selected design and methods. Methods are the specific techniques used to collect and analyse evidence. The design is appropriate when it answers the question with credible evidence and remains feasible and ethical.

3.1 Common designs

Cross-sectional studies collect evidence at one point or within a defined short period. Longitudinal studies collect data over time. Experiments assign an intervention or condition and compare outcomes; random allocation can reduce confounding when properly implemented. Quasi-experiments evaluate interventions without full random assignment and depend on explicit assumptions. Case studies examine a bounded case in context. Phenomenology explores lived experience; ethnography examines practices and meaning in a social setting; grounded theory develops explanations through iterative data collection and analysis; action research investigates and improves practice.

3.2 Sampling

The target population is the broader group to which the question refers. The accessible population is the part that can realistically be reached. A sampling frame is the list or mechanism from which units are selected. The sample is the subset included. Probability sampling gives eligible units known selection probabilities: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster and multistage sampling are examples. Non-probability sampling includes convenience, purposive, quota and snowball approaches. These may be appropriate for exploratory or qualitative work, but do not automatically support population-wide estimates.

3.3 Sample size

Sample size depends on the research question, design, outcome variability, desired precision, expected effect, statistical power, population structure, nonresponse and resources. A sample-size calculation must state its assumptions. A larger sample does not repair a biased sampling frame or invalid measurement. Qualitative sample adequacy depends on the study purpose, specificity of the sample, depth and quality of data and analytic approach; do not use a universal interview count without justification.

3.4 Validity, reliability and trustworthiness

Validity concerns whether the evidence supports the intended interpretation. Content validity asks whether a measure covers the construct; construct validity asks whether it behaves as theory predicts; criterion validity examines relationships with an external measure. Reliability concerns consistency under specified conditions, such as test-retest stability or inter-rater agreement. In qualitative work, credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability, reflexivity and a transparent audit trail are important quality considerations.

3.5 Methodology chapter

A methodology chapter commonly explains the research approach, design, setting, period, population, eligibility, sampling, sample-size rationale, instruments, pilot testing, data collection, analysis, quality assurance, ethics and limitations. Explain why each choice fits the question. A reader should be able to judge whether the study is sound and, where relevant, repeat its procedures.

4. Data collection, analysis and interpretation

4.1 Data collection instruments

Questionnaires should contain clear, neutral questions linked to objectives. Avoid leading, double-barrelled or ambiguous wording. Interviews use open questions and neutral probes. Focus groups examine group interaction but may be unsuitable for highly sensitive topics or settings with strong power differences. Observation schedules define what will be recorded. Document analysis examines provenance, authorship, purpose, audience, context and omissions. Experiments require defined apparatus, materials, conditions, controls, calibration and replication.

4.2 Quantitative analysis

Preserve raw data and document every cleaning step. Check duplicates, ranges, units, missing values and inconsistent coding. Descriptive statistics include frequencies, percentages, means, medians, standard deviations and interquartile ranges. Inferential methods estimate relationships or differences under stated assumptions. Select tests according to outcome type, design, distribution, independence and sampling structure. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals where appropriate; a p-value is not the probability that a hypothesis is true. Statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.

Worked descriptive example: In a hypothetical sample of 120 learners, 72 report at least one laboratory session in the previous month. The sample proportion is 72 ÷ 120 = 0.60, or 60%. This describes the sample. It does not automatically establish that 60% of all learners in a country have that experience; population inference depends on sampling, coverage, nonresponse and uncertainty.

4.3 Qualitative analysis

Organise transcripts, field notes and documents securely. Coding labels relevant segments of evidence. A codebook can define each code and give inclusion rules. Thematic analysis commonly involves familiarisation, initial coding, developing candidate themes, reviewing themes against the dataset, defining and naming themes and writing an evidence-based account. The process is iterative. A theme should express a patterned meaning relevant to the research question, not merely repeat an interview topic. Attend to divergent cases and support interpretations with carefully selected excerpts.

4.4 Mixed-methods integration

Integration may occur at design, collection, analysis or interpretation. A joint display can compare numerical results with qualitative themes. Evidence may converge, complement or diverge. If a survey indicates that most participants can access a platform while interviews reveal unreliable access at peak times, examine whether the survey measured occasional access while interviews explored frequency and quality. Explain what integration adds to the answer.

4.5 Findings, discussion and limitations

Present findings in an order linked to objectives and questions. Tables and figures should have clear titles, units, denominators and notes. The discussion interprets results in relation to literature, theory, context and alternative explanations. A limitation should state the issue, why it arose, which inference it affects and what readers should avoid concluding. Recommendations should follow from the evidence and identify a realistic audience and action.

5. Research ethics, integrity and data management

Research ethics protects participants, communities, researchers and the integrity of knowledge. Core principles include respect for persons, voluntary participation, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, privacy, confidentiality and transparency. Obtain the required institutional review and permissions before recruitment or data collection. A supervisor's informal approval does not replace formal ethics approval where it is required.

5.1 Informed consent and participant protection

Participants should receive understandable information about purpose, procedures, duration, foreseeable risks and benefits, data use, confidentiality limits, contacts and withdrawal arrangements. Consent is an ongoing process. Refusal must not affect grades, employment, services or access to support. When research involves minors or people who may lack capacity, follow the applicable law and ethics committee requirements for permission and assent.

5.2 Privacy and data security

Collect only necessary information. Replace names with study codes, store identity keys separately, restrict access and use approved secure storage. Explain retention and deletion periods. Small communities can create deductive disclosure risks even when names are removed. Cloud services and cross-border transfers require attention to institutional policy and applicable data protection law. For Kenya-based projects, examine the Data Protection Act and guidance from the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner as well as institutional requirements. This resource is educational guidance, not legal advice.

5.3 Research integrity

Fabrication means inventing data or results. Falsification means manipulating materials, processes or data so that the record is misleading. Plagiarism means presenting another person's words, ideas or work without appropriate acknowledgement. Other concerns include selective reporting, deceptive image manipulation, duplicate publication, undisclosed conflicts and inappropriate authorship. Keep raw data, analysis files and decision logs. Report protocol deviations transparently and do not hide inconvenient results.

5.4 Data management and reproducibility

A data management plan explains what data will be created or reused, how it will be documented, stored, secured, backed up, shared where permitted and retained or destroyed. Use a data dictionary, meaningful file names, version control and documented transformations. Preserve raw data separately from cleaned data. Reproducible workflows record software versions, scripts, package dependencies, exclusion rules and model specifications. Where data cannot be shared for privacy or legal reasons, explain the restriction and consider whether code or metadata can safely be shared.

6. Dissertation and thesis writing

6.1 Common dissertation structure

  1. Front matter: title page, declarations, abstract, acknowledgements where permitted, contents and lists of tables or figures.
  2. Chapter 1 — Introduction: context, problem, aim, objectives, questions, justification, scope and definitions.
  3. Chapter 2 — Literature review: critical synthesis, theory, conceptual framework and research gap.
  4. Chapter 3 — Methodology: design, setting, population, sampling, instruments, procedures, analysis, quality and ethics.
  5. Chapter 4 — Findings or results: evidence organised by objective or question.
  6. Chapter 5 — Discussion and conclusion: interpretation, contribution, limitations and recommendations.
  7. Back matter: references and appendices, including instruments where appropriate.

This is a common model, not a universal rule. Some disciplines combine findings and discussion, use article-based theses or organise chapters around themes, experiments or archival evidence. The current institutional handbook and supervisor's direction take precedence.

6.2 Proposal writing

A proposal is a reasoned plan, not a completed dissertation written in future tense. It normally includes a focused title, background, problem statement, aim, objectives, questions or hypotheses, justification, scope, literature review, framework, methodology, ethics, data management, limitations, timeline, budget, references and appendices. Each section should support the same research question.

6.3 Academic writing and referencing

Academic writing should be precise, coherent and evidence-based. Each paragraph should have a clear function. Define technical terms, maintain consistent terminology and distinguish evidence from interpretation. Use the citation style required by the programme, such as APA, Harvard, Chicago, Vancouver or IEEE. Check every in-text citation against the reference list and verify author names, dates, titles, page ranges and DOI links. A citation is not a substitute for analysis.

6.4 Responsible use of AI

AI tools may support brainstorming, language editing, alternative search terms or explanations of statistical concepts where institutional rules permit. They may fabricate references, misstate results, introduce bias or generate text that the student cannot defend. Verify every claim and reference against the original source. Do not enter confidential or identifiable research data into unapproved tools. Disclose assistance where required. AI detectors are not definitive proof of authorship or misconduct; follow a fair institutional process.

6.5 Oral defence and dissemination

A proposal presentation should explain the problem, gap, objectives, literature, framework, design, sampling, instruments, analysis, ethics, feasibility and contribution. A defence tests whether the researcher understands and can justify the work. Prepare to explain why the topic matters, how the gap was established, why the method fits, how participants were protected, what the findings mean, what the limitations are and what the study contributes. If you do not know an answer, acknowledge the limit and explain how you would investigate it.

Research may be shared through a thesis repository, journal article, conference paper, policy brief, technical report or community presentation. Check the credibility, scope, peer-review process, fees, copyright and data policies of a journal or publisher. Be alert to predatory outlets that promise rapid acceptance without credible review. Publication does not automatically create policy impact; explain how the evidence may inform practice and what further engagement is needed.

6.6 Dissertation quality checklist

  • The title accurately reflects the study.
  • The problem is supported by verified evidence.
  • Aim, objectives, questions and methods align.
  • The literature review synthesises and critiques sources.
  • The research gap is demonstrated rather than merely asserted.
  • The framework is explained and used.
  • Sampling, measurement and analysis are justified.
  • Ethics approval and consent are documented where required.
  • Data are accurately recorded, secured and analysed.
  • Findings include unexpected and null results where relevant.
  • Discussion connects findings to literature and theory.
  • Limitations are specific and recommendations are proportionate.
  • References are verified and formatting follows the handbook.
  • AI use complies with institutional policy.

7. Revision questions and applied assignments

  1. Differentiate a research problem, aim, objective and research question.
  2. Explain the difference between research methods and methodology.
  3. Compare positivist, interpretivist and pragmatic approaches.
  4. Discuss the strengths and limitations of quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods research.
  5. Develop a search strategy for a dissertation topic using Boolean operators.
  6. Explain why a literature review must synthesise rather than list sources.
  7. Identify and justify five types of research gap.
  8. Distinguish a theoretical framework from a conceptual framework.
  9. Compare probability and non-probability sampling.
  10. Explain how sample size and sampling design affect inference.
  11. Discuss validity and reliability in quantitative research.
  12. Explain credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability in qualitative research.
  13. Design a questionnaire and explain how it will be piloted.
  14. Discuss informed consent, confidentiality and data minimisation.
  15. Explain the difference between fabrication, falsification and plagiarism.
  16. Compare descriptive and inferential statistics.
  17. Explain why statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.
  18. Describe the main stages of thematic analysis.
  19. Explain how mixed-methods findings can be integrated.
  20. Outline the common chapters of a dissertation and their purposes.
  21. Discuss how limitations should shape conclusions and recommendations.
  22. Explain how AI-generated references and claims should be verified.
  23. Prepare five questions that may be asked during an oral defence.

Capstone assignment

Prepare a proposal in your own discipline. Include a focused title, background and problem statement, aim, three to five objectives, aligned research questions, a critical literature map, a theoretical or conceptual framework, a justified design, sampling or source-selection plan, draft instrument, analysis plan, ethics and data-management plan, realistic timeline, budget and verified references.
