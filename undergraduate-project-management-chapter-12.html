1. Purpose of monitoring and control

Monitoring collects and interprets information about project progress, while control compares performance with approved expectations and initiates appropriate action. The purpose is not to produce reports for their own sake; it is to identify deviations early, understand causes and protect intended outcomes. Effective control connects scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, procurement and benefits. A project may be on schedule but failing quality tests, or within budget while delivering less scope. Therefore, managers need an integrated view rather than isolated indicators.

2. Establishing baselines and performance measures

A baseline is an approved reference against which performance is assessed. Common baselines include scope, schedule and cost, supported by quality and benefits measures. Each indicator needs a definition, source, owner, frequency, threshold and interpretation rule. Baselines should be sufficiently detailed to support management but not so elaborate that maintaining them consumes delivery capacity. Record approved changes and preserve historical versions. Without controlled baselines, teams can reset targets after poor performance and make a troubled project appear successful.

3. Key performance indicators and critical success factors

A key performance indicator (KPI) measures an aspect of performance that matters to an objective. Critical success factors are conditions that must be present for success, such as effective sponsorship, user participation or reliable data. KPIs should be relevant, measurable, timely and actionable. Distinguish output measures, such as facilities completed, from outcome measures, such as improved access or service reliability. A balanced scorecard can combine financial, customer, internal process and learning perspectives. Avoid selecting indicators solely because data are easy to collect.

4. Schedule monitoring and milestone control

Schedule control compares actual progress and forecast dates with the approved schedule. Milestones represent significant events or decisions and should have clear completion evidence. Track actual start and finish dates, remaining duration, dependencies, critical and near-critical activities, float and resource constraints. A milestone marked complete without acceptance evidence is unreliable. When a date slips, determine whether the cause is local or affects downstream work. Recovery options include resequencing, resource changes, scope adjustment or schedule negotiation, each with risks and approval requirements.

5. Cost monitoring and forecasting

Cost control compares actual expenditure and commitments with the time-phased budget and forecast. Reports should distinguish incurred costs, outstanding commitments, accruals, approved changes and remaining forecast. Explain variances by cause and identify the expected final cost. A favourable variance may indicate delayed invoices rather than genuine savings. Forecasting should be updated using current productivity, prices, scope and risk information. Escalate early when the estimate at completion exceeds approved tolerance; late reporting reduces the options available to the sponsor.

6. Earned value management

Earned Value Management integrates scope, schedule and cost using Planned Value (PV), Earned Value (EV) and Actual Cost (AC). Schedule Variance is EV minus PV; Cost Variance is EV minus AC. Schedule Performance Index is EV divided by PV; Cost Performance Index is EV divided by AC. An index below 1 generally signals performance below the baseline on that dimension. These calculations depend on reliable progress measurement and a properly maintained performance measurement baseline. They are diagnostic signals, not substitutes for explaining causes or assessing future risks.

7. Worked earned value calculation

Assume a project has a Budget at Completion (BAC) of KSh 10,000,000. At the status date, PV is KSh 5,000,000, EV is KSh 4,000,000 and AC is KSh 5,000,000. Cost Variance = EV − AC = −KSh 1,000,000. Schedule Variance = EV − PV = −KSh 1,000,000. CPI = EV ÷ AC = 0.80 and SPI = EV ÷ PV = 0.80. If current cost efficiency continues, a simple EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = KSh 12,500,000. Explain that this forecast assumes future work performs at the current CPI.

8. Estimate at completion and variance at completion

Estimate at Completion (EAC) forecasts total project cost. Estimate to Complete (ETC) is the expected additional cost. Variance at Completion (VAC) is BAC minus EAC. A common formula, EAC = BAC/CPI, assumes current cost efficiency continues. Another, EAC = AC + (BAC − EV), assumes the remaining work will follow the original budget. A combined forecast may incorporate both cost and schedule efficiency where delay drives additional cost. Select the formula based on causal analysis, explain assumptions and update it as conditions change.

9. Quality and defect monitoring

Quality indicators may include first-pass acceptance, defect density, rework hours, test coverage, inspection failures, service incidents and user-reported problems. Measures should distinguish severity and consequence: one safety-critical defect may matter more than many cosmetic issues. Monitor trends and recurring causes, not just total counts. Ensure test results are traceable to the relevant version or asset. A project that reports high completion while carrying a large unresolved defect backlog may not be ready for handover. Agree quality thresholds and escalation routes in advance.

10. Risk and issue monitoring

Risk monitoring tracks changes in likelihood, impact, proximity, triggers and response effectiveness. Issue management records events that have already occurred, assigns owners and tracks resolution. A risk register should not become a substitute for an issue log. Monitor early warning indicators such as supplier lead times, staff turnover, design approvals, permit status and stakeholder complaints. Escalate exposures that exceed tolerance or cross organisational boundaries. Review whether assumptions remain valid and whether new risks have emerged from approved changes.

11. Change control and configuration management

Change control ensures proposed changes are assessed, authorised, recorded and incorporated into relevant plans. Configuration management identifies and controls the versions of products, documents, systems and technical baselines. A change request should describe the reason, options, impact on objectives, cost, schedule, quality, risk and benefits. Only authorised roles should approve changes. Keep traceability from requirement to design, test and delivered version. Informal changes can cause rework, inconsistent documents, disputes and unsafe operation.

12. Project reporting and dashboard design

A project report should answer what has happened, what it means, what is forecast and what decision is needed. An executive dashboard may show status, trends, key milestones, financial forecast, top risks, quality, benefits and decisions required. Use consistent definitions and show the reporting date. Red-amber-green ratings need explicit thresholds and narrative explanation. Avoid visual decoration that obscures poor performance. A concise report is not one that hides complexity; it is one that presents the most decision-relevant evidence clearly.

13. Data quality, governance and audit trails

Project data may be incomplete, late, duplicated or coded inconsistently. Establish data owners, definitions, validation rules, access permissions, retention periods and correction procedures. Reconcile project records with finance, procurement and operational systems. Preserve audit trails for status changes, approvals and baseline revisions. Protect personal, commercially sensitive and security-related information. Data governance should support trustworthy decisions while respecting applicable privacy and records-management obligations. Never present an estimate as an actual or a forecast as a confirmed outcome.

14. Evaluation: formative, summative and impact

Formative evaluation provides feedback during delivery so the project can improve. Summative evaluation judges performance at a defined point, often against objectives and criteria. Impact evaluation examines broader changes attributable to an intervention, which may require a longer time horizon and comparison strategy. Evaluation questions should be agreed early and linked to a theory of change. Consider unintended effects and distribution of benefits. A project can deliver outputs successfully but fail to achieve outcomes because adoption, context or external conditions differ from assumptions.

15. Benefits measurement and realisation

Benefits are measurable improvements expected from project outputs. Define each benefit, baseline, target, measurement method, data owner, timing and operational dependency. Some benefits appear only after users adopt a new process or service. Assign benefits ownership to people who remain accountable after the project team closes. Distinguish benefits from deliverables and activities. A training programme is an output; improved competency or reduced error may be a benefit. Review disbenefits and unintended consequences alongside positive outcomes.

16. Forecasting, scenarios and corrective action

Forecasting uses current evidence to estimate future dates, costs, resource needs and outcomes. Scenario analysis tests plausible combinations of conditions, such as supplier delay plus price inflation or lower adoption plus higher support demand. Corrective action addresses a performance problem; preventive action reduces the likelihood of a future problem. Actions should be specific, owned, resourced and time-bound. After implementation, verify whether the action improved performance and whether it created new risks. Avoid repeatedly changing plans without testing the underlying cause.

17. Independent assurance and stage gates

Independent assurance provides objective challenge on project viability, governance, controls, risk, delivery confidence and readiness. Stage gates are decision points where evidence is reviewed before committing to the next phase or releasing funds. Criteria should be proportionate and established in advance. An assurance review is not a substitute for management responsibility; it informs authorised decision-makers. Findings should identify evidence, implications, recommendations and owners. Projects should be able to pause or stop when the case for continued investment is no longer credible.

18. Monitoring social, environmental and service outcomes

Performance monitoring should include relevant social, environmental and service indicators, not only schedule and cost. Measures may cover accessibility, safety, emissions, energy, waste, local service reach, user satisfaction and equity of outcomes. Choose indicators that reflect the project's intended contribution and can be measured credibly. Disaggregate data where lawful and appropriate to identify who benefits and who may be excluded. Consult affected groups and explain limitations. Avoid claiming impact from simple before-and-after comparisons where other factors may explain the change.

20. Project closure, evaluation and lessons learned

Closure confirms deliverable acceptance, financial reconciliation, contract completion, document archiving, resource release and transfer of residual risks. Evaluation compares actual outputs and outcomes with the business case and agreed measures. Lessons should identify context, evidence, causes and practical recommendations rather than vague statements such as “communicate better”. Capture what worked, what failed, why and under what conditions the lesson may apply elsewhere. Assign post-project benefit owners and schedule reviews when longer-term outcomes can be observed.

Ask whose interests may be overlooked, what could change the conclusion, whether the proposed response creates new risks and how uncertainty will be communicated. Where evidence is incomplete, state the gap and propose a proportionate way to learn more. Review the result after implementation and preserve lessons that can improve future projects.

Study checkpoint: For assessment, define the concept, explain its purpose, describe the steps or components, apply it to a realistic example and evaluate at least one limitation. Support recommendations with evidence and identify how performance or compliance will be checked.

Integrated worked application

Consider a regional organisation implementing a service improvement across several sites. The sponsor has approved a strategic outcome, but the delivery team must translate it into an achievable plan. Begin with the intended user and organisational outcomes, then define the scope, constraints, assumptions and acceptance evidence. Map the stakeholders and identify who can approve decisions. Break the work into manageable packages, estimate the resources and sequence dependencies. Establish a baseline and a proportionate set of indicators. During implementation, compare actual evidence with the plan, investigate material differences and decide whether corrective action or formal change approval is required.

Suppose a new requirement emerges after a pilot reveals that a group of users cannot access the service through the intended channel. Do not treat this only as a technical defect. Analyse the requirement, accessibility implications, affected users, cost and schedule consequences, data and security considerations, operational support and expected benefits. Develop options such as adapting the interface, adding assisted access or revising rollout sequence. Evaluate each option against user outcomes, compliance, affordability and delivery risk. Present a recommendation with assumptions and an explicit decision request.

After approval, update the relevant plans and communicate the change to delivery and operational teams. Test the revised solution with representative users and collect evidence against acceptance criteria. At transition, confirm training, support, ownership, documentation and unresolved issues. Later, evaluate whether the service is used and whether the intended outcome has improved. This sequence demonstrates integration: project success is not a collection of separate documents but a coherent chain from need to sustainable benefit.

Extended case study: multi-site public service transformation

A public organisation intends to modernise a service delivered through twelve regional offices. The current process relies on paper records, repeated data entry and manual approvals. Users report long waiting times and inconsistent decisions. The proposed project introduces a shared digital platform, revised operating procedures and staff development. The organisation has a fixed funding envelope, varied internet connectivity, limited internal technical capacity and a target launch period. Some stakeholders support rapid implementation; others are concerned about privacy, job changes, accessibility and disruption.

Prepare a structured project response. Define the intended outcomes and measurable indicators. Identify stakeholders, their interests and engagement needs. Set out scope, exclusions, assumptions and constraints. Propose a delivery approach that allows learning while maintaining necessary governance. Develop a high-level schedule, budget logic, risk responses and quality criteria. Explain procurement and supplier oversight where external services are required. Plan training, data migration, operational readiness and post-launch support. Include an evaluation design that can distinguish outputs from outcomes and consider whether observed changes are plausibly connected to the intervention.

During implementation, the pilot identifies data inconsistencies and a connectivity gap in three offices. The supplier requests a variation and the sponsor asks whether the launch date can be retained. Analyse the decision using evidence rather than preference. Consider a phased release, additional validation, offline capability, targeted support or a revised timetable. Identify approval authority, impact on cost and benefits, and how users will be informed. State what evidence would justify proceeding, pausing or changing the plan. Your response should demonstrate professional judgement, ethical awareness and a clear audit trail.

Model answer guide: Identify the purpose and assumptions of each approach, compare their strengths and limitations, and explain which project conditions would make each more suitable. Avoid claiming that one method is universally superior.

3. Apply the chapter's methods to a project experiencing a major challenge.

Model answer guide: Define the problem, establish the evidence, identify affected stakeholders, develop feasible options, assess impacts on scope, time, cost, quality, risk and benefits, recommend an action and specify how it will be monitored.

4. Evaluate the ethical, legal and sustainability implications of a project decision.

Model answer guide: Identify relevant duties and affected groups, assess possible harm and distribution of benefits, explain applicable controls and show how transparency, participation and monitoring can improve the decision.

Model answer guide: Select indicators aligned to outcomes, define sources and frequency, explain thresholds and limitations, and distinguish activity, output, outcome and impact measures. Recommend a reporting format suited to decision-makers.

6. Explain how lessons learned should influence future projects.

Model answer guide: Use evidence to identify causes and context, distinguish effective practice from isolated success, formulate actionable recommendations and assign responsibility for embedding improvements.

Model answer guide: State the decision required, preferred option, evidence, assumptions, costs, benefits, main risks, alternatives, approvals, implementation responsibilities and review triggers.
