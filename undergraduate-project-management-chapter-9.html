1. Leadership and management in projects

Project management coordinates plans, resources, controls and decisions; leadership influences people toward a shared purpose. A project manager needs both. Management without leadership can create compliance without commitment, while leadership without sound management can generate enthusiasm without reliable delivery. Project environments are temporary, cross-functional and uncertain, so influence often matters more than formal authority. Effective leaders clarify outcomes, establish expectations, listen to concerns, make decisions at the right level and adapt their approach as the project moves from initiation to transition.

2. Leadership styles and situational judgement

Directive leadership can be useful during emergencies or when a team is inexperienced and safety-critical instructions must be clear. Participative leadership draws on specialist knowledge and increases ownership. Coaching develops capability; delegating gives competent team members authority within agreed boundaries. Transformational leadership can help communicate purpose during change, while transactional methods clarify performance expectations and consequences. No single style is universally best. Consider task urgency, competence, risk, culture, stakeholder needs and the maturity of the team before choosing an approach.

3. Emotional intelligence and self-awareness

Emotional intelligence includes recognising one's own emotions, understanding their effect on behaviour, reading social cues and regulating responses. Project managers face pressure from delay, conflict, uncertainty and competing demands. Self-awareness helps them avoid reacting defensively to bad news or interpreting disagreement as disloyalty. Empathy helps identify the concerns behind resistance, but it does not mean agreeing with every request. Leaders should seek feedback, reflect on decisions and use appropriate support when stress affects judgement or wellbeing.

4. Building and developing project teams

Team development begins with clear roles, shared objectives, access to information and agreed ways of working. Members need to understand how their tasks connect to other work packages and who resolves interface issues. Induction should cover scope, safety, quality, communication, decision rights and escalation. Team development continues through coaching, peer learning, feedback and opportunities to build capability. A team is not effective merely because skilled individuals have been assembled; collaboration, trust and coordination determine whether their expertise is integrated.

5. Team development stages and group dynamics

A common model describes forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning. It is a useful lens, not a rigid sequence that every team follows. New members, scope changes or leadership transitions can return a team to earlier dynamics. During forming, clarify purpose and expectations. During conflict, make disagreement constructive and address role ambiguity. Norms should support accountability and respectful challenge. High performance requires shared ownership and reliable coordination. At closure, recognise contributions and transfer knowledge rather than abruptly disbanding the team.

6. Motivation and performance

Motivation is influenced by meaningful work, autonomy, competence, recognition, fairness, security and relationships. Extrinsic rewards can support specific goals but may distort behaviour if measures are narrow or easily manipulated. Project managers should understand what motivates different team members rather than assuming a uniform preference. Set clear and achievable goals, provide timely feedback, remove obstacles and recognise contributions fairly. Monitor workload and avoid rewarding long hours at the expense of quality, safety or wellbeing.

7. Delegation and accountability

Delegation transfers authority to perform defined work while the manager retains accountability for the overall project responsibilities assigned to them. A good delegation agreement defines expected outcome, scope, constraints, resources, decision limits, reporting and escalation. Avoid delegating responsibility without authority or information. Agree checkpoints based on risk rather than excessive monitoring. When work is not delivered, investigate whether expectations, skills, capacity and dependencies were realistic before concluding that an individual failed.

8. Conflict management and negotiation

Conflict may arise from scarce resources, competing priorities, unclear roles, technical disagreement, cultural differences or incompatible stakeholder objectives. Avoiding conflict can be appropriate for minor issues, but persistent unresolved conflict damages trust. Competing may be necessary for urgent safety or compliance decisions; accommodating can preserve relationships when an issue matters more to the other party; compromise shares concessions; collaboration seeks a solution addressing underlying interests. Negotiation should distinguish positions from interests and use objective criteria where possible.

9. Psychological safety and speaking up

Psychological safety is the shared belief that people can raise questions, report mistakes and challenge assumptions without humiliation or retaliation. It does not remove accountability or performance standards. Leaders build it by responding constructively to concerns, acknowledging uncertainty, inviting dissent and following up on reported issues. In safety-critical projects, a culture where staff conceal near misses is dangerous. Establish confidential and accessible reporting routes, protect whistleblowers according to applicable rules and demonstrate that raising a concern leads to fair investigation.

10. Diversity, inclusion and cross-cultural teams

Project teams may include people from different countries, professions, languages, generations and social backgrounds. Diversity can broaden problem-solving, but only when people have equitable access to information and influence. Use plain language, clarify acronyms, rotate meeting times across time zones and provide accessible formats. Do not interpret silence as agreement or direct communication as hostility without understanding context. Make decisions transparent and assess whether recruitment, assignment and recognition practices create barriers. Respect local law and cultural context while maintaining non-negotiable standards for dignity and safety.

11. Communication planning and information needs

Communication planning identifies who needs what information, why, when, through which channel, in what format and from whom. A sponsor may need concise decisions and exceptions; a technical team needs specifications and interface details; a community may need accessible information about disruption and grievance channels. Communication should be timely, accurate, consistent and proportionate. Avoid sending every document to every stakeholder. Excessive information can hide important messages and create version confusion. Define authoritative repositories and rules for urgent communication.

12. Meetings, facilitation and decision records

A useful meeting has a purpose, participants with relevant knowledge or authority, an agenda, pre-reading where needed and a clear expected outcome. Facilitation ensures balanced participation and keeps discussion focused. Record decisions, rationale, actions, owners and due dates. Distinguish decisions from proposals and unresolved questions. Use time-boxing without suppressing legitimate technical concerns. Cancel meetings that have no clear purpose or can be handled asynchronously. Review recurring meetings to ensure they support coordination rather than merely consume capacity.

13. Stakeholder mapping and engagement

Stakeholder analysis identifies people and organisations that affect or are affected by the project. Map their interests, influence, impact, expectations, concerns and preferred engagement methods. Power-interest grids can help prioritise attention, but should not cause low-power groups to be ignored when they bear significant consequences. Engagement should be two-way and accessible. Record commitments and explain how feedback influenced decisions. Reassess the map when project phases, leadership, community conditions or regulatory requirements change.

14. Difficult conversations and feedback

Feedback should describe observable behaviour or work, its impact and the change required. Avoid labels about personality or motives. Ask questions and listen to the person's perspective before agreeing actions. Difficult conversations may involve missed commitments, poor quality, unsafe conduct or stakeholder dissatisfaction. Prepare evidence, choose a private and appropriate setting, state the issue clearly and agree a follow-up. Praise should be specific and genuine. Feedback is most useful when it supports learning and accountability rather than embarrassment.

15. Remote and hybrid team leadership

Remote teams need explicit coordination because informal information exchange is reduced. Agree core collaboration hours, response expectations, documentation standards, escalation routes and meeting etiquette. Use shared workspaces with version control and clear ownership. Avoid measuring commitment through online presence or message volume. Consider connectivity costs, accessibility, data security and time-zone burden. Hybrid meetings should not privilege people in the room; provide equal access to materials, discussion and decisions. Periodic in-person interaction may help relationships where feasible and safe.

16. Ethics, power and responsible leadership

Project leaders exercise power through resource allocation, access to information, performance assessment and decision-making. Ethical leadership requires honesty, fairness, confidentiality, respect, transparency and willingness to challenge improper pressure. Disclose conflicts of interest and follow procurement, safeguarding and anti-corruption rules. Do not manipulate status reports to protect reputation or pressure staff to conceal defects. When a decision creates harm or unfairly distributes benefits, examine who is affected and whether mitigation or escalation is required. Personal integrity is demonstrated through difficult decisions, not slogans.

17. Change leadership and organisational readiness

Project outputs create value only when people adopt and use them. Change leadership explains why change is needed, what will change, what will remain, how people will be supported and how success will be measured. Assess readiness through leadership commitment, skills, capacity, systems, culture and user confidence. Identify change impacts by role and location. Provide training, job aids, coaching and feedback routes. Adoption problems should not automatically be blamed on resistance; the solution may be redesign, better support or removal of practical barriers.

19. Team performance review and closure

Evaluate team performance using delivery outcomes, collaboration, quality, learning, safety and stakeholder feedback rather than a single productivity measure. Conduct retrospectives that identify what to continue, stop and improve. Recognise contributions equitably and address unresolved conflict before closure where possible. Transfer knowledge, archive decisions, release resources responsibly and support staff transition to new assignments. A temporary team should finish with clarity about ownership of unfinished work, operational issues and benefits. Closure is both an administrative process and a human transition.

Worked application and decision framework

Consider a medium-sized public-sector project with a fixed service deadline, several technical workstreams and a diverse group of users. The project manager first confirms the approved objectives and requirements, then identifies the decision that must be made. Evidence is collected from the schedule, budget, technical specialists, suppliers, operational staff and affected stakeholders. Assumptions are recorded separately from verified facts. Options are assessed against value, feasibility, risk, compliance, equity and lifecycle implications.

Suppose the team identifies three options: proceed with the original plan, introduce a controlled pilot, or defer the change until additional evidence is available. The team should compare direct and indirect consequences, including transition costs, staff capacity, service interruption and future flexibility. A weighted scoring model may structure discussion, but weights should be approved and sensitivity-tested. A high numerical score does not override a mandatory legal or safety requirement.

The recommendation should identify the preferred option, supporting evidence, unresolved uncertainty, resource requirements, approval authority, implementation sequence and review triggers. The decision log should record who approved it and why. After implementation, compare actual results with the expected outcomes and capture lessons. This worked process demonstrates how specialist project management methods connect analysis to accountable action.

Decision quality test: Is the problem defined? Are options genuinely comparable? Are assumptions visible? Are affected people represented? Are risks and lifecycle effects assessed? Is authority clear? Can the outcome be measured?

Integrated case study: Project Leadership, Teams and Communication in a county service improvement project

A county administration plans a service improvement project across several facilities. The project has a defined funding ceiling, a public launch commitment, limited specialist staff and communities with different access needs. During implementation, the team receives new information that challenges an assumption in the original plan. A supplier, operational manager and community representative propose different responses. The sponsor expects a recommendation supported by evidence and a realistic implementation route.

As a student, prepare an analysis using the chapter concepts. Identify the objective and decision boundary; distinguish facts, assumptions, issues and uncertainties; identify affected stakeholders; compare at least three response options; explain the effect on scope, time, cost, quality, risk and benefits; and recommend controls. State what additional evidence would change your recommendation. Include an action owner, due date, escalation threshold and measurable indicator.

In your discussion, consider fairness, accessibility, environmental and social impacts, data protection where relevant, and the ability of the operational team to sustain the outcome. Explain how you would communicate the decision to the sponsor, delivery team, supplier and community. Conclude with a short learning review describing what should be recorded for future projects.

University examination and revision questions

1. Explain the central purpose of project leadership, teams and communication.

Model answer: Define the concept, explain why it matters to project objectives and show how it links with scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, stakeholders and benefits. A strong answer uses a relevant example and distinguishes the method from the outcome it is intended to support.

2. Discuss four factors that influence the selection of an appropriate method.

Model answer: Consider project scale and complexity, uncertainty and data quality, regulatory and safety obligations, organisational capability, stakeholder needs, lifecycle consequences and the cost of analysis. Explain why the factors may lead to different choices in different contexts.

3. Apply the chapter framework to a project that is experiencing a significant change.

Model answer: Confirm the baseline and reason for change, identify impacts and affected parties, develop feasible options, assess evidence and risks, obtain authorised approval, update relevant plans and communicate the decision. Monitor implementation and verify whether the expected result was achieved.

4. Evaluate the limitations of relying only on numerical indicators.

Model answer: Indicators depend on definitions, data quality, timing and assumptions. They may hide distributional effects, emerging risks or user experience. Pair metrics with qualitative evidence, specialist judgement, audit trails and stakeholder feedback. Explain uncertainty rather than presenting a score as a complete description.

5. Explain how ethical and sustainability considerations should influence project decisions.

Model answer: Identify who receives benefits and who bears costs or risks. Consider legal duties, fairness, accessibility, worker and community welfare, environmental effects, lifecycle resource use and transparency. Demonstrate how these considerations affect option appraisal, controls, engagement and monitoring.

6. Prepare a concise recommendation for a project sponsor.

Model answer: State the decision required, preferred option, evidence, assumptions, expected benefits, costs, main risks, alternatives rejected, approvals needed, implementation responsibilities and review triggers. Use plain language and distinguish facts from estimates.

7. Short notes: define key terms, compare two approaches and identify one practical limitation for each.

Model answer: Award marks for accurate definitions, a meaningful comparison, contextual examples and balanced evaluation. Avoid listing terms without explaining their relevance to project decisions.

8. Essay: “Project success depends on more than delivering outputs on time and within budget.” Discuss.

Model answer: Explain the importance of time and cost while examining quality, safety, user adoption, benefits, sustainability, governance and operational readiness. Use examples to show why an efficient delivery process can still fail to create useful outcomes.
