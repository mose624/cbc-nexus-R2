Learning outcomes and academic pathway

Business and economics examine how individuals, organisations, markets and governments allocate resources, create value, manage risk and respond to incentives. Quantitative analysis should be combined with institutional, ethical, behavioural and social context.

Diploma foundations

Microeconomics

Scarcity requires choice. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone. Demand describes quantities consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices; supply describes quantities producers are willing and able to sell. Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Price changes cause movements along a curve; changes in income, preferences, technology or input costs can shift curves.

Worked equilibrium: If Qd=100−2P and Qs=20+2P, set them equal: 100−2P=20+2P; 80=4P; P*=20. Substitution gives Q*=60.

Accounting principles

The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Double-entry bookkeeping records equal debits and credits. The income statement reports performance over a period; the statement of financial position reports assets, liabilities and equity at a date; the cash-flow statement classifies cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit is not identical to cash flow.

Business management and marketing

Management coordinates people and resources through planning, organising, leading and controlling. Marketing begins with customer needs, segmentation, targeting and positioning. The marketing mix (often 4Ps: product, price, place and promotion) should be adapted to customer evidence, competition, channel economics and ethical obligations.

Entrepreneurship

A venture tests a value proposition: a defined customer problem, proposed solution, delivery model and reason customers may choose it. A business model explains customers, channels, key activities, partners, costs and revenue. Validate assumptions with interviews, prototypes and measured behaviour rather than relying only on stated enthusiasm.

Undergraduate theory and analytical tools

Macroeconomics

Gross domestic product measures the value of final production within an economy over a period. Real GDP adjusts for price changes. Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level; unemployment statistics depend on definitions of employment, availability and active job search. Fiscal policy uses taxation and public expenditure; monetary policy influences monetary and credit conditions, often through policy interest rates.

Corporate finance

Finance evaluates the timing, risk and opportunity cost of cash flows. Present value discounts future cash flows: PV=CFt/(1+r)t. Net present value (NPV) is the present value of expected inflows less the initial and subsequent investment outflows. Under appropriate assumptions, a positive NPV indicates value creation relative to the chosen discount rate.

Management and organisational behaviour

Study organisational structure, leadership, motivation, teams, culture, operations, supply chains, strategy and change. Frameworks such as SWOT or PESTLE organise questions but do not replace evidence. Evaluate how power, incentives, information, organisational routines and external institutions shape outcomes.

Econometrics and quantitative decisions

Regression estimates conditional relationships. In a simple model y=β₀+β₁x+u, β₁ describes the expected change in y associated with a one-unit change in x under the model. A causal interpretation requires a defensible identification strategy; omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error and selection can bias estimates. Report uncertainty, sample limitations and robustness checks.

Market structure and strategy

Competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly differ in entry barriers, product differentiation and strategic interdependence. Firms make decisions about pricing, capacity, innovation, market entry and positioning. Analyse the response of rivals and customers rather than assuming a firm acts in isolation.

Postgraduate and research-level study

Advanced study may include industrial organisation, behavioural and development economics, monetary and international economics, financial econometrics, asset pricing, risk management, corporate governance, strategy, operations research and impact evaluation. Research questions should identify a meaningful outcome, a theory of change, credible data and a method suited to the question.

Financial risk and governance

Risk analysis considers market, credit, liquidity, operational, model and regulatory risks. Diversification can reduce idiosyncratic risk but cannot eliminate all systematic exposure. Governance examines accountability, incentives, transparency, conflicts of interest and stakeholder rights. Financial ratios are diagnostic indicators whose meaning depends on industry, accounting policy and time period.

Responsible business and development

Assess labour conditions, consumer welfare, privacy, environmental externalities, tax conduct, inclusion and supply-chain impacts. Sustainability reporting should define boundaries, metrics, estimation methods and material uncertainties. Do not equate a voluntary disclosure with independently verified performance.

Worked quantitative examples

Contribution and break-even: Fixed costs are KSh 120,000; selling price per unit is KSh 500; variable cost is KSh 300. Contribution per unit = 500−300 = KSh 200. Break-even quantity = 120,000/200 = 600 units. This assumes constant prices and costs within the relevant range and all units produced are sold.

Simple present value: A cash flow of KSh 110,000 received in one year discounted at 10% has PV=110,000/1.10=KSh 100,000. Real investment decisions require all expected cash flows, timing, risk, taxes and a justified discount rate.

Elasticity: If price rises by 5% and quantity demanded falls by 10%, approximate price elasticity of demand is −10%/5%=−2; demand is elastic in absolute value. Use a midpoint calculation for finite changes when required.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Explain why a rise in demand does not always produce a large rise in equilibrium price.
The price effect depends on supply elasticity. A highly elastic supply curve permits greater quantity adjustment with a smaller price change; an inelastic supply curve produces a larger price response.
2. Distinguish profit from cash flow.
Profit is measured using accrual accounting and includes non-cash items; cash flow records actual cash receipts and payments. A profitable firm can still face liquidity problems.
3. Calculate break-even units if fixed costs are 90,000, price is 250 and variable cost is 160.
Contribution=90; break-even=90,000/90=1,000 units.
4. Why does regression correlation not establish causation?
Confounding, reverse causality, selection and measurement error may explain the association. A causal claim requires identification assumptions and credible research design.
5. Propose a market-entry analysis for a small firm.
Define target segment and need; estimate market size and willingness to pay; assess competitors, substitutes, regulation, channels, unit economics, capability and risks; test demand with a low-cost pilot and specify measurable decision criteria.

Frequently asked questions

Concise answers to common university study questions. Use the chapter explanations and recommended institutional materials for deeper study.

1. What is covered in university business and economics?

The subject area includes microeconomics, macroeconomics, accounting, corporate finance, marketing, management, econometrics, entrepreneurship and quantitative decision-making.

2. What is the difference between accounting profit and economic profit?

Accounting profit subtracts explicit costs from revenue; economic profit also accounts for implicit opportunity costs.

3. Why does business analysis use data?

Data can help test assumptions, measure performance, understand customers, quantify uncertainty and evaluate decisions, provided the analysis recognises bias and limitations.

4. Are financial examples investment advice?

No. The material is for education and analytical practice, not personalised investment, tax, legal or financial advice.

Chapter 1: Economic Reasoning, Scarcity and Decision-Making

Learning outcomes. Explain scarcity and opportunity cost; distinguish positive and normative statements; use marginal reasoning; interpret production possibility frontiers; and explain how incentives, institutions and information shape economic choices.

1.1 The economic problem

Economics studies how individuals, firms, governments and societies allocate scarce resources among competing uses. Scarcity exists because time, labour, land, capital, skills and natural resources are limited relative to wants. Scarcity does not mean that every person experiences the same shortage or that resources are distributed fairly. Economic analysis therefore examines both allocation and distribution: what is produced, how it is produced, who receives it and how outcomes change over time.

1.2 Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone when a choice is made. It is not necessarily the sum of every rejected alternative, nor is it always a cash payment. A student who spends an evening studying gives up the value of the best alternative use of that time, such as paid work or rest. A firm that uses a building it owns still incurs an opportunity cost if the building could be rented or used for another activity. Good decisions compare marginal benefits with marginal opportunity costs.

1.3 Marginal analysis and incentives

Marginal analysis asks how total benefits and costs change when an activity increases by one unit. A rational decision rule in a simplified model is to continue an activity while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, stopping near the point where they are equal. This is a framework, not a claim that people always have perfect information or calculate precisely. Behavioural economics studies bounded rationality, framing, present bias, social preferences and other systematic influences on choices. Incentives matter, but their effects depend on context, institutions and how people interpret them.

1.4 Production possibility frontier

A production possibility frontier (PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two outputs that can be produced with given resources and technology under stated assumptions. Points on the frontier are productively efficient in this simplified model; points inside indicate underused resources or inefficiency; points outside are unattainable with current resources and technology. A bowed-out frontier illustrates increasing opportunity cost when resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. Economic growth can shift the frontier outward through investment, education, innovation or improved institutions.

PPF interpretation: on the frontier = productive efficiency; inside = underutilisation; outside = currently unattainable. A movement along the curve represents a trade-off; an outward shift represents increased productive capacity.

1.5 Positive and normative economics

Positive economics analyses statements that can be assessed using evidence, such as whether a tax change is associated with a change in consumption. Normative economics concerns values and judgements, such as whether the tax is fair. Policy debate often combines both: evidence may estimate consequences, while ethical and political principles guide which outcomes are preferred. Analysts should identify the value assumptions behind recommendations rather than presenting them as purely technical facts.

1.6 Markets, institutions and coordination

Markets coordinate buyers and sellers through prices, contracts and competition. Prices can communicate scarcity and incentives, but markets operate within institutions: property rights, courts, regulations, social norms, infrastructure and monetary systems. Market outcomes may be inefficient or inequitable when externalities, public goods, market power, information asymmetry or missing markets are present. Government intervention can improve outcomes, but policy design also faces information limits, administrative costs, capture and unintended consequences.

1.7 Firms, households and the circular flow

Households supply factors of production and demand goods and services. Firms combine labour, capital, land and entrepreneurship to produce output. In the circular-flow model, factor payments flow from firms to households, while household expenditure flows to firms. In a more complete macroeconomic model, saving, investment, taxation, government expenditure, imports and exports are added. The model is a teaching device; actual economies include informal activity, unpaid care, non-market production and complex global supply chains.

1.8 Economic data and responsible interpretation

Economic indicators are constructed measures, not direct readings of an entire economy. Gross domestic product measures the market value of final goods and services produced within a territory over a period, subject to national accounting conventions. GDP per person is an average and does not reveal distribution, unpaid work, environmental depletion or every dimension of wellbeing. Inflation measures changes in a price index; unemployment depends on definitions of employment, job search and labour-force participation. Always state the measure, period, population and limitations.

Worked example – opportunity cost: A small business has KSh 100,000 and can purchase either equipment expected to generate net benefits of KSh 28,000 or inventory expected to generate net benefits of KSh 22,000 over the same period. If the estimates are comparable and risk is treated appropriately, choosing equipment has an opportunity cost of the best forgone alternative, KSh 22,000 in expected net benefit. The decision should also consider uncertainty, liquidity and strategic effects.

Chapter summary and review

  • Scarcity requires choices among competing uses of limited resources.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative.
  • Marginal analysis compares additional benefits with additional costs.
  • PPFs illustrate trade-offs, efficiency and productive capacity.
  • Economic indicators require careful definitions and do not measure all dimensions of welfare.
  1. Explain opportunity cost using a public university budget decision.
  2. Draw and interpret a PPF showing unemployment of resources and economic growth.

Chapter 2: Microeconomics — Demand, Supply, Elasticity and Market Equilibrium

2.1 Demand and the consumer

Demand describes the quantities of a good or service consumers are willing and able to purchase at different prices over a specified period, holding other relevant factors constant. The law of demand states that, in the usual case, quantity demanded falls as price rises, other things equal. A change in the good's own price produces a movement along a demand curve; changes in income, preferences, expectations, population or prices of related goods shift the curve. Normal goods generally experience higher demand as income rises; inferior goods may experience lower demand as income rises, although classification depends on context and income range.

2.2 Supply and the firm

Supply describes quantities producers are willing and able to sell at different prices, other things equal. Higher prices often encourage greater quantity supplied because additional production becomes profitable, subject to capacity and cost conditions. Input prices, technology, taxes, subsidies, expectations, weather and the number of firms can shift supply. A movement along supply is distinct from a shift of the entire curve. Short-run supply may be constrained by fixed capacity; long-run supply can respond through entry, exit and investment.

2.3 Market equilibrium and adjustment

Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At a price above equilibrium, a surplus may place downward pressure on price; below equilibrium, a shortage may place upward pressure on price, depending on adjustment institutions and constraints. Equilibrium is a model outcome, not proof that the market is fair or socially optimal. Price controls, rationing, bargaining, inventories and informal markets can alter adjustment mechanisms.

Demand slopes downward in the standard model; supply slopes upward. Their intersection gives the model's equilibrium price and quantity. A demand increase shifts demand right; a supply decrease shifts supply left.

2.4 Price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded associated with a one per cent change in price, using a defined method and holding other factors constant. In absolute value, demand is elastic when the response exceeds one, inelastic when it is below one, and unit elastic when it equals one. Elasticity depends on substitutes, necessity, budget share, time to adjust and market definition. The midpoint method calculates percentage changes using the average of initial and final values, reducing dependence on the direction of calculation.

Worked example: Price rises from 10 to 12, while quantity falls from 100 to 80. Midpoint percentage change in quantity = −20/90 ≈ −22.2%; midpoint percentage change in price = 2/11 ≈ 18.2%. Estimated elasticity ≈ −1.22, so demand is elastic over this interval. The estimate is specific to the observed range and conditions.

2.5 Income and cross-price elasticity

Income elasticity measures the percentage response of demand to a percentage change in income. A positive value is consistent with a normal good; a negative value is consistent with an inferior good. Cross-price elasticity measures the response of demand for one product to a price change in another. Positive cross-price elasticity is commonly associated with substitutes; negative values with complements. These are empirical relationships and may change across consumer groups or time.

2.6 Costs, revenue and firm decisions

Total revenue equals price multiplied by quantity sold. Total cost includes fixed and variable costs over the relevant period. Average cost is total cost per unit; marginal cost is the additional cost of producing one more unit. In standard profit-maximisation models, a firm chooses output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, subject to market structure, capacity, shutdown conditions and uncertainty. Accounting cost and economic cost differ because economic cost includes opportunity costs of owned resources.

2.7 Market structures

Perfect competition is a benchmark with many firms, homogeneous products, information and free entry and exit. Monopoly involves a single seller protected by barriers to entry; oligopoly has a small number of strategically interdependent firms; monopolistic competition combines many firms with differentiated products. Real markets often fall between textbook categories. Market power may affect price, innovation, quality and distribution. Competition policy assesses evidence, market definition, barriers and consumer or public effects under the applicable legal framework.

2.8 Market failure and public policy

Externalities occur when a transaction affects third parties not fully reflected in the market price. Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable in the ideal definition, which can create free-rider problems. Information asymmetry arises when one party knows more than another, potentially affecting quality, insurance or credit markets. Policy options include taxes, subsidies, regulation, public provision, disclosure rules and tradable permits. Evaluation should compare expected benefits, administrative costs, distributional effects, enforcement capacity and unintended consequences.

Review questions

  1. Distinguish a movement along a demand curve from a shift in demand.
  2. Explain how a production-cost increase affects supply and equilibrium in the standard model.
  3. Calculate and interpret price elasticity using the midpoint method.
  4. Compare monopoly and oligopoly in terms of strategic behaviour and barriers to entry.
  5. Give one example each of an externality, public good and information asymmetry.

Chapter 3: Macroeconomics — Output, Inflation, Employment and Policy

3.1 Measuring aggregate economic activity

Macroeconomics studies economy-wide outcomes such as output, employment, inflation, interest rates, productivity, public finance and international trade. Gross domestic product can be measured through production, income or expenditure approaches, which are conceptually equivalent when national accounts are complete and consistent. The expenditure identity is commonly written Y = C + I + G + (X − M), where consumption, investment, government purchases, exports and imports are defined under national accounting conventions. Transfers are not counted as government purchases of current output.

3.2 Nominal and real variables

Nominal GDP values output at current prices; real GDP adjusts for price changes using an appropriate deflator or volume measure. Growth in nominal output may reflect more production, higher prices or both. Real GDP per capita adjusts for population but remains an average. Productivity, distribution, unpaid activity, environmental sustainability and wellbeing require additional measures. Comparisons across countries require attention to exchange rates, purchasing-power adjustments, data quality and institutional differences.

3.3 Inflation and price indices

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level, commonly measured using a consumer price index or other price index. A price index tracks a defined basket or weighting structure and may not reflect every household's spending pattern. Inflation can arise from demand pressures, supply shocks, exchange-rate changes, expectations and monetary or fiscal conditions. Disinflation means inflation is slowing; deflation means the general price level is falling. Neither term should be confused with a fall in the price of one product.

3.4 Unemployment and labour markets

Unemployment statistics depend on definitions of working age, employment, active job search and availability. The unemployment rate does not include every person who wants more work; underemployment and labour-force participation provide additional information. Structural unemployment can arise from mismatch between skills and vacancies; frictional unemployment reflects transitions; cyclical unemployment is associated with fluctuations in aggregate demand. Informal employment and unpaid work complicate comparisons across economies.

3.5 Aggregate demand and aggregate supply

The aggregate demand and aggregate supply framework represents relationships between the overall price level and real output under a particular model. Shifts in consumption, investment, government purchases, net exports or monetary conditions may shift aggregate demand. Supply shocks, productivity, input costs and capacity can shift aggregate supply. The model helps organise short-run analysis, but its conclusions depend on assumptions about price adjustment, expectations, capacity and policy transmission.

3.6 Fiscal and monetary policy

Fiscal policy uses government spending, taxation and borrowing to influence aggregate demand and public services. Automatic stabilisers change budget balances as incomes and employment vary; discretionary measures require policy decisions. Monetary policy influences financial conditions through policy rates, liquidity operations, communication and other instruments, depending on the central-bank framework. Transmission occurs through borrowing costs, exchange rates, asset prices, expectations and credit conditions. Lags and uncertainty make policy calibration difficult.

3.7 Public debt and fiscal sustainability

Public debt is assessed relative to the economy's capacity to service it, the currency and maturity structure, interest costs, revenue base, growth prospects and institutional credibility. A debt ratio alone does not fully describe fiscal risk. Borrowing may finance productive infrastructure or stabilise an economy during downturns, but persistent deficits, weak revenue mobilisation and costly debt service can constrain future choices. Distributional effects and intergenerational consequences are central to evaluation.

3.8 International trade and exchange rates

Trade allows specialisation and exchange across borders. Comparative advantage is based on relative opportunity cost, not absolute productivity alone. Trade can raise aggregate efficiency while creating adjustment costs and uneven gains across workers, firms and regions. Exchange rates affect import prices, export competitiveness, external debt and capital flows. Balance-of-payments accounts record transactions between residents and non-residents under defined conventions. A current-account deficit is not automatically a crisis; its financing and underlying causes matter.

Chapter 4: Financial Accounting, Corporate Finance and Decision Analysis

4.1 Purpose of financial accounting

Financial accounting communicates an entity's financial position, performance and cash flows to users such as investors, lenders, regulators and other stakeholders. Financial statements are prepared under a reporting framework and rely on recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure rules. Accounting information is not a complete valuation of an organisation: it includes estimates, policies and historical transactions, while some internally generated resources may not be recognised as assets.

4.2 The accounting equation and double entry

The basic accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Every transaction affects at least two accounts under double-entry bookkeeping, maintaining the equation. Assets are resources controlled as a result of past events; liabilities are present obligations; equity is the residual interest. Revenue and expenses affect performance and, through retained earnings or equivalent accounts, influence equity. Correct classification and cut-off are essential for reliable reporting.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Income − Expenses = Profit or loss for the period
Opening equity + contributions + profit − distributions = closing equity (simplified)

4.3 Core financial statements

The statement of financial position reports assets, liabilities and equity at a reporting date. The income statement or statement of profit or loss reports income and expenses over a period. The cash-flow statement classifies cash flows into operating, investing and financing activities under the applicable framework. Notes explain accounting policies, estimates, commitments and additional detail. Profit is not the same as cash flow: credit sales can create revenue before cash is collected, and depreciation is an expense without a current-period cash payment.

4.4 Accruals, depreciation and working capital

Accrual accounting recognises economic events when recognition criteria are met rather than simply when cash changes hands. Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount of a tangible asset over its useful life; it is not necessarily a measure of current market value. Working capital is commonly current assets less current liabilities. Inventory, receivables and payables management affects liquidity and operating cash conversion. Ratios such as current ratio, quick ratio, gross margin and return on assets provide signals but require comparison over time and with relevant peers.

4.5 Time value of money

The time value of money recognises that a unit of currency today can be invested or used before a future receipt. Present value discounts future cash flows; future value compounds current amounts. The discount rate reflects time, risk and opportunity cost under a defined model. Net present value is the present value of expected cash inflows minus investment outflows. A positive NPV indicates value creation relative to the chosen discount rate and assumptions, not certainty that a project will succeed.

Worked example: A project is expected to generate KSh 60,000 in one year. At a 10% annual discount rate, its present value is 60,000/1.10 = KSh 54,545 (approximately). If the initial cost is KSh 50,000, simplified NPV is about KSh 4,545 before considering other cash flows, taxes, risk adjustments or working capital.

4.6 Risk, return and capital structure

Investors generally require compensation for bearing risk, but realised returns can differ from expected returns. Diversification can reduce some firm-specific risk but cannot eliminate market-wide risk. Debt can provide tax or financing advantages in some systems, but it creates contractual obligations and financial distress risk. Equity avoids fixed repayment but dilutes ownership and may carry a higher required return. Capital structure decisions depend on cash-flow stability, growth opportunities, asset types, taxes, regulation and market conditions.

4.7 Budgeting and management accounting

Management accounting supports planning, control and decisions inside an organisation. Budgets translate objectives into expected resources and activities. Variance analysis compares actual results with budget and investigates price, volume, efficiency and mix effects. Relevant-cost analysis focuses on future costs and benefits that differ between alternatives; sunk costs should not determine a forward-looking choice. Non-financial indicators such as quality, delivery reliability, customer retention and safety may be as important as financial measures.

  3. Explain why depreciation is an allocation rather than a direct cash outflow in the period.
  4. Calculate present value for a future receipt and interpret the discount rate.
  5. Compare debt and equity financing, including risks.
  6. Explain why sunk costs should not drive a future investment decision.

Chapter 5: Management, Organisational Behaviour and Marketing Strategy

5.1 Management and organisational purpose

Management coordinates people and resources to achieve organisational objectives. Common functions include planning, organising, leading and controlling. Strategy connects an organisation's purpose and capabilities to its external environment. Effective objectives are specific enough to guide action and measurable enough to support review. Organisations should consider financial results alongside customer value, employee wellbeing, legal duties, environmental impact and long-term resilience.

5.2 Organisational structure and culture

Structure defines roles, authority, reporting relationships and coordination mechanisms. Functional structures group similar expertise; divisional structures organise around products, regions or customers; matrix structures combine dimensions but may create competing priorities. Culture includes shared assumptions, values and practices. Culture can support learning and ethical behaviour, but strong norms can also suppress dissent. Leaders shape culture through incentives, decisions, communication and responses to failure.

5.3 Motivation, teams and leadership

Motivation theories examine needs, expectations, goals, fairness, autonomy and the relationship between effort and outcomes. No single theory explains all behaviour. Team performance depends on clear roles, psychological safety, task interdependence, communication and constructive conflict management. Leadership may be directive, participative, coaching or distributed depending on task, risk and team capability. Ethical leadership requires transparency, accountability and respect.

5.4 Human resource management

Human resource management includes workforce planning, recruitment, selection, onboarding, performance development, compensation, employee relations, occupational safety and retention. Fair selection uses job-relevant criteria and structured evaluation. Performance systems should clarify expectations and provide useful feedback rather than merely rank staff. Employment law and worker protections differ by jurisdiction; organisations must consult current local requirements and avoid discriminatory practices.

5.5 Marketing and customer value

Marketing identifies customer needs, creates value and develops relationships. Segmentation divides a market into meaningful groups; targeting selects groups an organisation can serve effectively; positioning defines the intended place of an offering in the customer's mind relative to alternatives. The marketing mix is often described through product, price, place and promotion, with extended frameworks adding people, process and physical evidence for services. Marketing claims must be accurate and substantiated.

Research and insight → segmentation → targeting → positioning → offer and experience → delivery → measurement and learning

5.6 Consumer behaviour and market research

Consumer decisions may be influenced by needs, information, social context, culture, perceived risk, habits and post-purchase experience. Research methods include surveys, interviews, observation, experiments and analysis of transaction data. Sampling and question wording can bias results. Correlation in customer data does not necessarily reveal causal impact. Ethical research requires appropriate consent, privacy safeguards and transparent use of personal data.

5.7 Digital marketing and performance

Digital channels include search, websites, email, social platforms, marketplaces and paid advertising. A useful measurement framework distinguishes reach, engagement, conversion, retention and customer lifetime value. Attribution models distribute credit for conversions across touchpoints, but each model has limitations. Search visibility depends on useful content, technical accessibility, relevance, trust and user experience; repeated keyword insertion is not a substitute for quality. Marketing automation should respect consent and provide clear controls.

5.8 Strategy execution and balanced performance

Strategy execution requires resources, accountable owners, milestones, risk controls and feedback. A balanced scorecard may combine financial, customer, internal-process and learning measures. Key performance indicators should connect to outcomes and be resistant to gaming. Review cycles should distinguish leading indicators from lagging outcomes and support adaptation when assumptions fail.

Review questions

  1. Compare functional and divisional organisational structures.
  2. Explain how organisational culture can both support and inhibit innovation.
  3. Apply segmentation, targeting and positioning to a university online course.
  4. Identify limitations of a survey measuring customer satisfaction.
  5. Distinguish reach, engagement, conversion and retention metrics.
  6. Explain why a balanced set of performance indicators is preferable to a single sales target.
