CBE NEXUS · UNDERGRADUATE PSYCHOLOGY · CHAPTER 13

Counselling Skills, Ethics and Professional Practice

Undergraduate textbook notes · Counselling theories · Communication · Ethics · Professional competence

Learning outcomes: Explain major counselling approaches; demonstrate core communication principles; describe assessment, contracting and formulation; apply ethical reasoning; and identify boundaries, supervision, safeguarding and referral responsibilities.

Introduction

Counselling is both a relational practice and a disciplined professional activity. It requires listening carefully, understanding the client's context, selecting methods for defensible reasons and working within clear ethical boundaries. Technical skills matter, but they are not separate from respect, collaboration and professional accountability. This chapter reviews the theories, skills and standards that support safe, culturally responsive and effective counselling across educational, community and health settings. It is an academic resource and does not replace local professional regulation, supervision or emergency services.

1. Meaning, scope and purposes of counselling

Counselling is a purposeful professional relationship in which a trained practitioner helps a person explore concerns, understand experiences, develop resources and make choices consistent with their values. It may address emotional distress, relationships, transitions, identity, bereavement, work, education, health behaviour and personal development. Counselling is not simply giving advice, persuading a client to adopt the counsellor's beliefs or offering reassurance without understanding the problem. It creates a structured space for reflection, emotional expression, learning and change. The precise scope depends on the practitioner's education, supervised experience, professional registration and local legal framework.

Counselling may be delivered individually, with couples, families or groups, and through face-to-face or remote formats. Its goals are negotiated rather than assumed. One client may want to make sense of grief; another may seek strategies for panic, communication or a difficult decision. The practitioner clarifies what counselling can and cannot offer, checks whether the presenting concern fits their competence and reviews progress regularly. Counselling can complement medical, social, educational or legal support, but it should not be presented as a replacement for services that the person needs.

2. Counselling, psychotherapy, coaching and related services

Counselling and psychotherapy overlap substantially. In some jurisdictions the terms describe different levels or styles of training; in others they are used interchangeably. Psychotherapy often refers to structured psychological treatment for persistent or complex difficulties, while counselling may include support with current problems and life transitions. These distinctions are not universal, so practitioners must explain their actual qualifications and scope rather than relying on a title. Coaching usually focuses on goals, performance or development and is not necessarily a mental-health treatment. Peer support draws on shared experience and can be valuable, but it has a different role from regulated clinical practice.

Counsellors also work alongside psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers, nurses, teachers, chaplains and community organisations. Referral is appropriate when a client needs medical assessment, specialist trauma care, substance withdrawal management, safeguarding intervention or another service outside the counsellor's competence. Ethical collaboration requires consent, clarity about roles and secure information sharing. A counsellor should never imply that a brief supportive conversation qualifies them to diagnose or treat every mental disorder.

3. Historical and theoretical foundations

Counselling developed from educational and vocational guidance, humanistic psychology, psychodynamic thought, behavioural learning theory, cognitive science and community mental-health practice. Different traditions offered different explanations of distress: unconscious conflict and early relationships; learned responses and reinforcement; beliefs and information processing; self-concept and the need for acceptance; or interaction between people and their environments. Contemporary practice includes integrative and pluralistic approaches, with increased attention to culture, power, trauma, evidence and client choice.

Historical traditions should be studied critically. Some theories were developed from narrow populations and may not generalise across cultures or social conditions. Earlier services sometimes pathologised difference or ignored structural injustice. Modern counsellors should distinguish a theory's useful concepts from assumptions unsupported by evidence. Theories are maps, not the person or the territory; they guide questions and interventions but must be tested against the client's account and outcomes.

4. Core counselling values and ethical principles

Respect for dignity, autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, fidelity and integrity provides a foundation for professional counselling. Respect requires taking the client's perspective seriously without endorsing harmful behaviour. Autonomy means supporting informed choices rather than controlling the person's decisions. Beneficence asks what is likely to help; non-maleficence requires considering foreseeable harm. Justice concerns fair access and avoiding discrimination. Fidelity includes reliability, honesty and keeping appropriate commitments. These principles may conflict, so ethical practice requires reasoned judgement rather than a checklist alone.

Ethical decisions should consider the client's rights, relevant law, professional codes, organisational policy, consultation and the likely consequences of available options. A counsellor should document the issue, alternatives considered, advice sought and rationale for action. Where a client is at risk, the counsellor balances confidentiality with duties to protect. Ethical practice also includes recognising power differences, avoiding exploitation, maintaining boundaries and being transparent about fees, cancellations, records and service limitations.

5. The counselling relationship and therapeutic alliance

The counselling relationship is a professional alliance organised around the client's goals and the agreed work. Its quality is influenced by trust, emotional safety, collaboration, empathy, respect, consistency and clarity. A client may initially be cautious, ambivalent or uncertain, especially after previous experiences of judgement or coercion. The counsellor should not interpret hesitation as resistance without exploring its meaning. Reliability is demonstrated through punctuality, clear explanations, appropriate boundaries and a willingness to acknowledge misunderstandings.

The alliance is not simply being friendly. It includes agreement about what the work is for, what each person will do and how progress will be reviewed. The counsellor invites feedback about pace, usefulness and cultural fit. If the client feels misunderstood, the practitioner listens without defensiveness and works to repair the relationship. Ruptures can provide important information about expectations, power, communication and safety.

6. Person-centred counselling

Person-centred counselling is associated with the work of Carl Rogers and emphasises the client's capacity for growth within a facilitative relationship. Three central therapist conditions are congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding. Congruence refers to genuineness and appropriate transparency; unconditional positive regard means valuing the person without making acceptance conditional on agreement; empathy involves entering the client's frame of reference while retaining professional perspective. The counsellor does not assume the role of expert who directs every decision.

Person-centred practice uses careful listening, reflection, clarification and acceptance to help clients explore experiences and develop self-understanding. It does not mean passive silence or absence of structure. The practitioner remains attentive to risk, goals, time limits and the need for referral. The approach may be especially useful when clients need a respectful space to explore identity, grief, relationships or difficult choices, although suitability depends on the presenting concern and the client's preferences.

7. Psychodynamic and attachment-informed counselling

Psychodynamic approaches explore recurring emotional and relational patterns, internal conflicts, defences and the influence of earlier experience on present life. Attachment-informed work considers how early and later relationships may shape expectations about closeness, safety, trust and help-seeking. These perspectives can help a client notice repeated patterns, such as withdrawing when support is offered or expecting rejection during disagreement. The counsellor approaches such hypotheses tentatively and avoids claiming certainty about events or motives that cannot be established.

Transference describes ways in which feelings and expectations from other relationships may be experienced in the therapeutic relationship; countertransference refers to the practitioner's emotional responses that may provide information but also require reflection. These concepts should not be used to dismiss a client's valid criticism. Supervision helps counsellors distinguish personal reactions from clinically relevant observations. Psychodynamic work requires appropriate training, clear boundaries and careful pacing, particularly where trauma or instability is present.

8. Behavioural approaches and learning principles

Behavioural counselling examines how actions are shaped by antecedents, consequences, modelling and learning history. A functional analysis identifies what happens before a behaviour, the behaviour itself and what follows it. For example, avoiding a feared presentation may reduce anxiety immediately, negatively reinforcing avoidance and making future presentations more difficult. Counselling may use graded practice, reinforcement of adaptive behaviour, activity scheduling, skills rehearsal and environmental changes. Interventions should be collaborative and should not expose clients to genuine danger.

Behavioural principles can support habit change, anxiety management, study routines, sleep-related behaviour and communication practice. The counsellor and client define observable goals and review whether the strategy works in the person's actual context. A behaviour that appears unhelpful may serve a protective function, particularly in unsafe environments. The practitioner therefore investigates context before attempting to change it. Behavioural methods should be adapted for disability, neurodiversity, culture and practical constraints.

9. Cognitive approaches and cognitive behavioural counselling

Cognitive approaches examine how interpretations, assumptions, attention and beliefs influence emotional and behavioural responses. Cognitive behavioural counselling commonly maps the interaction among situation, thought, emotion, physical response and behaviour. A client who interprets a delayed reply as proof of rejection may experience anxiety and repeatedly seek reassurance. The counsellor helps examine the evidence, generate alternative explanations and test predictions in a respectful, collaborative way. The aim is balanced thinking, not forced optimism or denial of real difficulties.

Cognitive techniques include guided discovery, thought records, behavioural experiments, problem-solving and identification of thinking patterns. The counsellor should avoid arguing with a client or treating every negative thought as distorted. Some fears accurately reflect discrimination, violence or economic insecurity; practical problem-solving and advocacy may be more appropriate than cognitive reframing alone. CBT-informed work is structured but flexible, and requires attention to the client's readiness, literacy, language and goals.

10. Solution-focused and strengths-based counselling

Solution-focused brief counselling concentrates on the client's preferred future, exceptions to the problem and small achievable changes. Questions may explore what would be different if the concern improved, when the problem is less intense and what the client is already doing that helps. Scaling questions can support discussion of confidence or progress, provided the numbers are treated as prompts rather than objective measurements. The approach does not require the counsellor to deny hardship or insist that every problem has a quick solution.

Strengths-based practice identifies capacities, relationships, knowledge, cultural resources, past achievements and community supports that may assist change. Strengths should be recognised accurately rather than used to minimise suffering. A person facing bereavement may have resilience and still need time, support and practical help. The counsellor collaborates to identify one or two realistic next steps and reviews whether they are useful. This approach can be particularly helpful in brief services, educational settings and contexts where clients want practical movement towards a chosen goal.

11. Motivational interviewing and readiness for change

Motivational interviewing is a collaborative method for exploring ambivalence and evoking a person's own reasons for change. It is often useful when a client feels both drawn to and concerned about a behaviour, such as substance use, medication adherence or health routines. Core communication skills include open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries. The counsellor listens for change talk, explores confidence and importance, and avoids argument, confrontation or shame.

Readiness is not a fixed personality trait. It may vary across behaviours and circumstances, and a client may be willing to consider one small step while remaining uncertain about a larger change. The counsellor respects autonomy and asks permission before offering information. Advice is more likely to be useful when linked to the client's goals and concerns. Motivational interviewing is not manipulation; it should not be used to pressure a person into choices that primarily serve the institution or practitioner.

12. Counselling communication skills: listening and questioning

Active listening involves attending to words, emotion, pace, silence, non-verbal communication and the meaning a client gives to events. Minimal encouragers, paraphrasing, summarising and reflection can communicate attention and help organise a complex account. Open questions invite elaboration; closed questions clarify specific facts. A counsellor balances both, avoiding rapid-fire interrogation. Questions should be purposeful, understandable and sensitive to culture, trauma and the client's communication style.

Silence may allow reflection, but it can also feel uncomfortable or unsafe. The counsellor checks rather than assumes its meaning. Reflection of feeling tentatively names an emotion and invites correction: “It sounds as though you felt overlooked; have I understood?” Summaries connect themes and check accuracy. Advice-giving should be cautious because premature solutions can displace the client's agenda. The counsellor should be alert to jargon, leading questions, double questions and assumptions embedded in language.

13. Empathy, validation and emotional regulation in sessions

Empathy is the effort to understand a client's subjective experience and communicate that understanding accurately. Validation acknowledges that an emotion or response makes sense in the context described; it does not mean approving every action or confirming every interpretation as fact. For example, a counsellor can validate fear after a threatening event while helping the client distinguish current danger from reminders of past danger. Validation is particularly important when clients have experienced stigma, discrimination or repeated dismissal.

Counsellors also need to regulate their own emotional responses. Strong feelings may arise when hearing about trauma, injustice, loss or risk. Grounding, reflective pauses, supervision and appropriate self-care support clear judgement. The practitioner should not use the client to meet personal emotional needs or disclose personal experiences in ways that shift attention away from the client. Self-disclosure, when used, must have a clear therapeutic rationale and should be reviewed for its effects.

14. Assessment, contracting and goal setting

Initial counselling assessment clarifies the presenting concern, its duration and impact, relevant history, strengths, support, previous help, expectations and immediate safety needs. The counsellor explains confidentiality and exceptions, records, session length, fees, cancellation arrangements, communication between sessions and the likely review point. A counselling contract is a shared understanding, not a device for removing professional responsibility. It should be written or communicated accessibly and revisited when circumstances change.

Goals should be meaningful, realistic and developed with the client. They may concern understanding, coping, relationships, behaviour, safety or practical decisions. Specific goals make review easier, but not every valuable outcome can be reduced to a number. A goal such as “feel less overwhelmed when discussing family conflict” may be explored through examples and the client's own rating of change. The counsellor checks that goals belong to the client rather than being imposed by a parent, employer, school or referring agency.

15. Case formulation and counselling plans

A counselling formulation is a concise, revisable account of how the client's difficulties may have developed and what currently maintains or relieves them. It can draw on presenting problems, precipitating events, learning history, relationships, beliefs, bodily states, culture, resources and structural conditions. The formulation links assessment to intervention: if avoidance maintains fear, gradual supported practice may be relevant; if practical insecurity drives distress, social or financial referral may be central. The formulation should be understandable to the client and open to correction.

A counselling plan identifies priorities, chosen approach, responsibilities, review arrangements and possible referral needs. It should be proportionate to the concern and the counsellor's competence. Plans need flexibility: a new risk, physical illness, bereavement or change in housing may require a different focus. The practitioner documents the rationale for significant decisions and avoids presenting hypotheses as established facts. A good plan is not a rigid script; it is a shared guide that supports purposeful work.

16. Working with anxiety, stress and panic

Counselling for anxiety begins by understanding triggers, thoughts, bodily sensations, avoidance, safety behaviours and functional impact. Stress may arise from workload, caregiving, conflict, illness, discrimination or uncertainty. Psychoeducation can explain the body's threat response without implying that symptoms are imaginary. Helpful strategies may include problem-solving, paced breathing where appropriate, grounding, attention skills, cognitive methods and gradual behavioural experiments. Persistent or severe symptoms may require specialist assessment and evidence-based treatment.

Panic experiences can feel frightening because bodily sensations are interpreted as signs of catastrophe. A counsellor explores medical considerations and does not assume that chest pain, fainting or breathing difficulty is psychological. When panic disorder is suspected, referral to a qualified clinician may be appropriate for assessment and structured CBT. Counselling should not promise instant control. The focus is on understanding patterns, increasing confidence and reducing the impact of anxiety on valued activities.

17. Depression, grief and adjustment difficulties

Counselling for low mood explores emotional experience, sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, motivation, relationships, physical health, substance use and safety. Grief is a varied response to loss and does not follow a single timetable. The counsellor avoids pathologising expected grief while recognising when distress is persistent, disabling or accompanied by significant risk. Adjustment difficulties may follow transitions such as separation, migration, unemployment, illness or academic failure. Support may combine emotional processing, practical problem-solving and reconnection with meaningful routines.

Where depression is severe, recurrent, accompanied by psychotic symptoms or associated with suicide risk, the counsellor should facilitate appropriate clinical assessment and coordinated care. Counselling is not a substitute for emergency intervention when immediate danger exists. The practitioner should discuss options respectfully and avoid suggesting that a person can recover through willpower alone. Progress may involve small changes in functioning and support, not simply the absence of sadness.

18. Trauma-informed counselling and boundaries of trauma work

Trauma-informed counselling recognises the possible effects of overwhelming experiences on safety, trust, memory, relationships and emotion regulation. It emphasises choice, collaboration, predictability, empowerment and cultural sensitivity. The counsellor does not pressure a client to disclose details or assume that retelling a traumatic event is always helpful. Initial work may focus on stabilisation, present safety, coping and practical support. A trauma history should be explored only when relevant and with attention to the client's readiness and consent.

Specialist trauma processing requires appropriate training, supervision and service support. Counsellors outside that scope should provide safe supportive work and refer when the client's needs exceed their competence. The practitioner should recognise dissociation, severe destabilisation, ongoing abuse and other factors that may require specialist assessment. Trauma-informed care is not a diagnosis and does not mean interpreting every difficulty as trauma-related. It is a way of reducing avoidable harm and respecting the effects of adversity.

19. Relationship, family and group counselling

Relationship counselling explores interaction patterns, communication, expectations, conflict, intimacy, trust and shared decisions. The counsellor clarifies whether the work is individual, couple or family-based and explains how confidentiality will operate when more than one person participates. Joint work is not automatically appropriate where there is coercive control, intimidation, violence or fear. Safety assessment should precede decisions about format. Each participant should have a fair opportunity to speak, while the counsellor avoids taking sides or assuming equal power where it does not exist.

Group counselling can reduce isolation, provide feedback and allow members to practise interpersonal skills. It requires clear selection, preparation, group agreements, management of conflict and attention to confidentiality. Unlike individual confidentiality, a counsellor cannot guarantee that every group member will keep information private. The limits should be explained before participation. Facilitators monitor inclusion, cultural dynamics, power differences and the risk that one member's disclosure may overwhelm or harm another.

20. Counselling children, adolescents and families

Counselling with children and adolescents requires developmental knowledge, age-appropriate communication and careful attention to consent, assent, parental responsibility and safeguarding law. The practitioner explains the service in language the young person can understand and clarifies what information may be shared with caregivers. A child should not be promised absolute secrecy. The counsellor considers school, family, peer relationships, online experiences, development, disability and physical health. Parents or caregivers may provide useful context, but the young person's perspective must not be displaced.

Family involvement can support change when it is safe and appropriate. The counsellor clarifies roles, avoids making the child responsible for adult conflict and assesses possible abuse or coercion. Where a young person is at risk, local safeguarding procedures take priority. Practitioners must understand the legal age and conditions for consent in their jurisdiction rather than assuming one universal rule. Consultation and supervision are particularly important in complex cases.

21. Culture, identity, faith and social justice

Culture shapes language, emotional expression, family roles, help-seeking, privacy and understandings of wellbeing. Counsellors practise cultural humility by asking rather than assuming, reflecting on their own position and being open to correction. Identity may include ethnicity, nationality, gender, disability, age, faith, class, migration history and other experiences, but no single category determines an individual's beliefs. The counsellor explores which identities and communities matter to the client and how they affect the concern.

Social justice is relevant because distress can be produced or maintained by poverty, discrimination, violence, exclusion and unequal access to services. Counselling should not redefine structural problems as individual weakness. The practitioner may help clients identify rights, resources and advocacy pathways while maintaining professional boundaries. Interpreters should be competent and impartial; family members should not routinely interpret sensitive sessions. The counsellor checks whether concepts and measures have equivalent meanings across languages and cultural settings.

22. Disability, neurodiversity and accessible practice

Accessible counselling recognises that people differ in sensory processing, communication, mobility, cognition, attention and energy. Reasonable adjustments may include step-free access, flexible session length, plain-language materials, written summaries, visual supports, captions, breaks or alternative communication methods. Neurodiversity-informed practice avoids treating every difference as a deficit and asks what creates distress or barriers in the person's environment. The client should be involved in deciding which adjustments are useful.

Capacity should not be inferred from diagnosis or disability. Practitioners support decision-making and follow relevant legal frameworks where capacity is genuinely in question. Counsellors should not attempt interventions beyond their training, and should coordinate with specialist services when necessary. Accessibility is not an optional courtesy; it is part of equitable professional practice. A service that cannot be accessed by a client may fail even if its theoretical intervention is sound.

23. Confidentiality, records and information sharing

Confidentiality supports trust and encourages honest discussion. At the outset, the counsellor explains what is recorded, who may access records, how information is stored, how long it is retained and the circumstances in which disclosure may be required. Records should be accurate, relevant, timely and respectful. They should distinguish the client's statements, the counsellor's observations and professional hypotheses. Sensitive details that are not necessary for care should not be collected merely out of curiosity.

Information sharing should be based on informed consent wherever possible and limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose. Exceptions may arise under law or where serious risk or safeguarding duties apply. The counsellor should consult policy, supervisors or legal/ethics advisers as appropriate and document the rationale. Digital records require access controls, secure devices and careful handling of messages, email and cloud services. Personal devices and informal messaging can create serious privacy risks.

24. Boundaries, dual relationships and professional conduct

Professional boundaries define the role, purpose and limits of the counselling relationship. Potential boundary concerns include social contact, gifts, financial arrangements, physical contact, personal disclosure, online connections and relationships outside the service. A dual relationship exists when the practitioner has another significant role with the client, such as employer, teacher, family friend or business partner. Some contexts make overlap difficult to avoid, particularly in small communities, but the counsellor must assess risks, power and alternatives carefully.

The key questions are whether the arrangement could impair objectivity, exploit the client, confuse roles, compromise confidentiality or create pressure. The counsellor discusses the issue, seeks supervision and documents decisions. Sexual or exploitative relationships with clients are incompatible with professional care and are prohibited by many professional codes. Practitioners should communicate respectfully, avoid discriminatory language and respond appropriately to complaints. Professional conduct continues outside the session, including online and in community settings.

25. Supervision, self-care and practitioner wellbeing

Supervision is a structured professional relationship that supports client welfare, reflective practice, ethical reasoning and skill development. It may include case discussion, review of recordings where consented, examination of the counsellor's emotional responses and planning for complex situations. Supervision should be regular, appropriately documented and provided by someone with relevant competence. It is not merely administrative reporting and does not remove the counsellor's responsibility for decisions. Practitioners should know when to seek urgent consultation.

Counselling work can involve exposure to distress, trauma narratives, conflict and high-stakes decisions. Self-care includes workload management, rest, peer support, reflective practice and access to personal support when needed. It is not a substitute for safe staffing or organisational responsibility. Signs of impairment, burnout or secondary traumatic stress should be taken seriously. A counsellor who cannot practise safely should seek support, adjust workload or pause practice in accordance with professional obligations.

26. Crisis response, suicide concerns and referral

When a client expresses suicidal thoughts, self-harm, intent to harm another person or inability to remain safe, the counsellor responds directly and calmly. Assessment explores immediacy, intent, planning, access to means, previous behaviour, current stressors, intoxication, protective factors and available support. The practitioner does not rely on a promise or contract as proof of safety. Risk is dynamic and should be reassessed when circumstances change. Concerns about abuse, neglect, exploitation or violence require safeguarding action under local law and policy.

If danger is immediate, connect the person with local emergency services, an emergency department or an appropriate crisis service; do not leave them relying solely on a routine counselling appointment. A collaborative safety plan may list warning signs, coping steps, trusted people, professional contacts and ways to reduce access to lethal means. The counsellor explains confidentiality limits and documents actions. Referral should be warm and practical: with consent, help the client understand where to go, what to expect and how continuity will be maintained.

27. Online counselling and digital professionalism

Online counselling may improve access but changes how privacy, communication and risk are managed. Before beginning, the counsellor checks identity, current location, emergency contacts, privacy, technology, accessibility and whether the presenting concern is suitable for remote work. A plan is needed for disconnection, technical failure and emergencies. The client should know whether sessions are recorded, how data are handled and what communication between sessions is appropriate. The counsellor should avoid using unapproved platforms or personal accounts that expose confidential information.

Digital professionalism includes secure passwords, device protection, careful use of shared computers and clear boundaries on social media. Text-based counselling can remove visual cues and create ambiguity about tone or urgency. Practitioners should agree response times and explain that messaging is not an emergency service. Digital self-help tools vary in evidence and privacy. Counsellors should review suitability, accessibility, data practices and escalation arrangements rather than recommending an app solely because it is popular.

28. Evaluation, endings and continuing professional development

Counselling evaluation asks whether the work is meeting the client's goals and whether the approach remains suitable. Progress may be monitored through discussion, goal review, brief measures and observation of changes in daily functioning. Measures should support, not replace, conversation. If there is little progress, the counsellor explores barriers, alliance difficulties, practical stressors, diagnosis or formulation questions, and whether another intervention or referral is needed. Endings should be planned, discussed and reviewed rather than imposed unexpectedly except where safety or service rules require a different process.

A planned ending summarises learning, strategies, remaining needs, relapse or setback plans and routes to further support. The counsellor acknowledges the meaning of ending and invites feedback. Continuing professional development includes reading research, attending training, supervision, reflective learning and understanding changes in law and professional standards. Competence is maintained through evidence-informed learning and honest recognition of limitations. Practitioners should not claim specialist expertise based solely on brief exposure to a technique.

Structured university examination questions and model answers

For essay questions, define the concept, explain its purpose, apply it to a realistic example and evaluate limitations or ethical considerations.

1. Define counselling and state four purposes.

Model answer: Counselling is a structured professional helping relationship. Purposes may include exploring concerns, developing coping, improving relationships, supporting decisions, reducing distress and promoting personal development.

2. Distinguish counselling from coaching.

Model answer: Counselling may address emotional distress, relationships and psychological difficulties within a professional helping framework. Coaching commonly focuses on goals and performance; scope and regulation vary.

3. Explain the principle of autonomy.

Model answer: Autonomy requires respecting a client's right to make informed choices, including the right to decline or discontinue counselling, subject to relevant law and immediate safety duties.

4. Describe Rogers' three core conditions.

Model answer: Congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding provide a facilitative relational climate in person-centred counselling.

5. What is a therapeutic alliance?

Model answer: A collaborative relationship involving agreement on goals, agreement on tasks and a trusting professional bond.

6. Explain functional analysis.

Model answer: It examines antecedents, a target behaviour and its consequences to understand what maintains a pattern and identify appropriate intervention points.

7. Give two cognitive counselling techniques.

Model answer: Guided discovery and thought records; other examples include behavioural experiments, problem-solving and identifying unhelpful assumptions.

8. What is solution-focused counselling?

Model answer: An approach that explores preferred futures, exceptions, strengths and small achievable steps rather than concentrating exclusively on problem origins.

9. State the four common communication skills in motivational interviewing.

Model answer: Open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries (often abbreviated OARS).

10. Differentiate empathy from sympathy.

Model answer: Empathy seeks to understand and communicate the client's perspective; sympathy expresses concern or sorrow, and may or may not demonstrate accurate understanding.

11. Why is informed consent ongoing?

Model answer: Understanding and circumstances can change. Clients need continuing opportunities to ask questions, revise choices and understand risks, boundaries and confidentiality limits.

12. What makes a counselling goal useful?

Model answer: It is meaningful to the client, realistic, collaboratively agreed and sufficiently clear to review, while allowing for outcomes that are not easily quantified.

13. Define case formulation.

Model answer: A revisable explanation of how relevant experiences, processes and contexts may contribute to and maintain a client's difficulties, linked to possible intervention.

14. How should a counsellor approach grief?

Model answer: Respect individual and cultural variation, avoid imposing a timetable, assess functioning and safety, and identify when specialist assessment may be needed.

15. State two principles of trauma-informed counselling.

Model answer: Prioritise safety and choice; avoid coercive disclosure and work collaboratively at a pace appropriate to the client's readiness and competence of the practitioner.

16. Why may couple counselling be unsuitable in coercive-control situations?

Model answer: Joint sessions can increase danger, enable intimidation or create pressure to disclose. Individual safety assessment and specialist safeguarding support may be necessary.

17. What are two considerations when counselling minors?

Model answer: Developmentally appropriate assent and consent arrangements, and clear explanation of confidentiality limits and safeguarding duties under local law.

18. Explain cultural humility.

Model answer: Ongoing self-reflection, curiosity, openness to correction and attention to power and context, without assuming that group membership determines an individual's beliefs.

19. Give three accessibility adjustments in counselling.

Model answer: Examples include captions, plain-language information, flexible pacing, step-free access, visual supports, breaks and alternative communication formats.

20. When can confidentiality be limited?

Model answer: Where the client consents, or where applicable law or serious safety and safeguarding duties require proportionate information sharing.

21. What is a dual relationship?

Model answer: A relationship in which the counsellor and client also have another significant role, potentially creating conflicts, exploitation or confusion of boundaries.

22. What is the purpose of supervision?

Model answer: To support client welfare, reflective learning, ethical reasoning, competence and safe management of complex cases.

23. Why is a safety plan not a substitute for emergency care?

Model answer: A plan supports coping and connection to help but cannot manage imminent danger alone; immediate risk requires urgent local emergency or crisis intervention.

24. Identify two requirements for safe online counselling.

Model answer: Check privacy and suitability, confirm the client's location and emergency arrangements, use secure platforms and agree a disconnection plan.

25. How should lack of progress be handled?

Model answer: Review goals, barriers, formulation, alliance, intervention fit and risk; seek supervision and consider adaptation, specialist referral or a different service.

26. What should a planned ending include?

Model answer: Review of progress, learning and remaining needs; a plan for setbacks; referral or re-entry information; and space for feedback about the counselling relationship.

27. Why is counsellor self-care an ethical issue?

Model answer: Practitioner impairment can affect judgement and client welfare. Counsellors must monitor fitness to practise, seek support and adjust work when safety is affected.

28. Explain the difference between validation and agreement.

Model answer: Validation recognises that an emotion is understandable in context; agreement endorses a particular interpretation or action. A counsellor can validate feelings while exploring facts and choices.

Chapter synthesis and revision guide

Competent counselling combines a clear professional contract, a collaborative alliance, purposeful communication, a defensible formulation and ongoing review. The counsellor selects an approach that fits the client's priorities and the practitioner's competence. Person-centred, psychodynamic, behavioural, cognitive, solution-focused and motivational approaches offer different tools; none should be applied without considering evidence, culture, context and safety.

Ethical practice is active and continuous. Confidentiality, consent, boundaries, competence, supervision, safeguarding and accurate records must be considered throughout the relationship. When needs exceed the counsellor's scope, referral is a sign of responsible practice. Crisis concerns require direct assessment and appropriate local emergency support rather than reliance on routine counselling alone.

Revision sequence: scope and history → ethical principles → alliance and theories → listening and communication → assessment and formulation → work with different client groups → confidentiality and boundaries → supervision → crisis response → evaluation and endings.
