1. Transition-metal position and scope

Transition metals occupy the d block, but the strict IUPAC definition concerns elements whose atoms have an incomplete d subshell or whose cations form such a subshell. This distinction matters for group 12, whose common ions are d10. Their chemistry is governed by accessible oxidation states, d-electron configurations, ligand interactions, covalency, geometry and redox energetics.

Analytical approach

For a rigorous interpretation, define the metal oxidation state, d-electron count, ligand donor atoms, coordination number and geometry before drawing conclusions. 2. d-Orbital splitting in ligand fields

In a free gaseous ion, the five d orbitals are degenerate in the simplest model. A surrounding ligand field removes that degeneracy. In an octahedral field, the eg set is raised relative to the t2g set; in a tetrahedral field the ordering reverses and the splitting is smaller. The magnitude depends on metal identity, oxidation state and ligand donor/acceptor properties.

3. Crystal-field stabilisation energy

Crystal-field stabilisation energy is calculated by summing the occupancy of each d-orbital set multiplied by its energy relative to the barycentre. For octahedral complexes, each t2g electron contributes −0.4Δo and each eg electron +0.6Δo. Pairing energy must be considered separately when comparing high-spin and low-spin configurations.

4. Spectrochemical series

Ligands produce different splitting magnitudes. The spectrochemical series is an empirical ordering, often placing halides toward weak-field behaviour and ligands such as CN− and CO toward strong-field behaviour. It is not a universal immutable ranking: metal oxidation state, covalency, geometry and solvent can affect observed spectra and spin states.

5. High-spin and low-spin complexes

When Δo is smaller than the energetic cost of pairing, electrons occupy eg orbitals before pairing and a high-spin arrangement results. When Δo is sufficiently large, electrons pair in t2g and a low-spin state may occur. This comparison is especially important for octahedral d4–d7 ions; tetrahedral complexes are usually high spin.

6. Magnetic properties and spin-only moments

Unpaired electrons produce paramagnetism; species with all electrons paired are diamagnetic. The spin-only magnetic moment is commonly estimated as μ = √[n(n+2)] Bohr magnetons, where n is the number of unpaired electrons. Orbital contributions, spin–orbit coupling, temperature and magnetic exchange can cause deviations.

7. Electronic spectra and d–d transitions

Absorption of visible or ultraviolet light can promote electrons between ligand-field-split d levels. The energy relates to wavelength through ΔE = hc/λ. d–d transitions may be weak because of Laporte selection rules in centrosymmetric complexes, while vibronic coupling and symmetry changes can relax the restriction. Charge-transfer bands are often much more intense.

8. Jahn–Teller distortions

An electronically degenerate ground state can distort to remove degeneracy and lower energy. Octahedral d9 and high-spin d4 configurations often show strong distortions, commonly elongation or compression along one axis. Distortion affects bond lengths, spectra, magnetic behaviour and reactivity; actual structures depend on lattice and ligand constraints.

9. Coordination number and geometry

Common coordination numbers include four, five and six. Four-coordinate complexes may be tetrahedral or square planar; d8 ions such as Pt(II) often favour square-planar geometry. Six-coordinate complexes are frequently octahedral, while five-coordinate species may be trigonal-bipyramidal or square-pyramidal. Steric and electronic factors jointly determine geometry.

Coordination compounds exhibit structural isomerism, including linkage, ionisation, hydrate and coordination isomerism, and stereoisomerism such as cis–trans, fac–mer and optical isomerism. 11. Chelate effect and macrocyclic ligands

Polydentate ligands bind through multiple donor atoms and form chelate rings. Chelation is often favoured entropically because replacing several monodentate ligands can release more particles into solution. Macrocyclic ligands can show additional preorganisation and kinetic inertness. Stability constants quantify equilibria but depend on solvent, pH and ionic strength.

13. Hard–soft acid–base interpretation

Hard metal ions tend to prefer hard donor atoms such as oxygen and fluoride, while softer ions often bind more strongly to sulfur, phosphorus and heavier halides. HSAB is a qualitative framework for trends in complex stability and selectivity. It should be used alongside charge, size, solvation, geometry and covalency rather than as a substitute for equilibrium data.

14. Ligand substitution mechanisms

Substitution reactions replace one coordinated ligand with another. Associative pathways involve bond formation to the entering ligand before departure; dissociative pathways involve loss of a leaving ligand first; interchange pathways show coupled bond-making and bond-breaking. Kinetic measurements, activation parameters and pressure effects help distinguish mechanisms.

15. Kinetic inertness and lability

Thermodynamic stability describes equilibrium preference, whereas kinetic inertness describes a slow substitution rate. Many low-spin d6 Co(III) complexes are substitution-inert, while many first-row divalent aqua ions are labile. These are broad patterns with exceptions; oxidation state, spin state, geometry and ligand field all influence activation barriers.

16. Trans effect and trans influence

The trans effect is a kinetic effect in square-planar substitution, where a ligand accelerates substitution of the ligand opposite it. The trans influence is a ground-state structural weakening or lengthening of the bond trans to a ligand. Strong π-acceptors and certain σ donors can show pronounced effects. Do not conflate the kinetic and structural concepts.

17. Metal–ligand bonding and covalency

Metal–ligand bonds can involve σ donation from ligand lone pairs and π interactions in which ligands donate or accept electron density. Covalent contributions influence ligand-field splitting, bond strengths, redox potentials and spectra. Crystal-field theory treats ligands electrostatically; ligand-field and molecular-orbital models provide a more complete covalent description.

18. π-donor and π-acceptor ligands

π-donor ligands such as halides can donate filled p orbitals into suitable metal orbitals, often reducing octahedral splitting in simple comparisons. π-acceptor ligands such as CO and CN− accept metal d-electron density into empty antibonding orbitals through back-bonding, often strengthening metal-to-ligand synergy and affecting vibrational frequencies.

19. Metal carbonyls and back-bonding

In metal carbonyls, CO donates a lone pair from carbon to the metal and the metal can back-donate from filled d orbitals into CO π* orbitals. Back-bonding weakens the C–O bond and lowers its IR stretching frequency. Electron counting and geometry help rationalise stability, but formal oxidation-state assignments can be non-intuitive.

20. 18-electron rule and electron counting

Many organometallic and carbonyl complexes are stable near 18 valence electrons, corresponding to filled metal s, p and d valence orbitals in a simplified model. Count metal d electrons and ligand donations consistently using either ionic or neutral methods. The rule has important exceptions, especially for early metals, bulky ligands and square-planar d8 complexes.

21. Oxidation states and redox couples

Transition metals commonly access multiple oxidation states because ns and (n−1)d orbital energies are comparable. Redox potentials depend on solvation, ligand stabilisation, pH and coupled chemical steps. Formal oxidation states aid electron bookkeeping but do not equal measured partial charges in covalent complexes.

22. Latimer and Frost diagrams

Latimer diagrams list standard potentials between adjacent oxidation states and permit calculation of composite potentials by free-energy addition. Frost diagrams plot nE° against oxidation state and help visualise relative thermodynamic stability and disproportionation tendencies. These diagrams apply under stated standard conditions; speciation and pH can change the conclusions.

23. Nernst equation and conditional potentials

The Nernst equation relates electrode potential to reaction quotient: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q. At 25 °C, the base-10 form uses approximately 0.05916/n volts per decade. Complex formation, protonation and ionic strength alter free-ion activities, so conditional potentials are often more relevant than tabulated standard values.

24. Aqueous hydrolysis and speciation

Transition-metal ions form aqua complexes that can undergo stepwise deprotonation, hydrolysis and precipitation. The dominant species depends on pH, total metal concentration, ligand environment and ionic strength. Pourbaix diagrams combine potential and pH to map thermodynamic domains, but they do not describe reaction rates or all kinetic effects.

Structured revision questions and model answers

Answers provide marking guidance. In assessed work, show electron counts, equations, units, assumptions and the evidence supporting structural assignments.

Question 1

Calculate the d-electron count of Fe(III).

Model answer / marking guidance: Neutral Fe is [Ar]3d6 4s2. Remove 4s electrons before 3d: Fe3+ is [Ar]3d5.

Question 2

For an octahedral d6 ion, compare high-spin and low-spin configurations.

Model answer / marking guidance: High spin: t2g4eg2, with four unpaired electrons in the simple filling picture. Low spin: t2g6eg0, all paired. The outcome depends on Δo relative to pairing energy.

Question 3

Estimate the spin-only moment for three unpaired electrons.

Model answer / marking guidance: μ = √[n(n+2)] = √15 ≈ 3.87 BM. Experimental values may differ because of orbital and spin–orbit contributions.

Question 4

Explain why tetrahedral complexes are often high spin.

Model answer / marking guidance: Tetrahedral splitting is typically smaller than octahedral splitting, often insufficient to overcome electron-pairing energy, so electrons remain unpaired where possible.

Question 5

Distinguish trans effect from trans influence.

Model answer / marking guidance: The trans effect is kinetic acceleration of substitution opposite a ligand; trans influence is a ground-state structural weakening or lengthening of the bond trans to it.

Question 6

Why does CO back-bonding lower the C–O stretching frequency?

Model answer / marking guidance: Metal-to-CO π back-donation populates CO antibonding π* orbitals, reducing C–O bond order and force constant, so the IR stretching frequency decreases.

Question 7

State the 18-electron rule and one limitation.

Model answer / marking guidance: Many stable organometallic complexes have 18 valence electrons, but square-planar d8 complexes often have 16 and early-metal or bulky-ligand systems can also deviate.

Question 8

What is the chelate effect?

Model answer / marking guidance: Multidentate ligands often form more stable complexes than comparable monodentate ligands, frequently because ligand substitution increases entropy and may benefit from preorganisation.

Question 9

Explain Jahn–Teller distortion in an octahedral d9 complex.

Model answer / marking guidance: Unequal occupancy of degenerate eg orbitals creates an electronic degeneracy; distortion, commonly tetragonal elongation, removes degeneracy and lowers energy.

Question 10

How does the Nernst equation account for concentration?

Model answer / marking guidance: E = E° − (RT/nF)lnQ. Changing activities changes Q and therefore the electrode potential from its standard value.

Question 11

What does a Frost diagram help predict?

Model answer / marking guidance: It compares relative thermodynamic stability of oxidation states and can indicate tendencies toward disproportionation or comproportionation under the specified conditions.

Question 12

Why can a thermodynamically stable complex be substitution-labile?

Model answer / marking guidance: Thermodynamic stability concerns equilibrium; substitution rate depends on activation barriers. A favourable equilibrium does not guarantee a slow reaction.

Question 13

Describe associative substitution.

Model answer / marking guidance: The entering ligand begins bonding before the leaving ligand departs, often producing a higher-coordinate transition state or intermediate.

Question 14

What is ligand-field stabilisation energy for octahedral d3?

Model answer / marking guidance: All three electrons occupy t2g: CFSE = 3(−0.4Δo) = −1.2Δo, excluding pairing contributions.

Question 15

Why are many square-planar complexes d8?

Model answer / marking guidance: The square-planar splitting pattern can strongly stabilise a low-spin d8 configuration, particularly for 4d and 5d metals such as Pd(II) and Pt(II).

Question 16

What does EPR detect?

Model answer / marking guidance: Electron paramagnetic resonance detects transitions between electron-spin states of species with unpaired electrons, providing information about spin and local environment.

Question 17

How does XANES differ from EXAFS?

Model answer / marking guidance: XANES near the absorption edge is sensitive to oxidation state and geometry; EXAFS oscillations beyond the edge provide local neighbour distances and coordination information.

Question 18

Why are standard potentials insufficient for complex solutions?

Model answer / marking guidance: Ligand binding, protonation, hydrolysis and ionic strength alter free-ion activities and therefore conditional redox potentials.

Question 19

Outline a typical Pd-catalysed cross-coupling cycle.

Model answer / marking guidance: A common cycle includes oxidative addition of an organic halide, transmetalation with an organometallic partner, and reductive elimination to form the new bond and regenerate the catalyst.

Question 20

What is β-hydride elimination?

Model answer / marking guidance: A metal-bound alkyl transfers a β hydrogen to the metal, forming a metal hydride and an alkene; the geometry and an available coordination site are required.

Question 21

Explain the role of the protein environment in metalloproteins.

Model answer / marking guidance: Protein residues, electrostatics, hydrogen bonds and solvent access tune metal redox potentials, ligand binding, intermediate stability and catalytic selectivity.

Question 22

Compare haem and iron–sulfur centres.

Model answer / marking guidance: Haem iron is held in a porphyrin macrocycle with axial ligands; iron–sulfur centres use sulfide and cysteine or other ligands in clusters for electron transfer and catalysis.

Question 23

What is metal-to-ligand charge transfer?

Model answer / marking guidance: An electronic excitation transfers electron density from metal-based orbitals to ligand-based orbitals, often producing intense absorption bands.

Question 24

Why can d–d bands be weak?

Model answer / marking guidance: In centrosymmetric complexes, d–d transitions are Laporte-forbidden in the electric-dipole approximation, though vibronic coupling and symmetry breaking give weak intensity.

Question 25

Define formation constant β2 for ML2.

Model answer / marking guidance: For M + 2L ⇌ ML2, β2 = [ML2]/([M][L]^2) in a concentration-based convention; rigorous thermodynamic constants use activities.

Question 26

Explain hard–soft preference for Hg(II) and oxygen donors.

Model answer / marking guidance: Hg(II) is relatively soft and often forms stronger interactions with soft sulfur or phosphorus donors than with hard oxygen donors, though full speciation and solvent effects matter.

Question 27

What does cyclic voltammetry reveal?

Model answer / marking guidance: It can reveal redox potentials, reversibility, electron-transfer kinetics and coupled chemical reactions from current–potential response, subject to electrode and solution conditions.

Question 28

Why is calibration of a reference electrode important?

Model answer / marking guidance: Potential scales can drift or differ between setups; calibration against a known standard improves comparability and correct interpretation of measured redox potentials.

Question 29

What does the trans influence affect structurally?

Model answer / marking guidance: It can lengthen or weaken a bond opposite a ligand in the ground-state coordination structure, often assessed by crystallographic bond distances.

Question 30

Explain oxidative addition formally.

Model answer / marking guidance: A substrate bond adds across a metal centre; the formal metal oxidation state and coordination number commonly each increase by two units.

Question 31

Why do catalysts not change equilibrium constants?

Model answer / marking guidance: A catalyst lowers activation barriers for forward and reverse pathways but does not alter the Gibbs energy difference between reactants and products.

Question 32

Distinguish homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis.

Model answer / marking guidance: Homogeneous catalysis occurs in the same phase as substrates; heterogeneous catalysis occurs at a separate phase, often a solid surface. They differ in separation, mass transfer and mechanistic accessibility.

Question 33

What is turnover frequency?

Model answer / marking guidance: Turnover frequency is the number of catalytic cycles per active site per unit time under defined conditions; it should be reported with measurement context.

Question 34

How does ligand steric bulk influence catalysis?

Model answer / marking guidance: Steric bulk can favour particular coordination numbers, accelerate ligand dissociation, control substrate approach and alter regio- or enantioselectivity.

Question 35

Why are 4d and 5d complexes often low spin?

Model answer / marking guidance: Their more spatially extended d orbitals and metal–ligand interactions often produce larger ligand-field splitting, making electron pairing more favourable.

Question 36

What causes spin crossover?

Model answer / marking guidance: When high-spin and low-spin states have similar energies, temperature, pressure, light or host-lattice effects can shift their relative populations.

Question 37

What is a conditional formation constant?

Model answer / marking guidance: It is an effective stability constant accounting for side reactions such as ligand protonation or metal hydrolysis at a specified pH and solution composition.

Question 38

How can Mössbauer spectroscopy help study iron?

Model answer / marking guidance: Isomer shift, quadrupole splitting and magnetic hyperfine features provide information about iron oxidation state, spin state and local symmetry.

Question 39

Why combine spectroscopy with crystallography?

Model answer / marking guidance: Diffraction provides solid-state structure, while spectroscopy probes electronic states, bonding and sometimes solution or time-dependent species; together they give stronger structural assignments.

Question 40

Name two factors that affect metal complex colour.

Model answer / marking guidance: Ligand-field splitting and charge-transfer transitions determine absorbed wavelengths; geometry, oxidation state and ligand identity also affect the spectrum.

Question 41

What is a Pourbaix diagram?

Model answer / marking guidance: A potential–pH diagram showing thermodynamically predominant species or stability domains as a function of electrode potential and pH.

Question 42

Explain metal–ligand π back-donation.

Model answer / marking guidance: Filled metal d orbitals donate electron density into empty ligand π* orbitals, strengthening metal–ligand synergy and weakening the ligand internal bond.

Question 43

Why can nickel be catalytically versatile?

Model answer / marking guidance: Nickel accesses multiple oxidation states and can form organometallic intermediates, enabling oxidative addition, insertion and reductive elimination in suitable ligand environments.

Question 44

What is the S-state cycle in photosystem II?

Model answer / marking guidance: It is a sequence of oxidation states of the Mn–Ca oxygen-evolving cluster that accumulates four oxidising equivalents before releasing O2 from water.

Question 45

How does chelation influence metal bioavailability?

Model answer / marking guidance: Ligands can reduce free-metal activity, alter solubility and transport, or promote uptake depending on complex stability, ligand properties and biological barriers.

Question 46

List essential controls for air-sensitive synthesis.

Model answer / marking guidance: Use dry reagents and apparatus, inert atmosphere, leak-tested connections, controlled additions, appropriate quenching, and validated analytical checks.

Question 47

What is catalyst deactivation?

Model answer / marking guidance: Loss of catalytic activity through poisoning, sintering, aggregation, ligand degradation, leaching or irreversible formation of inactive species.

Question 48

Why is life-cycle assessment relevant to catalyst choice?

Model answer / marking guidance: It accounts for metal mining, manufacture, energy, solvent and reagent use, recovery, toxicity and end-of-life impacts rather than judging only reaction yield.

Question 49

Explain spin–orbit coupling.

Model answer / marking guidance: It is the interaction between electron spin and orbital angular momentum; it can split energy levels and alter magnetic and spectroscopic properties, especially for heavier elements.

Question 50

What makes a good mechanistic proposal?

Model answer / marking guidance: It identifies plausible intermediates and elementary steps, balances atoms and charge, explains kinetic and spectroscopic evidence, and offers testable predictions.

Chapter synthesis

Transition-metal chemistry links d-electron structure to coordination geometry, ligand-field splitting, magnetism, spectroscopy, substitution kinetics and redox chemistry. Covalent metal–ligand interactions and ligand architecture explain why simple electrostatic models sometimes fail. The same principles underpin organometallic catalysis and biological metal centres. Reliable interpretation requires complementary measurements, explicit solution conditions and careful distinction between thermodynamic stability and kinetic persistence.

Advanced Coordination and Inorganic Chemistry: Theory, Derivations and Laboratory Applications

This extension develops the quantitative and experimental reasoning behind ligand-field and coordination chemistry. Treat every structure as a model to be tested against composition, magnetic data, spectroscopy, equilibrium measurements and reaction kinetics.

Expanded theoretical treatment

1. Ligand-field analysis as a quantitative model

For an octahedral d-metal ion, the five d orbitals divide into a lower-energy t₂g set and a higher-energy e_g set. The separation is Δₒ. Relative to the barycentre, each t₂g electron contributes −0.4Δₒ and each e_g electron contributes +0.6Δₒ. Thus CFSE = (−0.4n(t₂g)+0.6n(e_g))Δₒ, before pairing-energy terms are included. A reliable assignment must state oxidation state, d-electron count, geometry, ligand strength and whether pairing is energetically favoured. Do not infer spin state from ligand identity alone when geometry or oxidation state is uncertain.

2. Thermodynamic stability is not kinetic inertness

The overall formation constant β describes the equilibrium preference for complex formation, whereas substitution rates describe how rapidly a complex reaches a new coordination environment. A thermodynamically stable complex may be kinetically labile, and a metastable complex may be inert. Compare ΔG° = −RT ln β with activation free energy ΔG‡ obtained from rate measurements; they answer different questions.

3. Electronic spectra and selection rules

For a d–d transition, the photon energy is related to the wavenumber by ΔE = hcṽ. In practical spectroscopy, energy in kJ mol⁻¹ is 0.01196 × ṽ when ṽ is in cm⁻¹. Octahedral centrosymmetric complexes often show weak d–d bands because Laporte selection rules restrict transitions that do not change parity; vibronic coupling and distortion can relax the restriction. Charge-transfer bands are often much more intense and should not be misassigned as d–d transitions.

4. Magnetism as an independent structural test

For a first approximation to a spin-only magnetic moment, μso = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons. Deviations can arise from orbital contributions, spin–orbit coupling, covalency, exchange coupling, temperature dependence and sample impurities. Magnetic susceptibility should therefore be interpreted alongside UV–visible spectra and structural data.

5. Substitution mechanisms and stereochemical evidence

Associative substitution is supported when the rate increases with entering-ligand concentration and a higher-coordinate transition state or intermediate is plausible. Dissociative substitution is supported when the rate is largely independent of entering-ligand concentration and bond cleavage dominates. A rate law alone is not a complete mechanism: use activation parameters, pressure effects, stereochemical outcomes and ligand-trapping experiments.

6. Redox diagrams and conditional potentials

Standard potentials apply to defined standard states. Complexation, pH, ionic strength and ligand protonation can shift formal or conditional potentials. When a ligand stabilises one oxidation state more strongly, the redox couple is shifted accordingly. Always identify the actual species in solution before applying a tabulated potential.

University-level derivation

For an octahedral d⁶ ion, compare low-spin t₂g⁶e_g⁰ with high-spin t₂g⁴e_g². The crystal-field contributions are −2.4Δₒ and (−1.6+1.2)Δₒ = −0.4Δₒ, respectively. The low-spin arrangement has two more electron pairs than the high-spin arrangement under the conventional pairing-count comparison, so its energy advantage is ΔE = 2Δₒ − 2P, where P is the effective pairing-energy penalty per additional pair. Low spin is favoured when Δₒ > P. This result is a model comparison; real systems can require covalency, orbital and geometry corrections.

Laboratory experiment and data interpretation

Prepare a series of Co(II) or Ni(II) coordination solutions using controlled ligand concentration and solvent composition. Record UV–visible spectra against solvent/reagent blanks, identify absorption maxima, convert wavelengths to wavenumbers, and compare the pattern with the proposed geometry. Where permitted, obtain magnetic susceptibility or use a validated magnetic-balance method. For a formation-equilibrium experiment, titrate metal ion with ligand while maintaining pH and ionic strength, fit the absorbance or potentiometric response to an appropriate speciation model, and report uncertainty. Include waste segregation for transition-metal solutions and avoid disposal to the drain.

Challenging structured examination questions with model answers

Question 1

A d⁶ ion forms an octahedral complex with Δₒ = 18,000 cm⁻¹ and an effective pairing energy P = 20,000 cm⁻¹. Use the simple model to predict the preferred spin state and justify your answer.

Model answer

Since Δₒ < P, the pairing penalty exceeds the additional ligand-field stabilisation in the simplified comparison. The high-spin arrangement is predicted. State that this is a first-order model and that actual spin state should be checked experimentally.

Question 2

A complex has three unpaired electrons. Calculate its spin-only magnetic moment.

Model answer

μso = √[3(3+2)] = √15 = 3.87 BM. Experimental deviations should be discussed in terms of orbital contribution, spin–orbit coupling and exchange.

Question 3

An absorption maximum occurs at 520 nm. Calculate its wavenumber and molar transition energy.

Model answer

ṽ = 1/(520 × 10⁻⁷ cm) = 19,231 cm⁻¹. Energy = 0.01196 × 19,231 = 230 kJ mol⁻¹ (approximately).

Question 4

Explain why a large formation constant does not prove that ligand substitution is slow.

Model answer

β is an equilibrium constant and determines standard free-energy change. Substitution rate depends on the activation free energy and pathway. Thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness are distinct.

Question 5

A substitution reaction rate doubles when entering-ligand concentration doubles, with other variables fixed. What mechanistic clue does this provide?

Model answer

It suggests that the entering ligand participates in or before the rate-determining step, consistent with an associative contribution. It is not definitive proof; pressure, activation parameters and stereochemical evidence are needed.
