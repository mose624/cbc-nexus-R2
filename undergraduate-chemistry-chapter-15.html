1. Natural products: scope, history and significance

Natural products are chemical substances produced by living organisms, including plants, microorganisms, animals and marine species. They include primary metabolites required for growth and secondary or specialised metabolites that provide ecological advantages such as defence, signalling, competition and attraction. Natural products have shaped the development of organic chemistry, pharmacology, agriculture and biotechnology. Their structural diversity arises from enzyme-controlled biosynthetic pathways, selective oxidation, cyclisation, rearrangement and coupling reactions. In medicinal chemistry, natural products may serve as drugs, lead structures, pharmacological probes or inspiration for simplified synthetic analogues.

2. Primary and secondary metabolism

Primary metabolism comprises pathways essential for energy production, biosynthesis of macromolecular precursors and maintenance of cellular function. Examples include glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, amino-acid biosynthesis and nucleotide metabolism. Secondary metabolism, more accurately called specialised metabolism, produces compounds that are not universally essential for basic growth but can improve survival in particular ecological contexts. The boundary is not absolute: specialised metabolites may influence development, microbiome interactions and stress responses, while intermediates from primary metabolism feed directly into specialised pathways.

3. Biosynthetic logic and precursor relationships

Natural-product biosynthesis can be understood by identifying a small number of recurring building blocks: acetate and malonate units, amino acids, isoprene units, shikimate-derived aromatic precursors and sugars. Enzymes assemble these units through condensation, cyclisation, oxidation, reduction, methylation, glycosylation and other transformations. Retrosynthetic analysis of a natural product often reveals these biosynthetic fragments. Recognising precursor patterns helps predict plausible pathways and understand why related organisms produce families of structurally related metabolites.

4. Polyketide biosynthesis

Polyketides are assembled from acyl-CoA building blocks, commonly acetyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, by polyketide synthases. Decarboxylative Claisen condensations extend the growing carbon chain. Subsequent reduction, dehydration, cyclisation and aromatisation determine oxidation state and ring architecture. Type I, II and III polyketide synthases differ in organisation and catalytic strategy. Polyketides include antibiotics, immunosuppressants, anticancer agents and toxins, making their biosynthesis a major target for pathway engineering.

5. Fatty-acid synthases and polyketide synthases compared

Fatty-acid synthases and polyketide synthases share iterative carbon–carbon bond-forming chemistry but differ in programming and product outcomes. Fatty-acid synthases generally produce relatively regular, highly reduced chains, whereas polyketide synthases can vary the extent of reduction and introduce diverse cyclisation patterns. Modular polyketide synthases contain domains that act in sequence, while iterative systems reuse catalytic domains. Domain organisation and substrate specificity influence chain length, stereochemistry and functional-group placement.

6. Nonribosomal peptide biosynthesis

Nonribosomal peptides are assembled by large enzyme systems called nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs). Adenylation domains select and activate amino acids, thiolation domains carry intermediates on phosphopantetheine arms, and condensation domains form peptide bonds. NRPS systems can incorporate D-amino acids, N-methylated residues, hydroxy acids and unusual building blocks not normally used in ribosomal proteins. Their products include antibiotics, siderophores, immunosuppressants and toxins.

7. Ribosomally synthesised and post-translationally modified peptides

RiPPs are produced from ribosomally synthesised precursor peptides that undergo enzyme-mediated post-translational modifications. The precursor often contains a leader region recognised by tailoring enzymes and a core region transformed into the mature natural product. Modifications may include dehydration, cyclisation, thioether formation, glycosylation and oxidation. RiPP biosynthesis combines the genetic programmability of ribosomal synthesis with the structural diversity of enzymatic tailoring.

8. Terpenoids and isoprene biosynthesis

Terpenoids are constructed from five-carbon isoprene-derived units. The two principal biosynthetic routes to isopentenyl diphosphate and dimethylallyl diphosphate are the mevalonate pathway and the methylerythritol phosphate pathway. Prenyltransferases join these units to form geranyl, farnesyl and geranylgeranyl diphosphates, precursors to monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, diterpenes and larger terpenoids. Cyclases and oxidising enzymes generate remarkable structural diversity.

9. Mevalonate and methylerythritol phosphate pathways

The mevalonate pathway begins with acetyl-CoA condensation to form HMG-CoA, followed by reduction to mevalonate and phosphorylation/decarboxylation steps that yield isopentenyl diphosphate. The MEP pathway begins with pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and proceeds through deoxyxylulose phosphate and methylerythritol phosphate intermediates. Different organisms distribute these pathways differently; some use both. Understanding pathway location and regulation supports metabolic engineering and antimicrobial target discovery.

10. Monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes and diterpenes

Monoterpenes contain ten carbons and commonly arise from geranyl diphosphate; sesquiterpenes contain fifteen carbons and commonly arise from farnesyl diphosphate; diterpenes contain twenty carbons and commonly arise from geranylgeranyl diphosphate. Terpene synthases generate carbocationic intermediates that undergo cyclisation, hydride shifts and rearrangements. Oxygenases, reductases and conjugating enzymes then modify the hydrocarbon skeleton, producing alcohols, ketones, epoxides and glycosides.

11. Triterpenes, steroids and sterol biosynthesis

Triterpenes are commonly formed by coupling two farnesyl diphosphate units to squalene, followed by epoxidation and cyclisation. Sterol biosynthesis transforms this linear precursor into polycyclic frameworks through highly controlled carbocationic cascades. Cholesterol and related sterols contribute to membrane organisation and serve as precursors to steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D. Plant sterols and fungal sterols have distinct biological roles and are important in nutrition and pharmacology.

12. Shikimate pathway and aromatic natural products

The shikimate pathway converts phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate into chorismate, a branch-point precursor for aromatic amino acids and many aromatic natural products. Phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan feed into phenylpropanoids, alkaloids and other specialised metabolites. The pathway is absent from animals, making some enzymes attractive antimicrobial or herbicide targets. Pathway flux depends on nutrient status, feedback regulation and competition among branches.

13. Phenylpropanoid pathway

Phenylpropanoids derive largely from phenylalanine through deamination to cinnamate, followed by hydroxylation, methylation, activation and chain modification. This pathway supplies precursors for lignin, flavonoids, coumarins, stilbenes and many plant defence compounds. Enzyme families such as cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, methyltransferases and transferases create chemical diversity. Phenylpropanoid metabolism links plant structural biology, pigmentation, UV protection and human dietary exposure.

14. Flavonoids: structural classes and biosynthesis

Flavonoids share a C6–C3–C6 carbon framework, typically formed through condensation of a hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA derivative with malonyl-CoA units. Chalcone synthase produces a chalcone intermediate that can cyclise and undergo further oxidation or reduction. Major classes include flavones, flavonols, flavanones, flavan-3-ols, anthocyanidins and isoflavonoids. Substitution pattern, glycosylation and polymerisation affect colour, solubility, stability and biological activity.

15. Anthocyanins and natural pigments

Anthocyanins are glycosylated anthocyanidins responsible for many red, purple and blue colours in flowers, fruits and leaves. Their colour depends on pH, substitution, copigmentation, metal complexation and molecular environment. The flavylium cation is prominent under acidic conditions, while hydration and deprotonation produce other forms at higher pH. These equilibria influence pigment stability in foods and biological tissues and are exploited in natural colourant research.

16. Tannins and polyphenolic chemistry

Tannins are polyphenolic compounds capable of binding proteins and other macromolecules. Hydrolysable tannins contain gallic-acid-derived units esterified to a polyol core, whereas condensed tannins are oligomers or polymers of flavan-3-ol units. Their chemistry includes oxidation, complexation, hydrolysis and polymerisation. Tannins influence astringency, plant defence, digestion and interactions with minerals and proteins; analytical characterisation is challenging because natural mixtures are heterogeneous.

17. Coumarins and related phenylpropanoid lactones

Coumarins are benzopyrone natural products commonly formed through phenylpropanoid pathways and intramolecular lactonisation. Substitution and oxidation create simple coumarins, furanocoumarins and pyranocoumarins. Some interact with enzymes involved in coagulation, oxidative metabolism or photobiology. Furanocoumarins can absorb ultraviolet light and may cause phototoxic effects. Structural identification requires attention to aromatic proton patterns, lactone carbonyl signals and characteristic long-range correlations.

18. Alkaloids: definition and classification

Alkaloids are a diverse group of nitrogen-containing natural products, often basic and pharmacologically active. Classical alkaloids frequently derive from amino acids, but the category also includes compounds with non-amino-acid origins and neutral or weakly basic nitrogen. Classification may be based on biosynthetic origin, ring system or structural family. Alkaloids include analgesics, stimulants, antimalarials, anticancer agents and toxic defence metabolites; their activity depends on molecular target and exposure.

19. Tropane alkaloids

Tropane alkaloids contain a bicyclic tropane framework derived biosynthetically from ornithine-related metabolism. Atropine and scopolamine are examples with important antimuscarinic pharmacology. Their stereochemistry, ester substituents and bridge geometry influence receptor affinity and pharmacokinetics. Natural occurrence and biosynthetic regulation vary among plant families. Because these compounds can be toxic, discussion of pharmacology must distinguish therapeutic use under professional control from unsafe self-administration.

20. Pyrrolizidine and quinolizidine alkaloids

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids contain fused nitrogen heterocycles and occur in many plant species, often as defence compounds. Some unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids undergo metabolic activation to reactive intermediates that can damage liver tissue and genetic material. Quinolizidine alkaloids contain a different bicyclic nitrogen framework and include compounds with diverse physiological effects. Structural class, saturation, esterification and metabolism determine toxicological risk.

21. Indole alkaloids and tryptophan-derived metabolites

Indole alkaloids derive commonly from tryptophan or tryptamine and include complex structures with multiple stereocentres. Biosynthetic routes may involve decarboxylation, methylation, prenylation, oxidative coupling and cyclisation. The indole nucleus also appears in serotonin, melatonin and many non-alkaloid natural products. The electron-rich heteroaromatic ring supports electrophilic substitution, while its nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic system and is not pyridine-like basic.

23. Purine alkaloids and methylxanthines

Their methylation patterns affect potency, metabolism and solubility. Analytical methods such as HPLC and LC–MS are used to quantify these compounds in foods, beverages and biological samples.

24. Glycosides: chemical structure and classification

Glycosides contain a sugar-derived unit linked to a non-sugar aglycone through a glycosidic bond. O-glycosides are common, but N-, S- and C-glycosides also occur. Glycosylation can increase aqueous solubility, alter transport, protect reactive functional groups and change biological recognition. Hydrolysis may release the aglycone, but enzymatic specificity and bond type determine susceptibility. Glycosides include flavonoids, cardiac glycosides, saponins and cyanogenic compounds.

25. Cardiac glycosides

Cardiac glycosides contain a steroid-like aglycone and one or more sugar residues. Some inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase and thereby influence intracellular ion balance and cardiac contractility. Their therapeutic window can be narrow, and toxicity may involve gastrointestinal, neurological and cardiac effects. Structural features such as the unsaturated lactone and hydroxylation pattern contribute to activity. Clinical use requires carefully controlled dosing and monitoring; natural origin does not imply safety.

26. Saponins and amphiphilic natural products

Saponins are glycosides with hydrophobic triterpenoid or steroidal aglycones and hydrophilic sugar chains. This amphiphilic structure allows interactions with membranes and can produce foaming in aqueous solutions. Saponins occur in many plants and have been investigated for immunological, antimicrobial and formulation properties. Their effects depend strongly on structure, concentration, membrane composition and route of exposure; broad claims about health benefits require rigorous evidence.

27. Cyanogenic glycosides and chemical defence

Cyanogenic glycosides are plant metabolites that can release hydrogen cyanide after enzymatic hydrolysis and subsequent decomposition. In intact tissue, glycosides and activating enzymes may be compartmentalised; tissue damage brings them into contact. This provides a chemical defence against herbivores. Food processing can reduce cyanogenic potential under validated conditions, but safety depends on species, concentration and processing quality. Analytical quantification is important for risk assessment.

28. Glucosinolates and isothiocyanates

Glucosinolates are sulfur- and nitrogen-containing plant metabolites characteristic of many Brassicales. Tissue disruption allows myrosinase enzymes to hydrolyse glucosinolates, generating unstable intermediates that can rearrange to isothiocyanates, nitriles or related products depending on conditions. These compounds contribute to pungency and plant defence. Their biological effects are under investigation, but dose, metabolism, food matrix and individual variation are important.

29. Terpenoid natural products in medicine

Terpenoid drugs and leads include compounds with antimalarial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activities. Their mechanisms vary widely: some bind defined proteins, some disrupt membranes, and others alter signalling or metabolism. Structural complexity can create challenges in synthesis, formulation and supply. Semisynthesis, fermentation and plant-cell culture may provide scalable production routes. A natural-product scaffold is a starting point for evidence-based optimisation, not proof of clinical efficacy.

30. Microbial natural products and antibiotics

Bacteria and fungi produce specialised metabolites that mediate competition and communication. Antibiotics such as beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines and macrolides arise from distinct biosynthetic strategies. Microbial genome sequencing has revealed many silent or cryptic biosynthetic gene clusters that may encode undiscovered compounds. Activation of these pathways through cultivation changes, genetic engineering or heterologous expression is an important discovery strategy.

31. Antibiotic resistance and natural-product discovery

Antibiotic resistance arises through mechanisms such as drug inactivation, target modification, reduced permeability and active efflux. Natural-product discovery must therefore consider both potency and resistance liability. New compounds may target previously unused binding sites, inhibit resistance mechanisms or act synergistically with existing drugs. Responsible development includes susceptibility testing, mechanism studies, toxicity evaluation, pharmacokinetics and stewardship considerations.

32. Marine natural products

Marine organisms and their associated microbes produce structurally unusual metabolites influenced by ecological competition, symbiosis, pressure, salinity and light. Marine natural products include peptides, alkaloids, terpenoids and halogenated compounds. The true biosynthetic producer may be a symbiotic microorganism rather than the visible host. Sustainable supply requires aquaculture, fermentation, total synthesis or semisynthesis rather than destructive harvesting of rare organisms.

33. Bioprospecting, biodiversity and benefit sharing

Bioprospecting seeks useful biological resources and associated knowledge for research and development. Ethical practice requires compliance with access and benefit-sharing frameworks, prior informed consent where applicable, mutually agreed terms and respect for Indigenous and local communities. Biodiversity conservation is scientifically and ethically important because habitat loss can eliminate organisms and biosynthetic pathways before they are studied. Research design should include transparent provenance and fair benefit sharing.

Model answer and marking guidance

Natural products can be beneficial, inactive or harmful depending on dose, route, purity, interactions and individual susceptibility. Toxicity may result from reactive metabolites, receptor effects, enzyme inhibition, membrane disruption or contamination. Assessment includes acute and repeated-dose studies, genotoxicity, organ-specific endpoints and interaction potential. Product identity and batch consistency matter: a botanical extract is a chemically complex mixture, not a single defined substance. A strong answer should define the relevant compound class or process, explain the chemical basis, and connect structure or pathway to function, analysis or application. Credit should be given for accurate terminology, a logical mechanism or pathway where appropriate, and recognition of limitations or safety considerations.

Marking focus: definition and classification; chemical or biosynthetic reasoning; appropriate example; and evidence-aware discussion of limitations.

Chapter summary

  • Natural products arise from primary-metabolic precursors through enzyme-controlled specialised pathways.
  • Polyketide, nonribosomal peptide, terpenoid, shikimate-derived and alkaloid pathways generate diverse scaffolds.
  • Extraction and chromatographic separation must preserve analyte integrity and document sample provenance.
  • Structure elucidation relies on complementary HRMS, IR and multidimensional NMR evidence.
  • Biological activity requires reproducibility, selectivity, mechanism studies, ADME assessment and toxicology.
  • Natural origin does not guarantee safety, efficacy, sustainability or consistent composition.
  • Ethical sourcing, biodiversity conservation and equitable benefit sharing are integral to responsible research.

Academic and laboratory note

This chapter is a learning resource, not a clinical treatment guide or an operational protocol for handling toxic compounds. Consult validated analytical methods, institutional safety procedures, current pharmacopoeial standards and peer-reviewed literature before laboratory or clinical decisions. Verify plant identity and provenance; never infer safety from traditional use or a product's natural origin.
