Biology · Cell biology

Cell structure and functions: plant and animal cells

Cells are the basic structural and functional units of living organisms. Learn how cell structures support life, compare plant and animal cells and apply the ideas to microscope questions.

CBE Nexus original study guide · Labelled diagrams, comparison and assessment

1. The cell as the basic unit of life

All living organisms are made of cells. Some organisms, such as bacteria, consist of one cell, while plants and animals are made of many specialised cells. A cell contains structures that work together to obtain materials, release energy, remove waste and carry out life processes.

2. Common structures found in many cells

  • Cell membrane: controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.
  • Cytoplasm: jelly-like material where many chemical reactions take place.
  • Nucleus: contains genetic material and helps control cell activities.
  • Mitochondria: sites of aerobic respiration, releasing energy for cell activities.
  • Ribosomes: sites where proteins are made.

3. Structures that distinguish plant cells

Plant cells commonly have a cellulose cell wall outside the cell membrane. The wall supports the cell and helps it maintain its shape. Many plant cells contain chloroplasts with chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy for photosynthesis. A large permanent vacuole contains cell sap and helps maintain turgidity.

5. How structure supports specialised function

Cells become specialised to perform particular roles. A root hair cell has a long extension that increases surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions. A red blood cell has a biconcave shape that provides a large surface area for gas exchange and contains haemoglobin to carry oxygen. A nerve cell has a long fibre that carries electrical impulses over distances. A palisade cell contains many chloroplasts to absorb light for photosynthesis.

Think about adaptation: When asked how a cell is adapted to its function, link a visible structural feature to the job it performs. Do not simply list the feature without explaining its benefit.

6. Worked example: microscope magnification

Magnification compares the size of an image with the actual size of the specimen. Use Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Both measurements must be in the same units before dividing.

Worked example

A cell image is 30 mm long. The actual cell is 0.06 mm long. Calculate magnification.

  1. Write the formula: magnification = image size ÷ actual size.
  2. Substitute: 30 mm ÷ 0.06 mm.
  3. Calculate: 500.
Answer: ×500 magnification. Magnification has no unit because the units cancel.

7. Assessment questions

1. State two functions of the cell membrane.
It forms the boundary of the cell and selectively controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.
2. Name the organelle where aerobic respiration takes place.
The mitochondrion (plural: mitochondria).
3. Give two structures found in typical plant cells but not typical animal cells.
A cellulose cell wall and chloroplasts in photosynthetic plant cells. A large permanent vacuole is also characteristic of many mature plant cells.
4. Explain how a root hair cell is adapted for absorption.
Its long hair-like extension increases surface area in contact with soil, improving absorption of water and mineral ions.
5. An image of a cell measures 24 mm and the actual cell is 0.08 mm long. Calculate magnification.
Magnification = 24 ÷ 0.08 = ×300.
Exam technique: In a comparison question, state a clear difference for each feature. In an adaptation question, connect structure to function. In a magnification calculation, convert units first if the image and actual size use different units.
