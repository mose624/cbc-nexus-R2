The mole concept: amount of substance, molar mass and calculations

The mole provides a practical way to count extremely small particles by measuring a sample. This guide builds the idea from relative formula mass to step-by-step calculations.

CBE Nexus original study guide · Formulae, worked examples and assessment

1. Why do chemists use the mole?

Atoms, ions and molecules are too small to count one by one in a laboratory. Chemists therefore use the mole as a counting unit. One mole contains approximately 6.02 × 10²³ specified particles. This number is called the Avogadro constant, represented by NA.

Important distinction: The mole measures amount of substance. It does not directly measure mass or volume. The mass of one mole depends on the identity of the substance.

2. Relative atomic mass and molar mass

Relative atomic mass (Ar) compares the average mass of an atom with a standard reference. Relative formula mass (Mr) is found by adding the relative atomic masses of all atoms shown in a chemical formula. Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance, usually expressed in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹).

Worked example 1: calculate the relative formula mass of water

Water is H₂O. Use Ar(H) = 1 and Ar(O) = 16.

  1. There are two hydrogen atoms: 2 × 1 = 2.
  2. There is one oxygen atom: 1 × 16 = 16.
  3. Add: Mr(H₂O) = 2 + 16 = 18.
Answer: 18. The molar mass of water is 18 g mol⁻¹.

3. Calculating amount of substance from mass

Use the relationship n = m ÷ M, where n is amount in moles (mol), m is mass in grams (g), and M is molar mass in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹).

Worked example 2: find the amount in 9 g of water

Given mass = 9 g and molar mass of water = 18 g mol⁻¹.

  1. Write the formula: n = m ÷ M.
  2. Substitute: n = 9 ÷ 18.
  3. Calculate: n = 0.5 mol.
Answer: 0.5 moles of water.

4. Calculating mass from moles

Rearrange the same relationship to obtain m = n × M.

Worked example 3: mass of 0.25 mol of carbon dioxide

For CO₂, Mr = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44. Therefore the molar mass is 44 g mol⁻¹.

  1. Use m = n × M.
  2. Substitute: m = 0.25 × 44.
  3. Calculate: m = 11 g.
Answer: 11 g of carbon dioxide.

5. Connecting moles to the number of particles

Use N = n × NA, where N is the number of particles and NA is 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Worked example 4: particles in 2 moles

N = 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ particles.

State which particles are being counted: atoms, molecules or ions, depending on the substance.

6. A reliable method for mole calculations

  1. Read the question and identify the given quantity and the quantity required.
  2. Write the chemical formula correctly.
  3. Calculate the relative formula mass if it is not supplied.
  4. Choose the relationship that connects the known value to the unknown.
  5. Substitute values with units and show the arithmetic.
  6. Check whether the answer is reasonable and give the correct unit.

7. Assessment questions

1. Define one mole of a substance.
One mole contains approximately 6.02 × 10²³ specified elementary particles.
2. Calculate the relative formula mass of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. Use Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16.
Mr = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100.
3. Find the number of moles in 4 g of hydrogen gas, H₂. Use H = 1.
M(H₂) = 2 g mol⁻¹. n = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 mol.
4. Calculate the mass of 0.2 mol of sodium chloride, NaCl. Use Na = 23, Cl = 35.5.
M(NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g mol⁻¹. m = 0.2 × 58.5 = 11.7 g.
5. How many molecules are present in 0.5 mol of oxygen molecules?
N = 0.5 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
Common mistake: Do not use the mass in grams as though it were the number of moles. First calculate or identify molar mass, then use n = m ÷ M.
