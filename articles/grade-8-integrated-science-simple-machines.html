Simple machines: how tools make work easier

Explore levers, pulleys, inclined planes, wheels and axles. Learn how a machine changes the size or direction of a force and practise calculating mechanical advantage and efficiency.

CBE Nexus original study guide · Diagrams, worked examples and revision questions

1. What is a machine?

A machine is a device that helps us do work more conveniently. A simple machine can change the magnitude or direction of an applied force, or allow the force to act over a different distance. It does not create energy. In an ideal machine, the work put in equals the useful work obtained; in real machines, some energy is transferred to the surroundings, often as heat or sound.

Work done: When a force moves an object in the direction of the force, work done = force × distance moved in that direction. The SI unit is the joule (J).

2. Main types of simple machines

Lever

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point called the fulcrum. The effort is the force applied; the load is the resistance being moved. A first-class lever has the fulcrum between effort and load (for example, a seesaw). In a second-class lever the load is between fulcrum and effort (a wheelbarrow). In a third-class lever the effort is between fulcrum and load (the human forearm lifting an object).

Pulley

A pulley is a grooved wheel that supports a rope or cable. A fixed pulley mainly changes the direction of effort. A movable pulley can reduce the effort needed by sharing the load between sections of rope. In an ideal system, the velocity ratio is related to the number of rope sections supporting the moving load.

Inclined plane

An inclined plane is a sloping surface used to raise or lower a load over a longer distance with a smaller effort. A ramp is an everyday example. A longer, gentler ramp generally reduces the force needed, although the load travels farther.

Wheel and axle; screw; wedge

A wheel and axle consists of a large wheel fixed to a smaller axle. A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. A wedge is two inclined planes joined to form a sharp edge; an axe and chisel are examples.

3. Mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency

Mechanical advantage (MA) compares the load with the effort. Velocity ratio (VR) compares the distance moved by the effort with the distance moved by the load. Efficiency compares useful work output with work input.

MA = Load ÷ Effort

VR = Distance moved by effort ÷ Distance moved by load

Efficiency (%) = (MA ÷ VR) × 100

For a real machine, efficiency is normally below 100% because some input energy is dissipated through friction, sound, deformation or heating.

4. Worked example: mechanical advantage

Example 1

A lever lifts a load of 240 N when an effort of 60 N is applied. Find its mechanical advantage.

  1. Write MA = load ÷ effort.
  2. Substitute MA = 240 N ÷ 60 N.
  3. Divide: MA = 4.
Answer: Mechanical advantage = 4. The lever multiplies the applied force by a factor of four in this example.

Example 2: efficiency

A machine has a mechanical advantage of 3 and a velocity ratio of 4. Calculate its efficiency.

  1. Efficiency = (MA ÷ VR) × 100%.
  2. Substitute = (3 ÷ 4) × 100%.
  3. Calculate = 75%.
Answer: 75%.

5. Reducing friction and using machines safely

Friction between moving surfaces can reduce efficiency. Lubricating suitable moving parts, using bearings, keeping surfaces clean and aligning components can reduce unwanted friction. Machines should be used within their rated loads. Learners should not experiment with heavy suspended loads or moving machinery without teacher supervision.

6. Assessment questions

1. What is the fixed turning point of a lever called?
The fulcrum.
2. Classify a wheelbarrow according to its lever class.
A wheelbarrow is a second-class lever because the load is between the fulcrum (wheel) and the effort (handles).
3. A machine lifts a load of 360 N using an effort of 90 N. Calculate its mechanical advantage.
MA = 360 ÷ 90 = 4.
4. A machine has MA = 2.5 and VR = 5. Find its efficiency.
Efficiency = (2.5 ÷ 5) × 100% = 50%.
5. Explain why a ramp makes it easier to move a heavy object to a raised platform.
The ramp increases the distance over which the object is raised, reducing the force needed compared with lifting it vertically. The work required is not eliminated.
Exam technique: Write the formula first, substitute values with units, show the arithmetic and state the final answer. Remember that mechanical advantage and velocity ratio have no units.
