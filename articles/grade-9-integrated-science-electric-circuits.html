Grade 9 · Integrated Science · Electricity

Electric circuits: components, current, voltage and resistance

Learn how a complete electric circuit works, identify common components, interpret a simple circuit diagram and use Ohm’s law to solve numerical questions.

CBE Nexus original study guide · Includes a labelled diagram, worked examples and self-assessment

1. What is an electric circuit?

An electric circuit is a complete conducting path through which electric charge can flow. A simple circuit may contain a cell or battery, connecting wires, a switch and a load such as a lamp. The circuit must be closed for current to flow continuously. When the switch is open, the conducting path is broken and the lamp goes off.

Remember: A cell supplies energy to charges in the circuit. The lamp, buzzer or motor transfers electrical energy into another form such as light, sound or movement.

2. Main circuit components and their functions

  • Cell: provides a potential difference that drives charge around the circuit.
  • Battery: two or more cells connected together.
  • Connecting wires: provide a conducting path.
  • Switch: opens or closes the circuit.
  • Lamp: converts electrical energy mainly into light and heat.
  • Resistor: limits current and produces a potential difference across it.
  • Ammeter: measures current and is connected in series.
  • Voltmeter: measures potential difference and is connected in parallel across a component.

3. Electric current, potential difference and resistance

Current is the rate of flow of electric charge. It is measured in amperes (A) using an ammeter. Potential difference is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points; it is measured in volts (V) using a voltmeter. Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current; it is measured in ohms (Ω).

For a fixed resistor at constant temperature, increasing the potential difference increases the current in the same proportion. This relationship is described by Ohm’s law.

4. Ohm’s law

V = I × R

Where V is potential difference in volts, I is current in amperes and R is resistance in ohms. Rearrange the formula when another quantity is required: I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.

Worked example 1: calculate potential difference

A resistor has a resistance of 6 Ω. A current of 2 A flows through it. Calculate the potential difference.

  1. Write the relationship: V = I × R.
  2. Substitute the values: V = 2 × 6.
  3. Multiply: V = 12 V.
Answer: 12 volts. Always include the correct unit.

Worked example 2: calculate resistance

A component has a potential difference of 9 V across it and carries a current of 0.5 A. Find its resistance.

  1. Use R = V ÷ I.
  2. Substitute: R = 9 ÷ 0.5.
  3. Calculate: R = 18 Ω.
Answer: 18 ohms.

5. Series and parallel connections

In a series circuit, components are connected along one path. The same current passes through each component. If one component breaks, the path is interrupted and all components go off. In a parallel circuit, components are connected on separate branches. Each branch has the same potential difference as the supply, and one branch can continue working if another branch is broken.

FeatureSeries connectionParallel connection
Paths for currentOne pathTwo or more branches
CurrentSame through each componentDivides between branches
Potential differenceShared across componentsSame across each branch
If one lamp failsAll lamps may go offOther branches can remain on

6. Assessment questions

1. State what is meant by an electric circuit.
A complete conducting path through which electric charge can flow.
2. Which instrument measures current, and how is it connected?
An ammeter measures current and is connected in series with the component.
3. A current of 0.4 A flows through a 15 Ω resistor. Calculate the potential difference.
V = I × R = 0.4 × 15 = 6 V.
4. A 12 V supply produces a current of 3 A. Calculate the resistance.
R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω.
5. Give one advantage of connecting household lamps in parallel.
Each lamp receives the supply potential difference and can operate independently of the others.
Study tip: Draw the circuit before calculating. Write the formula, substitute values with units, show each step and check that the final unit matches the quantity requested.
