Chapter 1: Atomic Structure and Quantum Theory

1.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to explain the development of atomic models; distinguish protons, neutrons and electrons; interpret atomic number and mass number; apply the principal, angular momentum, magnetic and spin quantum numbers; describe atomic orbitals; write electron configurations; and use the de Broglie and Heisenberg relationships in appropriate calculations.

1.2 The structure of the atom

An atom consists of a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. The nucleus contains positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. Electrons carry a negative charge and occupy quantum states described by wavefunctions. Most of the atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus, while the spatial distribution of electrons determines much of its chemical behaviour.

The atomic number, Z, is the number of protons. The mass number, A, is the total number of protons and neutrons. Therefore, the number of neutrons is A − Z. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

Nuclear notation: ᴬZX  where Z = protons and A = protons + neutrons

1.3 Isotopes and ions

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with equal proton numbers but different neutron numbers. They have very similar chemical properties because they have the same electron arrangement in neutral atoms, although their masses and some physical properties differ. An ion is formed when an atom or group of atoms gains or loses electrons. Loss of electrons forms a cation; gain forms an anion.

Worked example 1: isotope composition

Determine the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of chlorine-37, written ³⁷₁₇Cl.

Solution: Protons = Z = 17. Neutrons = A − Z = 37 − 17 = 20. Since the atom is neutral, electrons = 17.

Answer: 17 protons, 20 neutrons and 17 electrons.

1.4 Development of the quantum model

Classical models could not fully explain atomic stability or the discrete line spectra of atoms. The quantum model describes allowed energies and probability distributions rather than electrons travelling in fixed planetary paths.

  • Planck: proposed that energy exchange occurs in discrete packets, with energy proportional to frequency.
  • Einstein: used light quanta (photons) to explain the photoelectric effect.
  • Bohr: introduced quantised energy levels for the hydrogen atom, explaining its principal spectral lines.
  • de Broglie: proposed that particles have wave character.
  • Heisenberg: formulated an uncertainty relation for conjugate quantities such as position and momentum.
  • Schrödinger: developed a wave equation whose solutions describe quantum states.

1.5 Electromagnetic radiation and photon energy

Electromagnetic radiation travels in vacuum at the speed of light, c. Wavelength (λ), frequency (ν) and speed are related by:

c = λν

Photon energy is quantised:

E = hν = hc/λ

where h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Shorter wavelength corresponds to higher frequency and higher photon energy.

Worked example 2: photon energy

Calculate the energy of a photon with wavelength 500 nm.

Convert wavelength: 500 nm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.

Use E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸)/(5.00 × 10⁻⁷).

Answer: E = 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon (three significant figures).

1.6 de Broglie matter waves

de Broglie proposed that a particle with momentum p has an associated wavelength:

λ = h/p = h/(mv)

This wave character is significant for microscopic particles such as electrons. For large everyday objects, the associated wavelength is so small that wave behaviour is not readily observed.

Worked example 3: electron wavelength

An electron moves at 2.00 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Estimate its de Broglie wavelength using electron mass m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

λ = h/(mv) = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ / [(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(2.00 × 10⁶)].

Answer: λ ≈ 3.64 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.364 nm.

1.7 Heisenberg uncertainty principle

The uncertainty principle states that position and momentum cannot both be specified with unlimited precision for the same quantum state. For one spatial dimension:

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 = h/(4π)

Here Δx and Δp are standard uncertainties in position and momentum, and ħ = h/(2π). Since p = mv for a non-relativistic particle of fixed mass, Δp = mΔv. This is not merely a limitation of measuring instruments; it is a fundamental feature of quantum states.

1.8 Atomic orbitals and quantum numbers

An orbital is a one-electron spatial wavefunction (or the associated probability distribution) in an atom. It is not a fixed path. An electron state in a hydrogen-like atom is labelled by four quantum numbers:

1.9 Shapes and nodes of orbitals

An s orbital is spherically symmetric. A p subshell has three mutually perpendicular p orbitals, commonly represented as two-lobed probability distributions. The five d orbitals have more varied angular distributions. These drawings represent probability amplitude or probability density conventions; they are not solid boundaries around an electron.

A node is a region where the wavefunction is zero. For hydrogen-like orbitals, the total number of nodes is n − 1; angular nodes = l; radial nodes = n − l − 1.

1.10 Electron configurations and filling rules

Electron configurations describe how electrons occupy atomic orbitals. Three principles guide the usual ground-state filling order:

  • Aufbau principle: orbitals are filled in order of increasing energy, subject to the atom's electronic structure.
  • Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.
  • Hund's rule: degenerate orbitals are singly occupied with parallel spins before pairing, for the lowest-energy arrangement.

A commonly used introductory filling sequence is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d and 7p. Exceptions occur, especially among transition elements, because subshell energies are close and electron-electron interactions matter.

Worked example 4: electron configuration of sulfur

Sulfur has atomic number 16, so a neutral sulfur atom has 16 electrons. Filling the orbitals gives:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴

The shell distribution is 2, 8, 6. The highest occupied principal shell is n = 3, and sulfur has six valence electrons in its 3s and 3p subshells.

1.11 Chapter summary

  • Atomic number identifies an element; mass number counts protons plus neutrons.
  • Photon energy is E = hν = hc/λ.
  • Particle wavelength is λ = h/p.
  • Position and momentum obey ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2.
  • Quantum numbers specify an allowed electron state; orbitals are probability-based quantum descriptions.
  • Electron configurations follow Aufbau, Pauli and Hund principles, with known exceptions.

1.13 Atomic spectra and electronic transitions

Atomic spectra provide direct experimental evidence for quantised electronic energies. An atom absorbs radiation when a photon promotes an electron from an initial state to a higher state, provided the photon energy matches the allowed energy difference and the transition satisfies the relevant selection rules. An excited atom can emit radiation when it returns to a lower state. The photon energy is the difference between the states: ΔE = Eupper − Elower = hν. Since c = λν, the wavelength is λ = hc/ΔE. Each element has a characteristic pattern of spectral lines, which supports elemental identification and spectroscopic analysis.

An emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms release photons. An absorption spectrum is produced when atoms absorb selected wavelengths from a continuous source. Dark absorption lines correspond to wavelengths that can drive allowed upward transitions. Real spectral lines may be broadened by Doppler motion, collisions, instrumental resolution and finite excited-state lifetimes. These effects do not remove the underlying quantisation; they influence the observed line shape and width.

For hydrogen, the Bohr model gives En = −13.6 eV/n². Transitions ending at n = 1 form the Lyman series, transitions ending at n = 2 form the Balmer series and transitions ending at n = 3 form the Paschen series. The Rydberg equation is 1/λ = RH(1/n1² − 1/n2²), with n2 greater than n1. These simple expressions apply to hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron ions; multi-electron atoms require more detailed models.

1.14 Photoelectric effect

The photoelectric effect occurs when light ejects electrons from a material. The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends on photon frequency, not merely light intensity. Below a threshold frequency, no electrons are emitted in the idealised experiment. Einstein explained this using photons with energy hν. A minimum energy, the work function Φ, is required to liberate an electron; the remainder appears as maximum kinetic energy: Kmax = hν − Φ. Increasing intensity above threshold increases the photon flux and can increase the number of emitted electrons, but it does not increase the energy of each photon.

1.15 Schrödinger equation and the wavefunction

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is Ĥψ = Eψ. Ĥ is the Hamiltonian operator, ψ is the wavefunction and E is an allowed energy eigenvalue. The Hamiltonian contains kinetic and potential energy. For a hydrogen-like atom, the potential is the Coulomb attraction between the nucleus and electron. Solving the equation with physically acceptable boundary conditions gives allowed energy states and their wavefunctions.

The wavefunction is not itself a directly observable probability. The Born interpretation states that |ψ|² is a probability density. The probability of finding an electron in a small volume dτ is |ψ|²dτ. A normalised wavefunction satisfies ∫|ψ|²dτ = 1 over all space. Normalisation therefore ensures that the total probability of finding the electron somewhere is unity. The sign or phase of a wavefunction is important when orbitals combine, although the sign alone is not a probability.

1.16 Radial probability and nodes

For hydrogen-like atoms, the wavefunction separates into radial and angular factors. The radial part describes variation with distance from the nucleus; the angular part describes directional dependence. For an orbital labelled by n and l, total nodes = n − 1, angular nodes = l and radial nodes = n − l − 1. Thus a 3p orbital has one angular node and one radial node. A 2p orbital has one angular node and no radial node. A node is a location or surface at which the wavefunction is zero.

Probability density at a point must be distinguished from radial probability. The probability of finding an electron in a spherical shell includes the volume of the shell, which increases with radius. For a spherically symmetric distribution, the radial distribution includes a factor proportional to 4πr². Consequently, the most probable radial distance need not coincide with the point at which |ψ|² is greatest.

1.17 Penetration, shielding and orbital energies

In multi-electron atoms, orbital energies depend on both principal quantum number and angular momentum. An s orbital penetrates closer to the nucleus than a p orbital of the same n; p generally penetrates more than d, and d more than f. More penetrating electrons experience stronger effective nuclear attraction because they spend more time in regions less shielded by core electrons. This helps explain common subshell energy orderings, but the order can change with electron configuration and ionisation.

A useful approximate expression is Zeff ≈ Z − S, where Z is nuclear charge and S is a screening constant. Rules such as Slater's rules estimate S, but they are models rather than exact quantum-mechanical results. The effective nuclear charge depends on which electron is being considered and the approximation used.

1.18 Transition-metal configurations and ions

For transition elements, 4s electrons are generally removed before 3d electrons when cations form. Iron is commonly represented as [Ar]3d⁶4s²; Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁵. The simplified filling sequence is not a permanent ordering of orbital energies in every atom and ion. Chromium and copper are familiar exceptions to the simplest Aufbau prediction: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ respectively. The total energy depends on electron-nucleus attraction, electron-electron repulsion, exchange and correlation; half-filled and filled subshell descriptions alone are not a complete explanation.

1.19 Magnetic properties

Species with one or more unpaired electrons are paramagnetic and are attracted into a magnetic field. Species with all electrons paired are diamagnetic and are weakly repelled. In the spin-only approximation, the magnetic moment is μ = √[n(n + 2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons and BM denotes Bohr magnetons. The approximation is useful for many introductory transition-metal problems, although orbital contributions, spin-orbit coupling and covalency can affect measured values.

1.20 Integrated problem-solving method

For numerical questions, list known quantities and units first. Convert nanometres to metres before using SI values of h and c. Distinguish energy per photon from energy per mole; multiply by Avogadro's constant only when a molar quantity is required. For electron configurations, count electrons after accounting for charge, then apply Aufbau, Pauli exclusion and Hund's rule. For transition-metal ions, remove electrons from the appropriate outer subshell. Show the equation, substitution, units and final significant figures.

Chapter 2: Periodic Properties of the Elements

2.1 Learning outcomes

You should be able to explain the modern periodic law, relate periodic position to electron configuration, and describe trends in atomic and ionic radii, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.

2.2 Modern periodic law and electronic structure

The modern periodic law states that the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The periodic table arranges elements by increasing atomic number. Periods correspond broadly to the highest occupied principal shell, while blocks reflect the type of subshell being filled. The s-, p-, d- and f-blocks therefore connect periodic position with electron configuration.

2.3 Effective nuclear charge and shielding

In a multi-electron atom, an outer electron experiences attraction to the nucleus reduced by repulsion from other electrons. This net attraction is often discussed using effective nuclear charge, Zeff. Shielding is not identical for all orbitals: penetration and orbital shape influence how strongly an electron experiences the nucleus. Across a period, Zeff generally increases for valence electrons, helping explain contraction in atomic size.

2.4 Atomic and ionic radii

Atomic radius is not a uniquely fixed boundary because electron clouds do not end sharply. Different operational definitions include covalent, metallic and van der Waals radii. Across a period, atomic radius generally decreases as effective nuclear charge increases. Down a group, radius generally increases because additional principal shells are occupied.

Cations are usually smaller than their parent atoms because electron loss reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the outer shell. Anions are usually larger because added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion within the valence shell.

2.5 First ionisation energy

First ionisation energy is the minimum energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state:

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group. The trend has exceptions due to subshell energies and electron pairing. For example, the first ionisation energy of boron is lower than that of beryllium because the electron removed from boron occupies a higher-energy 2p subshell. Oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen partly because pairing in one 2p orbital introduces additional repulsion.

2.6 Successive ionisation energies

Successive ionisation energies increase because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. A particularly large jump occurs after all valence electrons have been removed and the next electron must be taken from an inner shell. This jump can help infer the number of valence electrons.

Worked example 5: interpreting ionisation energies

An element has successive ionisation energies (arbitrary units): 580, 1820, 2740, 11 600. The large jump occurs between the third and fourth removals.

Interpretation: The atom most likely has three valence electrons. After these are removed, the fourth electron comes from a more stable inner shell. The element is therefore likely to be in Group 13 (Group IIIA in older notation), subject to checking the full data and chemical context.

2.7 Electron affinity and electronegativity

Electron affinity describes the energy change when a gaseous atom accepts an electron to form an anion. Sign conventions vary between textbooks: some report energy released as a positive magnitude, while thermodynamic conventions report an exothermic enthalpy change as negative. Always state the convention being used. Electron affinity trends contain important exceptions, including the relatively less favourable addition of an electron to a compact filled subshell.

Electronegativity is the relative tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electron density. It is a scale-dependent property, not the same quantity as electron affinity or ionisation energy. Fluorine is the most electronegative element on the commonly used Pauling scale.

  2. Explain the general decrease in atomic radius across Period 3.
  3. Why is the first ionisation energy of aluminium lower than that of magnesium?
  4. Explain the large jump between the third and fourth ionisation energies in the worked example.

Suggested answers
  1. Effective nuclear charge is the net positive nuclear attraction experienced by an electron after shielding by other electrons.
  2. Proton number increases while added electrons enter the same principal shell; shielding does not rise enough to offset the increased nuclear attraction.
  3. Aluminium loses a 3p electron, which is higher in energy and more shielded than magnesium's 3s electron, so it is easier to remove.
  4. The first three electrons are valence electrons; the fourth is removed from a lower-energy inner shell.
  5. Electron affinity concerns the energy change for adding an electron to a gaseous atom; electronegativity measures an atom's attraction for shared electron density in a bond.

2.10 Physical basis of periodicity

Periodicity is the recurrence of similar valence-shell patterns as atomic number increases. When a new period begins, electrons enter a new principal shell. As that shell fills, nuclear charge, shielding, penetration and electron pairing change systematically. Elements in the same group often have related valence configurations and therefore similarities in common oxidation states, bonding patterns and reactivity. These are trends rather than absolute rules; heavier p-block elements, transition elements and lanthanides show important deviations.

2.11 Effective nuclear charge and screening

Shielding describes the reduction in nuclear attraction experienced by an electron because other electrons repel it and partially screen the nucleus. Inner-shell electrons generally shield outer electrons more effectively than electrons in the same shell. Penetration describes the extent to which electron density reaches close to the nucleus. For orbitals of the same principal quantum number, s orbitals generally penetrate most strongly, followed by p, d and f. A more penetrating electron spends more time close to the nucleus and can experience greater effective attraction.

A useful introductory model is Zeff ≈ Z − S. It explains why an increase in proton number across a period is not completely cancelled by added electrons. However, effective nuclear charge is orbital-dependent. Slater-style screening rules provide estimates, not exact values, and should be described as approximations.

2.12 Atomic radii and their definitions

Atoms have diffuse electron clouds rather than sharp surfaces, so atomic radius is an operational quantity. Covalent radius is commonly inferred from half the internuclear distance between identical atoms joined by a covalent bond. Metallic radius is inferred from nearest-neighbour distances in a metal lattice. Van der Waals radius is based on non-bonded close contacts. These definitions give different numerical values, so comparisons should use the same radius definition and similar chemical environments.

Across a period, proton number increases while added electrons enter the same general valence shell. Shielding increases, but not enough to offset the increased nuclear charge; the electron cloud is pulled inward. Down a group, an additional principal shell is occupied and the valence electrons are generally farther from the nucleus, so radius increases. Transition-metal radii often change more gradually because d electrons provide some shielding while the d orbitals contract.

Lanthanide contraction is the gradual decrease in size across the lanthanide series. The 4f electrons shield the increasing nuclear charge relatively poorly, causing the outer electron cloud to contract. This influences the sizes and chemistry of elements after the lanthanides, including similarities between some 4d and 5d transition elements.

2.13 Ionic radii and isoelectronic species

A cation is generally smaller than its neutral parent because electron removal reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the outer shell. An anion is generally larger because added electrons increase repulsion in the valence region. Ionic radius also depends on oxidation state, coordination number, spin state and crystal structure. For isoelectronic species, electron number is fixed; increasing nuclear charge generally contracts the electron cloud.

Worked example: isoelectronic radius order

O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each contain ten electrons. Their proton numbers are 8, 9, 11 and 12 respectively. Mg²⁺ has the greatest nuclear charge and is smallest; O²⁻ has the smallest nuclear charge and is largest. Decreasing radius: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺.

2.14 First ionisation energy

First ionisation energy is the enthalpy change for removing one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground states to form gaseous singly charged cations. It is endothermic and positive under the usual convention. The gas-phase definition matters: ionisation energy is not the same as the work function of a solid or the energy needed to remove an electron from a solvated ion.

Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally increases and makes electron removal more difficult. Down a group, increased shell distance and shielding generally make removal easier. The trend has local exceptions. Boron has a lower first ionisation energy than beryllium because its first removed electron is in a higher-energy 2p subshell rather than 2s. Oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen partly because one 2p orbital contains a pair of electrons, increasing repulsion and making removal of one paired electron easier.

2.15 Successive ionisation energies

Successive ionisation energies refer to sequential electron removal from X, X⁺, X²⁺ and so forth. Each removal generally requires more energy because the remaining species becomes more positively charged. A large jump after a certain number of removals indicates that the next electron is being taken from a more tightly bound inner shell. This can help estimate the number of valence electrons. However, oxidation-state stability also depends on bonding, lattice enthalpy, hydration and other thermodynamic contributions.

2.16 Electron affinity and sign conventions

Electron affinity describes the energy change for X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). Some texts define electron affinity as energy released and report an exothermic process as a positive number; others report the enthalpy change, which is negative when energy is released. Follow the convention specified in the question. Trends are irregular because electron attachment depends on shell size, electron repulsion and subshell occupancy. Chlorine's electron attachment is more exothermic than fluorine's under common conventions because fluorine's compact 2p shell produces stronger repulsion for the incoming electron.

2.17 Electronegativity and bond polarity

Electronegativity is a relative measure of an atom's tendency to attract shared electron density in a chemical bond. The Pauling scale is derived from bond-energy comparisons; the Mulliken scale relates electronegativity to ionisation energy and electron affinity. Values from different scales should not be mixed without identifying the scale. Electronegativity is not identical to electron affinity, which concerns electron attachment to a gaseous atom.

A difference in electronegativity helps predict bond polarity. In a polar covalent bond, shared electron density shifts toward the more electronegative atom, producing partial charges δ− and δ+. There is no universal sharp boundary between ionic and covalent bonding. Molecular polarity also depends on geometry: polar bonds can cancel in a symmetrical molecule such as CO₂, whereas the bent structure of H₂O gives a net molecular dipole.

2.18 Metallic character, reactivity and oxides

Metallic character describes the tendency to lose electrons and exhibit metallic bonding and related physical properties. It generally increases down a group and toward the left of the periodic table. Alkali-metal reactivity generally increases down Group 1 because the outer electron becomes easier to remove. Halogen oxidising strength generally decreases down Group 17, although actual reactivity depends on the full reaction and conditions.

Across Period 3, oxide behaviour changes broadly from basic through amphoteric to acidic. Sodium and magnesium oxides are basic; aluminium oxide is amphoteric; silicon dioxide and many oxides of phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine are acidic. This reflects changes in bonding and the ability of oxides to react with acids, bases or water. Silicon dioxide is a network solid and does not simply dissolve in water to form a molecular acid.

2.19 Diagonal relationships and second-period elements

Some second-period elements resemble elements diagonally below them: lithium shares features with magnesium, beryllium with aluminium, and boron with silicon. These partial similarities arise partly because opposing trends in size and charge density can produce comparable polarising power and bonding behaviour. They do not mean the elements have identical chemistry.

Second-period elements are often distinctive because of their small size, high electronegativity and lack of low-energy valence d orbitals in the simple bonding model. They often follow the octet rule more closely than heavier congeners, although the octet rule is a useful model rather than an inviolable law.

2.20 Writing strong periodicity explanations

A strong answer links electron configuration to the property. To compare Na and Cl radii, state that both are in Period 3, proton number increases across the period and added electrons enter the same general shell. Shielding does not fully offset the extra nuclear charge, so effective attraction rises and radius generally decreases. To compare Na and K, identify the additional occupied shell in potassium and explain why its valence electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

For ionisation-energy exceptions, identify the subshell from which the electron is removed. The Mg/Al comparison requires discussion of 3s versus 3p electrons. The N/O comparison requires discussion of the paired 2p electron in oxygen. Avoid simply repeating a trend; explain the physical cause and connect it to the electron being considered.

Worked comparison: Na, Mg and Al

These elements occupy Period 3. Proton number increases from 11 to 12 to 13 while valence electrons enter the same principal shell. Effective nuclear attraction generally increases, so the broad radius order is Na > Mg > Al. The precise numerical values depend on the radius definition.

Worked comparison: Na and K ionisation energy

Na has a 3s valence electron; K has a 4s valence electron. The potassium electron is farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons. It is easier to remove, so IE₁(Na) is greater than IE₁(K).

Worked comparison: Be and B

Be is 1s²2s² and B is 1s²2s²2p¹. The electron removed from B is in the higher-energy, less penetrating 2p subshell. It is easier to remove than a 2s electron from Be, so B has the lower first ionisation energy despite its greater nuclear charge.
