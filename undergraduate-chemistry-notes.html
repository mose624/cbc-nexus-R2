Chapter 1: Atomic Structure and Quantum Theory

1.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to explain the development of atomic models; distinguish protons, neutrons and electrons; interpret atomic number and mass number; apply the principal, angular momentum, magnetic and spin quantum numbers; describe atomic orbitals; write electron configurations; and use the de Broglie and Heisenberg relationships in appropriate calculations.

1.2 The structure of the atom

An atom consists of a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. The nucleus contains positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. Electrons carry a negative charge and occupy quantum states described by wavefunctions. Most of the atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus, while the spatial distribution of electrons determines much of its chemical behaviour.

1.3 Isotopes and ions

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with equal proton numbers but different neutron numbers. They have very similar chemical properties because they have the same electron arrangement in neutral atoms, although their masses and some physical properties differ. An ion is formed when an atom or group of atoms gains or loses electrons. Loss of electrons forms a cation; gain forms an anion.

Worked example 1: isotope composition

Determine the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of chlorine-37, written ³⁷₁₇Cl.

Solution: Protons = Z = 17. Neutrons = A − Z = 37 − 17 = 20. Since the atom is neutral, electrons = 17.

Answer: 17 protons, 20 neutrons and 17 electrons.

1.4 Development of the quantum model

Classical models could not fully explain atomic stability or the discrete line spectra of atoms. The quantum model describes allowed energies and probability distributions rather than electrons travelling in fixed planetary paths.

  • Planck: proposed that energy exchange occurs in discrete packets, with energy proportional to frequency.
  • Einstein: used light quanta (photons) to explain the photoelectric effect.
  • Bohr: introduced quantised energy levels for the hydrogen atom, explaining its principal spectral lines.
  • de Broglie: proposed that particles have wave character.
  • Heisenberg: formulated an uncertainty relation for conjugate quantities such as position and momentum.
  • Schrödinger: developed a wave equation whose solutions describe quantum states.

1.5 Electromagnetic radiation and photon energy

Electromagnetic radiation travels in vacuum at the speed of light, c. Wavelength (λ), frequency (ν) and speed are related by:

c = λν

Photon energy is quantised:

E = hν = hc/λ

where h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Shorter wavelength corresponds to higher frequency and higher photon energy.

Worked example 2: photon energy

Calculate the energy of a photon with wavelength 500 nm.

Convert wavelength: 500 nm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.

Use E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸)/(5.00 × 10⁻⁷).

Answer: E = 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon (three significant figures).

1.6 de Broglie matter waves

de Broglie proposed that a particle with momentum p has an associated wavelength:

λ = h/p = h/(mv)

This wave character is significant for microscopic particles such as electrons. For large everyday objects, the associated wavelength is so small that wave behaviour is not readily observed.

Worked example 3: electron wavelength

An electron moves at 2.00 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Estimate its de Broglie wavelength using electron mass m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

λ = h/(mv) = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ / [(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(2.00 × 10⁶)].

Answer: λ ≈ 3.64 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.364 nm.

1.7 Heisenberg uncertainty principle

The uncertainty principle states that position and momentum cannot both be specified with unlimited precision for the same quantum state. For one spatial dimension:

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 = h/(4π)

Here Δx and Δp are standard uncertainties in position and momentum, and ħ = h/(2π). Since p = mv for a non-relativistic particle of fixed mass, Δp = mΔv. This is not merely a limitation of measuring instruments; it is a fundamental feature of quantum states.

1.9 Shapes and nodes of orbitals

An s orbital is spherically symmetric. A p subshell has three mutually perpendicular p orbitals, commonly represented as two-lobed probability distributions. The five d orbitals have more varied angular distributions. These drawings represent probability amplitude or probability density conventions; they are not solid boundaries around an electron.

A node is a region where the wavefunction is zero. For hydrogen-like orbitals, the total number of nodes is n − 1; angular nodes = l; radial nodes = n − l − 1.

1.10 Electron configurations and filling rules

Electron configurations describe how electrons occupy atomic orbitals. Three principles guide the usual ground-state filling order:

  • Aufbau principle: orbitals are filled in order of increasing energy, subject to the atom's electronic structure.
  • Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.
  • Hund's rule: degenerate orbitals are singly occupied with parallel spins before pairing, for the lowest-energy arrangement.

A commonly used introductory filling sequence is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d and 7p. Exceptions occur, especially among transition elements, because subshell energies are close and electron-electron interactions matter.

Worked example 4: electron configuration of sulfur

Sulfur has atomic number 16, so a neutral sulfur atom has 16 electrons. Filling the orbitals gives:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴

The shell distribution is 2, 8, 6. The highest occupied principal shell is n = 3, and sulfur has six valence electrons in its 3s and 3p subshells.

1.11 Chapter summary

  • Atomic number identifies an element; mass number counts protons plus neutrons.
  • Photon energy is E = hν = hc/λ.
  • Particle wavelength is λ = h/p.
  • Position and momentum obey ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2.
  • Quantum numbers specify an allowed electron state; orbitals are probability-based quantum descriptions.
  • Electron configurations follow Aufbau, Pauli and Hund principles, with known exceptions.

1.13 Atomic spectra and electronic transitions

Atomic spectra provide direct experimental evidence for quantised electronic energies. An atom absorbs radiation when a photon promotes an electron from an initial state to a higher state, provided the photon energy matches the allowed energy difference and the transition satisfies the relevant selection rules. An excited atom can emit radiation when it returns to a lower state. The photon energy is the difference between the states: ΔE = Eupper − Elower = hν. Since c = λν, the wavelength is λ = hc/ΔE. Each element has a characteristic pattern of spectral lines, which supports elemental identification and spectroscopic analysis.

An emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms release photons. An absorption spectrum is produced when atoms absorb selected wavelengths from a continuous source. Dark absorption lines correspond to wavelengths that can drive allowed upward transitions. Real spectral lines may be broadened by Doppler motion, collisions, instrumental resolution and finite excited-state lifetimes. These effects do not remove the underlying quantisation; they influence the observed line shape and width.

For hydrogen, the Bohr model gives En = −13.6 eV/n². Transitions ending at n = 1 form the Lyman series, transitions ending at n = 2 form the Balmer series and transitions ending at n = 3 form the Paschen series. The Rydberg equation is 1/λ = RH(1/n1² − 1/n2²), with n2 greater than n1. These simple expressions apply to hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron ions; multi-electron atoms require more detailed models.

1.14 Photoelectric effect

The photoelectric effect occurs when light ejects electrons from a material. The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends on photon frequency, not merely light intensity. Below a threshold frequency, no electrons are emitted in the idealised experiment. Einstein explained this using photons with energy hν. A minimum energy, the work function Φ, is required to liberate an electron; the remainder appears as maximum kinetic energy: Kmax = hν − Φ. Increasing intensity above threshold increases the photon flux and can increase the number of emitted electrons, but it does not increase the energy of each photon.

1.15 Schrödinger equation and the wavefunction

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is Ĥψ = Eψ. Ĥ is the Hamiltonian operator, ψ is the wavefunction and E is an allowed energy eigenvalue. The Hamiltonian contains kinetic and potential energy. For a hydrogen-like atom, the potential is the Coulomb attraction between the nucleus and electron. Solving the equation with physically acceptable boundary conditions gives allowed energy states and their wavefunctions.

The wavefunction is not itself a directly observable probability. The Born interpretation states that |ψ|² is a probability density. The probability of finding an electron in a small volume dτ is |ψ|²dτ. A normalised wavefunction satisfies ∫|ψ|²dτ = 1 over all space. Normalisation therefore ensures that the total probability of finding the electron somewhere is unity. The sign or phase of a wavefunction is important when orbitals combine, although the sign alone is not a probability.

1.16 Radial probability and nodes

For hydrogen-like atoms, the wavefunction separates into radial and angular factors. The radial part describes variation with distance from the nucleus; the angular part describes directional dependence. For an orbital labelled by n and l, total nodes = n − 1, angular nodes = l and radial nodes = n − l − 1. Thus a 3p orbital has one angular node and one radial node. A 2p orbital has one angular node and no radial node. A node is a location or surface at which the wavefunction is zero.

Probability density at a point must be distinguished from radial probability. The probability of finding an electron in a spherical shell includes the volume of the shell, which increases with radius. For a spherically symmetric distribution, the radial distribution includes a factor proportional to 4πr². Consequently, the most probable radial distance need not coincide with the point at which |ψ|² is greatest.

1.17 Penetration, shielding and orbital energies

In multi-electron atoms, orbital energies depend on both principal quantum number and angular momentum. An s orbital penetrates closer to the nucleus than a p orbital of the same n; p generally penetrates more than d, and d more than f. More penetrating electrons experience stronger effective nuclear attraction because they spend more time in regions less shielded by core electrons. This helps explain common subshell energy orderings, but the order can change with electron configuration and ionisation.

A useful approximate expression is Zeff ≈ Z − S, where Z is nuclear charge and S is a screening constant. Rules such as Slater's rules estimate S, but they are models rather than exact quantum-mechanical results. The effective nuclear charge depends on which electron is being considered and the approximation used.

1.18 Transition-metal configurations and ions

For transition elements, 4s electrons are generally removed before 3d electrons when cations form. Iron is commonly represented as [Ar]3d⁶4s²; Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁵. The simplified filling sequence is not a permanent ordering of orbital energies in every atom and ion. Chromium and copper are familiar exceptions to the simplest Aufbau prediction: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ respectively. The total energy depends on electron-nucleus attraction, electron-electron repulsion, exchange and correlation; half-filled and filled subshell descriptions alone are not a complete explanation.

1.19 Magnetic properties

Species with one or more unpaired electrons are paramagnetic and are attracted into a magnetic field. Species with all electrons paired are diamagnetic and are weakly repelled. In the spin-only approximation, the magnetic moment is μ = √[n(n + 2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons and BM denotes Bohr magnetons. The approximation is useful for many introductory transition-metal problems, although orbital contributions, spin-orbit coupling and covalency can affect measured values.

1.21 Deeper treatment of the hydrogen atom

The hydrogen atom is the most important exact starting point for atomic quantum theory because it contains one electron moving in the Coulomb field of a nucleus. In the simplest non-relativistic model, the potential energy is proportional to −1/r. The Schrödinger equation can be separated into radial and angular parts by using spherical polar coordinates because the potential has spherical symmetry. The angular solutions are spherical harmonics, while the radial solutions depend on n and l. This separation explains why the principal and angular momentum quantum numbers arise naturally in the solution rather than being arbitrary labels attached to a classical orbit.

The energy of a hydrogen-like atom depends principally on n in the non-relativistic Coulomb model. The degeneracy of states with different l but the same n is a special feature of the idealised Coulomb potential. In multi-electron atoms, electron-electron repulsion and shielding break this simple degeneracy, so orbitals with the same n but different l generally have different energies. Fine structure, relativistic effects, spin-orbit coupling and the Lamb shift introduce further corrections even in hydrogen. At introductory undergraduate level, the non-relativistic model is usually sufficient to explain the main shell structure and spectral series, but it is important to know what has been neglected.

The reduced mass of the electron-nucleus system is more accurate than simply using the electron mass because the nucleus also moves. For most introductory calculations, the difference is small, but high-precision spectroscopy must account for it. Isotopes of hydrogen therefore have slightly different spectral positions because the reduced mass changes with nuclear mass. This is one reason isotopic spectra can be distinguished with sufficiently high resolution.

1.22 Selection rules and spectral intensity

Not every mathematically possible pair of states produces a strong electric-dipole spectral line. In the simplest electric-dipole approximation for atomic transitions, a common orbital angular momentum selection rule is Δl = ±1, while the magnetic quantum number changes by Δmₗ = 0 or ±1 depending on the orientation and polarisation of the radiation. Spin is usually unchanged in an electric-dipole transition, so Δmₛ = 0. These rules explain why some transitions are allowed and others are forbidden or weak in a particular approximation. Forbidden does not always mean impossible: weaker multipole transitions or perturbations can make otherwise forbidden transitions observable.

Line intensity depends on the population of the initial state, the transition probability, the radiation field and experimental conditions. In thermal equilibrium, higher-energy states are generally less populated than lower-energy states, with populations described by Boltzmann statistics. In a discharge lamp or plasma, the population distribution may be non-equilibrium. Therefore, the brightest line is not necessarily the transition with the largest energy difference; it also depends on how many atoms occupy the starting state and the transition probability.

1.23 Quantum numbers and orbital capacity: derivation

For a given principal quantum number n, the allowed angular momentum quantum numbers are l = 0, 1, …, n−1. For each l, the magnetic quantum number has 2l+1 possible values. Each spatial orbital can accommodate two electrons with opposite spin projections. The total number of spatial orbitals in a shell is therefore the sum of (2l+1) over l from zero to n−1, which equals n². Since each orbital accommodates two electrons, the maximum shell capacity is 2n². This result provides a quick consistency check for shell populations.

For n = 3, l can be 0, 1 or 2. The 3s subshell has one orbital, 3p has three and 3d has five, giving nine spatial orbitals and a maximum of 18 electrons. For n = 4, the allowed subshells are 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f, containing 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 orbitals and a maximum of 32 electrons. The shell capacity is a mathematical maximum; it does not mean that every orbital in a shell is filled before an orbital in a higher shell begins to fill.

1.24 Spin, antisymmetry and the Pauli principle

Electron spin is an intrinsic quantum property with spin quantum number s = 1/2. A measurement of spin along a chosen axis gives one of two projections, conventionally labelled +1/2 and −1/2. The Pauli exclusion principle follows from the antisymmetric character of the total electronic wavefunction when two identical electrons are exchanged. In a single orbital, the spatial part is the same for two electrons, so their spin functions must differ. This gives the familiar opposite-spin pairing rule. The principle applies to all electrons, not just those in the same subshell.

Hund's rule is an energy principle for degenerate orbitals. For many-electron atoms, a configuration with maximum spin multiplicity is often lower in energy because of exchange stabilisation and reduced electron repulsion associated with the spatial distribution of parallel-spin electrons. The simple orbital-box diagrams are a useful representation, but the full explanation is quantum mechanical. Hund's rule is applied within a set of degenerate orbitals; it does not mean electrons always occupy different shells before pairing in a lower-energy subshell.

1.25 Effective use of electron configurations

Electron configurations allow chemists to predict valence electron counts, likely ion formation, magnetic behaviour and broad chemical similarities. For main-group elements, the outer s and p electrons are commonly treated as valence electrons. For transition elements, d electrons can also participate in bonding and oxidation-state changes. A configuration should always be written for the actual species being discussed: a neutral atom, a cation or an anion. For an anion, add electrons; for a cation, remove electrons from the appropriate outer orbitals. When writing transition-metal cations, remove the ns electrons before (n−1)d electrons in the usual ground-state treatment.

Core notation, such as [Ne]3s²3p⁵ for chlorine, abbreviates the filled inner-shell configuration of the preceding noble gas. It makes valence patterns easier to identify. However, a noble-gas core is a notational convenience, not a claim that the inner electrons are completely unaffected by chemical bonding. In advanced treatments, core polarisation and core-valence correlation can contribute to measurable properties.

Example: convert photon energy to molar energy

Calculate the energy in kJ mol⁻¹ for radiation of wavelength 400 nm. First convert λ = 400 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. 1.27 Additional worked calculation: hydrogen transition

Example: transition from n = 4 to n = 2

Using Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV, E₄ = −13.6/16 = −0.850 eV and E₂ = −13.6/4 = −3.40 eV. The atom loses energy ΔE = E₄ − E₂ = 2.55 eV in magnitude, so the emitted photon has energy 2.55 eV. Chapter 2: Periodic Properties of the Elements

2.1 Learning outcomes

You should be able to explain the modern periodic law, relate periodic position to electron configuration, and describe trends in atomic and ionic radii, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.

2.2 Modern periodic law and electronic structure

The modern periodic law states that the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The periodic table arranges elements by increasing atomic number. Periods correspond broadly to the highest occupied principal shell, while blocks reflect the type of subshell being filled. The s-, p-, d- and f-blocks therefore connect periodic position with electron configuration.

2.3 Effective nuclear charge and shielding

In a multi-electron atom, an outer electron experiences attraction to the nucleus reduced by repulsion from other electrons. This net attraction is often discussed using effective nuclear charge, Zeff. Shielding is not identical for all orbitals: penetration and orbital shape influence how strongly an electron experiences the nucleus. Across a period, Zeff generally increases for valence electrons, helping explain contraction in atomic size.

2.4 Atomic and ionic radii

Atomic radius is not a uniquely fixed boundary because electron clouds do not end sharply. Different operational definitions include covalent, metallic and van der Waals radii. Across a period, atomic radius generally decreases as effective nuclear charge increases. Down a group, radius generally increases because additional principal shells are occupied.

Cations are usually smaller than their parent atoms because electron loss reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the outer shell. Anions are usually larger because added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion within the valence shell.

2.5 First ionisation energy

First ionisation energy is the minimum energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state:

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group. The trend has exceptions due to subshell energies and electron pairing. For example, the first ionisation energy of boron is lower than that of beryllium because the electron removed from boron occupies a higher-energy 2p subshell. Oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen partly because pairing in one 2p orbital introduces additional repulsion.

2.6 Successive ionisation energies

Successive ionisation energies increase because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. A particularly large jump occurs after all valence electrons have been removed and the next electron must be taken from an inner shell. This jump can help infer the number of valence electrons.

Worked example 5: interpreting ionisation energies

An element has successive ionisation energies (arbitrary units): 580, 1820, 2740, 11 600. The large jump occurs between the third and fourth removals.

Interpretation: The atom most likely has three valence electrons. After these are removed, the fourth electron comes from a more stable inner shell. The element is therefore likely to be in Group 13 (Group IIIA in older notation), subject to checking the full data and chemical context.

2.7 Electron affinity and electronegativity

Electron affinity describes the energy change when a gaseous atom accepts an electron to form an anion. Sign conventions vary between textbooks: some report energy released as a positive magnitude, while thermodynamic conventions report an exothermic enthalpy change as negative. Always state the convention being used. Electron affinity trends contain important exceptions, including the relatively less favourable addition of an electron to a compact filled subshell.

Electronegativity is the relative tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electron density. It is a scale-dependent property, not the same quantity as electron affinity or ionisation energy. Fluorine is the most electronegative element on the commonly used Pauling scale.

2.10 Physical basis of periodicity

Periodicity is the recurrence of similar valence-shell patterns as atomic number increases. When a new period begins, electrons enter a new principal shell. As that shell fills, nuclear charge, shielding, penetration and electron pairing change systematically. Elements in the same group often have related valence configurations and therefore similarities in common oxidation states, bonding patterns and reactivity. These are trends rather than absolute rules; heavier p-block elements, transition elements and lanthanides show important deviations.

2.11 Effective nuclear charge and screening

Shielding describes the reduction in nuclear attraction experienced by an electron because other electrons repel it and partially screen the nucleus. Inner-shell electrons generally shield outer electrons more effectively than electrons in the same shell. Penetration describes the extent to which electron density reaches close to the nucleus. For orbitals of the same principal quantum number, s orbitals generally penetrate most strongly, followed by p, d and f. A more penetrating electron spends more time close to the nucleus and can experience greater effective attraction.

A useful introductory model is Zeff ≈ Z − S. It explains why an increase in proton number across a period is not completely cancelled by added electrons. However, effective nuclear charge is orbital-dependent. Slater-style screening rules provide estimates, not exact values, and should be described as approximations.

2.12 Atomic radii and their definitions

Atoms have diffuse electron clouds rather than sharp surfaces, so atomic radius is an operational quantity. Covalent radius is commonly inferred from half the internuclear distance between identical atoms joined by a covalent bond. Metallic radius is inferred from nearest-neighbour distances in a metal lattice. Van der Waals radius is based on non-bonded close contacts. These definitions give different numerical values, so comparisons should use the same radius definition and similar chemical environments.

Across a period, proton number increases while added electrons enter the same general valence shell. Shielding increases, but not enough to offset the increased nuclear charge; the electron cloud is pulled inward. Down a group, an additional principal shell is occupied and the valence electrons are generally farther from the nucleus, so radius increases. Transition-metal radii often change more gradually because d electrons provide some shielding while the d orbitals contract.

Lanthanide contraction is the gradual decrease in size across the lanthanide series. The 4f electrons shield the increasing nuclear charge relatively poorly, causing the outer electron cloud to contract. This influences the sizes and chemistry of elements after the lanthanides, including similarities between some 4d and 5d transition elements.

2.13 Ionic radii and isoelectronic species

A cation is generally smaller than its neutral parent because electron removal reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the outer shell. An anion is generally larger because added electrons increase repulsion in the valence region. Ionic radius also depends on oxidation state, coordination number, spin state and crystal structure. For isoelectronic species, electron number is fixed; increasing nuclear charge generally contracts the electron cloud.

Worked example: isoelectronic radius order

O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each contain ten electrons. Their proton numbers are 8, 9, 11 and 12 respectively. Mg²⁺ has the greatest nuclear charge and is smallest; O²⁻ has the smallest nuclear charge and is largest. Decreasing radius: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺.

2.14 First ionisation energy

First ionisation energy is the enthalpy change for removing one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground states to form gaseous singly charged cations. It is endothermic and positive under the usual convention. The gas-phase definition matters: ionisation energy is not the same as the work function of a solid or the energy needed to remove an electron from a solvated ion.

Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally increases and makes electron removal more difficult. Down a group, increased shell distance and shielding generally make removal easier. The trend has local exceptions. Boron has a lower first ionisation energy than beryllium because its first removed electron is in a higher-energy 2p subshell rather than 2s. Oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen partly because one 2p orbital contains a pair of electrons, increasing repulsion and making removal of one paired electron easier.

2.15 Successive ionisation energies

Successive ionisation energies refer to sequential electron removal from X, X⁺, X²⁺ and so forth. Each removal generally requires more energy because the remaining species becomes more positively charged. A large jump after a certain number of removals indicates that the next electron is being taken from a more tightly bound inner shell. This can help estimate the number of valence electrons. However, oxidation-state stability also depends on bonding, lattice enthalpy, hydration and other thermodynamic contributions.

2.16 Electron affinity and sign conventions

Electron affinity describes the energy change for X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). Some texts define electron affinity as energy released and report an exothermic process as a positive number; others report the enthalpy change, which is negative when energy is released. Follow the convention specified in the question. Trends are irregular because electron attachment depends on shell size, electron repulsion and subshell occupancy. Chlorine's electron attachment is more exothermic than fluorine's under common conventions because fluorine's compact 2p shell produces stronger repulsion for the incoming electron.

2.17 Electronegativity and bond polarity

Electronegativity is a relative measure of an atom's tendency to attract shared electron density in a chemical bond. The Pauling scale is derived from bond-energy comparisons; the Mulliken scale relates electronegativity to ionisation energy and electron affinity. Values from different scales should not be mixed without identifying the scale. Electronegativity is not identical to electron affinity, which concerns electron attachment to a gaseous atom.

A difference in electronegativity helps predict bond polarity. In a polar covalent bond, shared electron density shifts toward the more electronegative atom, producing partial charges δ− and δ+. There is no universal sharp boundary between ionic and covalent bonding. Molecular polarity also depends on geometry: polar bonds can cancel in a symmetrical molecule such as CO₂, whereas the bent structure of H₂O gives a net molecular dipole.

2.18 Metallic character, reactivity and oxides

Metallic character describes the tendency to lose electrons and exhibit metallic bonding and related physical properties. It generally increases down a group and toward the left of the periodic table. Alkali-metal reactivity generally increases down Group 1 because the outer electron becomes easier to remove. Halogen oxidising strength generally decreases down Group 17, although actual reactivity depends on the full reaction and conditions.

Across Period 3, oxide behaviour changes broadly from basic through amphoteric to acidic. Sodium and magnesium oxides are basic; aluminium oxide is amphoteric; silicon dioxide and many oxides of phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine are acidic. This reflects changes in bonding and the ability of oxides to react with acids, bases or water. Silicon dioxide is a network solid and does not simply dissolve in water to form a molecular acid.

2.19 Diagonal relationships and second-period elements

Some second-period elements resemble elements diagonally below them: lithium shares features with magnesium, beryllium with aluminium, and boron with silicon. These partial similarities arise partly because opposing trends in size and charge density can produce comparable polarising power and bonding behaviour. They do not mean the elements have identical chemistry.

Second-period elements are often distinctive because of their small size, high electronegativity and lack of low-energy valence d orbitals in the simple bonding model. They often follow the octet rule more closely than heavier congeners, although the octet rule is a useful model rather than an inviolable law.

2.20 Writing strong periodicity explanations

A strong answer links electron configuration to the property. To compare Na and Cl radii, state that both are in Period 3, proton number increases across the period and added electrons enter the same general shell. Shielding does not fully offset the extra nuclear charge, so effective attraction rises and radius generally decreases. To compare Na and K, identify the additional occupied shell in potassium and explain why its valence electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

For ionisation-energy exceptions, identify the subshell from which the electron is removed. The Mg/Al comparison requires discussion of 3s versus 3p electrons. The N/O comparison requires discussion of the paired 2p electron in oxygen. Avoid simply repeating a trend; explain the physical cause and connect it to the electron being considered.

Worked comparison: Na, Mg and Al

These elements occupy Period 3. Proton number increases from 11 to 12 to 13 while valence electrons enter the same principal shell. Effective nuclear attraction generally increases, so the broad radius order is Na > Mg > Al. The precise numerical values depend on the radius definition.

Worked comparison: Na and K ionisation energy

Na has a 3s valence electron; K has a 4s valence electron. The potassium electron is farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons. It is easier to remove, so IE₁(Na) is greater than IE₁(K).

Worked comparison: Be and B

Be is 1s²2s² and B is 1s²2s²2p¹. The electron removed from B is in the higher-energy, less penetrating 2p subshell. It is easier to remove than a 2s electron from Be, so B has the lower first ionisation energy despite its greater nuclear charge.

2.21 Quantitative interpretation of periodic data

Periodic trends are often tested using tables of measured radii, ionisation energies or electron affinities rather than asking students to repeat a memorised direction. Begin by checking what is being compared: are the species neutral atoms, ions, gaseous species or atoms in a solid? Are radii covalent, metallic or ionic? Are ionisation energies first or successive? Are electron affinities reported as released energy or enthalpy change? A correct chemical explanation can still produce an incorrect answer if the data definitions are misunderstood.

When values are plotted against atomic number, the broad periodic pattern may be interrupted by local irregularities. These irregularities are not necessarily experimental errors. They may reflect changes in subshell occupancy, electron pairing, orbital penetration or the type of radius being measured. A strong data interpretation identifies the overall trend, identifies deviations, and then uses electron structure to explain the deviations. Avoid drawing a straight-line relationship through data that clearly show a periodic pattern.

2.22 Isoelectronic series: deeper reasoning

An isoelectronic series contains atoms, ions or molecules with the same number of electrons. Because electron-electron repulsion and shell occupancy are broadly comparable, increasing nuclear charge tends to contract the electron cloud. This is a particularly clean comparison for ionic radii. However, the species may have different charges, different bonding environments and different coordination numbers in crystals. The simple size order is therefore most reliable when the compared ions are genuinely isoelectronic and their radii are reported using compatible coordination assumptions.

For example, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each contain ten electrons. Their nuclear charges rise from 8 to 12. The increasing attraction contracts the electron cloud in the order O²⁻ largest to Mg²⁺ smallest. This argument is different from comparing Na and K atoms, where the number of occupied shells changes. Always identify whether a comparison is isoelectronic or involves different shell structures.

2.23 Ionisation energy and thermochemical cycles

Ionisation energy is one contribution to the energetics of forming ionic compounds, but it is not the only contribution. Removing electrons from a gaseous atom costs energy, while adding electrons to gaseous atoms may release or absorb energy. Formation of a crystal lattice can release a substantial amount of energy, and hydration of ions in water can also be strongly exothermic. A Born–Haber cycle combines enthalpy changes such as atomisation, bond dissociation, ionisation, electron attachment and lattice formation to assess the enthalpy of formation of an ionic solid. This explains why a simple comparison of ionisation energies cannot by itself predict which ionic compound is most stable.

For a metal that forms a 2+ ion, the second ionisation energy is usually greater than the first. Whether formation of M²⁺ is favourable in a compound depends on whether the energy released in subsequent steps, such as lattice formation and solvation, compensates for the energy required to remove the second electron. This is why magnesium commonly forms Mg²⁺, whereas the second ionisation energy of sodium is so large that Na²⁺ is not a common stable ion in ordinary chemistry: after losing its 3s electron, sodium has a noble-gas configuration, and the next electron would be removed from an inner shell.

2.24 Periodic trends in chemical reactivity

Reactivity is not a single periodic property with one universal scale. For metals, a useful first approximation is to consider how readily atoms lose electrons, but bond formation, solvation and lattice energy also influence observed reactions. For non-metals, electron attraction and bond energies may matter, but reaction kinetics and molecular structure are also important. Alkali metals become more reactive down Group 1 in many reactions with water because the outer electron is more readily removed and the products are stabilised. Halogen oxidising ability generally decreases down Group 17 because the overall tendency to accept electrons changes with atomic size and related thermodynamic terms.

When comparing reactivity, specify the reaction. A metal may react rapidly with water but slowly with oxygen because the oxide layer protects the surface. A halogen's reaction with a particular halide ion depends on the relative oxidising strengths and the reaction conditions. Periodic trends help formulate predictions, but they do not replace balanced equations, thermodynamic reasoning or kinetic evidence.

2.25 Oxide acid-base behaviour across Period 3

Across Period 3, the character of common oxides changes from strongly basic for sodium and magnesium through amphoteric for aluminium to acidic for non-metals. Sodium oxide reacts with water to form sodium hydroxide. Magnesium oxide is basic but reacts with water less vigorously. Aluminium oxide reacts with both acids and strong bases, which is the defining behaviour of an amphoteric oxide. Silicon dioxide is an extended covalent network and reacts with strong bases under suitable conditions, but it does not behave like a soluble molecular oxide. Oxides of phosphorus and sulfur form acidic solutions or acids under appropriate conditions.

This trend is linked to changes in bonding and the polarising power of the cation or central atom. Highly electropositive metals form ionic oxides containing oxide ions that react with acids or water. Across the period, covalent character increases and non-metal oxides behave as acid anhydrides. The actual chemistry depends on oxidation state and structure: an element can form several oxides with different properties, and not every oxide fits a simple one-word classification.

2.26 Electronegativity, bond polarity and molecular dipoles

Electronegativity differences help predict bond dipoles, but the molecular dipole is a vector sum of all bond dipoles and any relevant lone-pair contributions. In CO₂, each C=O bond is polar, but the linear geometry makes the two bond dipoles equal and opposite, so the molecule has no permanent dipole moment. In H₂O, the O–H bonds are polar and the bent geometry prevents cancellation, giving a net dipole. In NH₃, the trigonal-pyramidal geometry also gives a net dipole. Therefore, one cannot infer molecular polarity from electronegativity difference alone; molecular shape is essential.

Bond polarity is also influenced by the surrounding chemical environment. The same element can have different effective electronegativities in different bonding situations, and oxidation state can influence electron distribution. Electronegativity values are useful comparative scales rather than exact fixed charges assigned to atoms.

2.27 Periodicity in transition elements

Transition elements show trends in atomic and ionic size, oxidation states, colour, magnetism and complex formation. Their d orbitals are progressively occupied across a series, and the energy differences between d orbitals can be affected by ligand fields. Several oxidation states are common because ns and (n−1)d electrons can have comparable energies. The first-row transition elements often show a gradual decrease in atomic radius across the series, but the pattern is not perfectly smooth. Their ions may have different radii depending on oxidation state and spin state.

Transition-metal ions are often coloured because electronic transitions can occur between d-derived energy levels, although not all d-electron configurations produce visible colour. Magnetic behaviour depends on the number of unpaired electrons and on whether a complex is high-spin or low-spin. These properties lead naturally from periodic trends into coordination chemistry, ligand-field theory and spectroscopy.

2.28 Periodicity in the p-block and inert-pair behaviour

Heavier p-block elements may show oxidation states that differ from the simple group valence count. In several heavier groups, the lower oxidation state becomes increasingly stable down the group. This is often discussed using the inert-pair effect, which describes the tendency of the outer ns² pair to participate less readily in bonding for heavier p-block elements. Relativistic effects, orbital energies and bonding contributions all influence this behaviour. The phrase “inert pair” is a useful descriptive model, but a full explanation requires more detailed electronic structure.

For example, the +1 oxidation state becomes important for heavier Group 13 elements, while +2 becomes important for heavier Group 14 elements. The stability of oxidation states depends on the compound and its bonding; the trend should not be used as an absolute rule for every chemical environment.

2.30 Chapter synthesis

Periodic trends are best understood as connected consequences of electronic structure. Nuclear charge supplies the attractive force; shielding and penetration determine how much of that force a valence electron experiences; shell number influences distance; and electron-electron interactions modify the simple picture. Atomic size, ionisation energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, metallic character and chemical reactivity are different properties, but they are linked through these underlying factors. A rigorous answer identifies the property, states the relevant electron configuration, explains the balance of attraction and repulsion, and notes important exceptions or limitations.

1.29 Connecting atomic structure to chemical bonding

Atomic structure is not an isolated topic: it provides the language needed to explain chemical bonding. When atoms approach, their electron wavefunctions overlap. Depending on the relative phase and symmetry of the orbitals, combinations can be bonding or antibonding. A bonding combination concentrates electron density between nuclei and can lower the energy of the system; an antibonding combination introduces a node between nuclei and generally raises the energy. The number of molecular orbitals formed equals the number of atomic orbitals combined in the simple linear-combination-of-atomic-orbitals model. Electrons fill the resulting molecular orbitals according to energy, Pauli exclusion and Hund's rule.

Valence-shell configurations help identify which atomic orbitals are available for bonding. For example, carbon has four valence electrons and can form four covalent bonds in many stable compounds. Nitrogen has five valence electrons and commonly forms three bonds with one lone pair in simple Lewis structures. Oxygen has six valence electrons and commonly forms two bonds with two lone pairs. These patterns are useful starting points, but molecular orbital theory, formal charge, resonance and molecular geometry are required for a fuller description.

2.31 Connecting periodicity to bonding and structure

Periodic properties influence the type and strength of chemical bonding. Large differences in electronegativity often correlate with substantial charge separation, but ionic character is a continuum rather than a binary category. Small, highly charged cations can strongly polarise anions, increasing covalent character in compounds that might otherwise be described as ionic. This is one reason that simple rules based only on electronegativity difference are useful for introductory prediction but cannot replace structural evidence.

Atomic and ionic size influence lattice structures, coordination numbers and the relative stability of different crystal arrangements. Charge density, often discussed qualitatively as charge divided by ionic volume, helps explain polarising power. A small cation with a high positive charge can distort the electron cloud of a large anion. The resulting redistribution of electron density affects bond character and physical properties. This connects periodic trends with Fajans' rules, solid-state chemistry and the structures of ionic materials.

Periodic trends also influence acid-base behaviour. The charge and size of a metal cation affect the acidity of hydrated metal ions because a small, highly charged cation can polarise coordinated water molecules and promote proton release. Across a period, changes in oxide bonding help explain the shift from basic to acidic oxides. In aqueous chemistry, however, solubility, hydrolysis, oxidation state and complex formation must also be considered.

2.32 Exam-style synthesis: constructing an evidence-based explanation

When asked to rank elements or ions, first write their electron configurations. Identify whether the comparison changes shell number, nuclear charge, subshell occupancy or electron count. State the dominant factor and then discuss exceptions. For an isoelectronic series, compare nuclear charge at fixed electron count. For elements in one group, compare principal shell and shielding. For neighbouring elements in one period, compare effective nuclear charge and subshell occupancy. For an ionisation-energy anomaly, identify whether the removed electron comes from a new subshell or a paired orbital. For molecular polarity, combine electronegativity with molecular geometry. This sequence of reasoning is more reliable than memorising isolated arrows on a periodic table.

In laboratory and data-analysis work, always identify the experimental conditions and the operational definition of a property. Ionic radii depend on coordination environment; ionisation energies refer to gaseous species; electron-affinity sign conventions vary; and electronegativity depends on the chosen scale. State these limitations where they affect the conclusion. The best undergraduate answers are precise about both the prediction and the evidence supporting it.

Undergraduate inorganic and physical chemistry · Core lecture chapter

Scope: This chapter develops bonding from electrostatics and quantum mechanics through Lewis theory, ionic solids, valence-bond theory, molecular orbital theory, coordination bonding, intermolecular forces and structure–property relationships. It is intended for first- and second-year undergraduate Chemistry. Models are introduced with their assumptions and limitations; no single bonding model explains every chemical observation.

3.1 Learning outcomes

After studying this chapter, students should be able to explain why atoms form bonds in terms of energy and electron distribution; distinguish ionic, covalent, metallic and coordinate bonding without treating them as mutually exclusive categories; construct Lewis structures and calculate formal charges; apply resonance; use VSEPR theory to predict molecular geometry; explain hybridisation as a useful orbital model; distinguish sigma and pi bonding; compare valence-bond and molecular-orbital descriptions; construct qualitative MO diagrams for diatomic species; calculate bond order and relate it to bond length, strength and magnetism; explain molecular polarity; analyse hydrogen bonding and other intermolecular forces; describe lattice enthalpy and Born–Haber cycles; and connect bonding to conductivity, hardness, solubility, melting behaviour and spectroscopy.

3.2 Why do atoms bond?

A chemical bond is a stabilising interaction between atoms or ions that produces a lower-energy arrangement than the separated species under the same conditions. The phrase “atoms want full shells” is a mnemonic, not a physical explanation. A more fundamental account considers the total energy of the system: nuclear–nuclear repulsion, electron–electron repulsion, electron–nuclear attraction, electron kinetic energy and the surrounding environment. A bond forms when an arrangement of nuclei and electrons gives a favourable total energy and a potential-energy minimum at a finite internuclear distance.

Consider two hydrogen atoms approaching. At large separation, each atom is approximately independent. As they approach, each electron is attracted to both nuclei. This cross-attraction can lower electronic energy. At very short separation, nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron repulsions, together with the rise in kinetic energy associated with confining electrons, become dominant. The competition creates an equilibrium separation. Bond dissociation energy is the energy required to separate bonded atoms into specified products, commonly gas-phase atoms in a homolytic process. Its value depends on the defined process and reference states.

Bond formation is not guaranteed merely because a pair of atoms can attract. The energy change must be favourable in the relevant environment, and entropy and temperature influence whether a process is thermodynamically favourable. In reactions, bonds are broken and formed in a coupled process. Breaking a bond requires energy; forming a bond releases energy. The net enthalpy change can be estimated from average bond enthalpies, but such estimates are approximate because a bond enthalpy depends on the molecular environment.

3.3 Bonding descriptions and the continuum of bond character

Ionic bonding is commonly described as electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Covalent bonding involves shared electron density between nuclei. Metallic bonding describes delocalised valence electrons interacting with a lattice of positive ion cores. Coordinate or dative covalent bonding is a covalent interaction in which both electrons in a shared pair are formally donated by one atom or ligand. These are useful limiting descriptions. Real materials may show mixed character: many salts have polarisation and covalent contributions, polar covalent molecules have partial charges, and metallic solids may show directional or covalent contributions.

Electronegativity difference is one guide to bond polarity, but it is not a universal switch dividing all bonds into ionic and covalent categories. Electron density, oxidation states, lattice environment, coordination number and polarising power all matter. In a molecule, bond polarity is a vector property: bond dipoles must be combined according to molecular geometry. A molecule can contain polar bonds yet have zero net dipole moment if the vector contributions cancel.

3.4 Electrostatic foundations: Coulomb's law

The electrostatic potential energy between two point charges q₁ and q₂ separated by distance r in a medium of permittivity ε is U = q₁q₂/(4πεr). Opposite charges have negative interaction energy under the usual zero-at-infinite-separation convention; like charges have positive interaction energy. In vacuum, ε is replaced by ε₀. The force magnitude follows the inverse-square law. These expressions explain why ion–ion attraction increases with charge magnitude and decreases with separation.

For a crystal, one cannot calculate stability by considering a single cation–anion pair alone. Each ion interacts with many neighbours, including ions of the same and opposite charge. The total electrostatic energy is a lattice sum. The Madelung constant summarises the geometrical contribution for a particular crystal arrangement, while ionic charges and interionic distance determine the scale of the electrostatic term. Short-range repulsion must also be included because electron clouds cannot be compressed without limit.

U = q₁q₂ / (4πε₀r)  (vacuum, point-charge model)

Worked example 3.1: electrostatic energy of an ion pair

Estimate the Coulomb potential energy for a hypothetical Na⁺–Cl⁻ pair separated by 0.280 nm in vacuum. Use e = 1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and 1/(4πε₀) = 8.988 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻².

Convert distance: r = 0.280 nm = 2.80 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Since q₁ = +e and q₂ = −e, U = −(8.988 × 10⁹)(1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹)²/(2.80 × 10⁻¹⁰).

U ≈ −8.23 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per ion pair. Multiplying by Avogadro's constant gives approximately −495 kJ mol⁻¹ for this isolated pair model.

Interpretation: The negative sign indicates attraction relative to infinite separation. This is not the lattice enthalpy of NaCl: a real crystal contains many interacting ions, and short-range repulsion and thermochemical conventions must be considered.

3.5 Lewis structures: valence electrons and connectivity

Lewis structures are electron-counting diagrams. Dots represent valence electrons and lines represent shared electron pairs. They are valuable because they provide a compact first description of connectivity, formal charge and lone-pair placement. They do not show the true three-dimensional electron density, and a line should not be interpreted as a rigid rod. A systematic method reduces errors: count total valence electrons, adjust for charge, choose a plausible skeletal arrangement, connect atoms with single bonds, complete terminal-atom octets where appropriate, place remaining electrons on the central atom, and form multiple bonds if needed to improve electron counts and formal charges.

Hydrogen is normally assigned two electrons in its valence shell. Second-period elements such as carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine commonly satisfy an octet in simple Lewis structures. Beryllium and boron compounds can be electron-deficient, while elements in the third period and beyond are sometimes represented by expanded-valence Lewis structures. Expanded-octet notation is useful bookkeeping, but it should not be interpreted as proof that central atoms use large populations of low-energy d orbitals to form ordinary hypervalent bonds. Modern descriptions often invoke delocalised molecular orbitals and polar bonding.

3.6 Formal charge and choosing among Lewis structures

Formal charge is an electron-bookkeeping quantity calculated by assigning all bonding electrons equally between bonded atoms. It is not necessarily the actual partial charge. For an atom, formal charge = valence electrons in the free atom − nonbonding electrons − one-half of bonding electrons. A useful alternative is FC = V − N − B, where V is the valence-electron count, N is the number of nonbonding electrons and B is the number of bonds when each bond is counted as one pair. The sum of formal charges must equal the overall charge of the species.

When several valid Lewis structures are possible, favour arrangements that give appropriate octets where possible, minimise the magnitude of formal charges, place negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms and avoid unnecessary charge separation. These are guidelines rather than absolute laws. For some species, an electron-deficient central atom is more realistic than an artificial structure that forces an octet by assigning implausible charge distribution.

Worked example 3.2: formal charges in nitrate

For one resonance contributor of NO₃⁻, draw nitrogen central, one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds. Nitrogen has four bonding pairs and no lone pair; the double-bonded oxygen has two lone pairs; each single-bonded oxygen has three lone pairs.

Nitrogen: V = 5, N = 0, B = 4, so FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Double-bonded oxygen: V = 6, N = 4, B = 2, so FC = 0. Each single-bonded oxygen: V = 6, N = 6, B = 1, so FC = −1. Total = +1 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −1, matching the ion charge. Three equivalent contributors place the double bond at different oxygen atoms.

3.7 Resonance and electron delocalisation

Resonance is used when one Lewis structure cannot adequately represent the electron distribution. Resonance contributors have the same arrangement of nuclei but differ in the placement of electrons. The actual quantum-mechanical state is a delocalised state that cannot be represented by any one contributor alone. It is not rapidly switching between drawings. Equivalent resonance contributors contribute equally in the idealised model. Non-equivalent contributors contribute unequally, with more stable contributors generally having greater weight. Contributors conserve the total electron count and nuclear framework. Moving a lone pair or π pair is allowed; moving an atom or changing the sigma-bond skeleton is not resonance.

Resonance is central to explaining equal C–O bond lengths in carbonate, equal N–O distances in nitrate and the unusual stability of aromatic systems. In carbonate, the three C–O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid even though an individual Lewis contributor depicts one double bond and two single bonds. The average bond order in a simple three-contributor description is 4/3. This is not a claim that the bonds physically alternate between single and double forms.

3.8 Octet-rule exceptions and electron counting

The octet rule is a useful empirical pattern for many main-group compounds, not a universal law. Electron-deficient species such as BF₃ and BeCl₂ have fewer than eight electrons around the central atom in simple Lewis representations. Odd-electron species such as NO and ClO₂ cannot give every atom a conventional octet because the total electron count is odd. Hypervalent species such as SF₆ and XeF₄ are often drawn with more than eight electrons around the central atom, but their bonding is better understood using molecular orbitals and polarised multicentre descriptions rather than a literal expansion of an atomic valence shell.

For an odd-electron molecule, place the unpaired electron where it gives a chemically reasonable structure and then examine formal charges. Radical character can influence reactivity, magnetic properties and spectroscopy. In transition-metal chemistry, the octet rule is often not the most useful organising principle: d-electron counts, ligand-field splitting, oxidation state and metal–ligand orbital interactions provide a more relevant framework.

3.9 Ionic bonding and lattice enthalpy

An ionic solid is a three-dimensional arrangement of cations and anions held together by electrostatic interactions. The term ionic bond does not mean that one isolated pair of ions forms a discrete molecule in the crystal. The formula unit expresses the simplest stoichiometric ratio. Ionic solids often have high melting points, are brittle and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved if mobile ions are present. They are generally poor electrical conductors as crystalline solids because their ions are fixed at lattice sites.

Lattice enthalpy has two common sign conventions. It may mean the enthalpy released when gaseous ions form one mole of an ionic solid, which is exothermic and negative, or the enthalpy required to separate one mole of solid into gaseous ions, which is endothermic and positive. Always state the convention. The magnitude tends to increase with larger ionic charges and smaller interionic distances, but crystal structure and polarisation also matter.

Worked example 3.3: qualitative comparison of lattice enthalpy

Compare expected lattice enthalpy magnitudes of NaCl and MgO. NaCl contains singly charged ions; MgO contains Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions. The product of charge magnitudes is 1 for NaCl and 4 for MgO. The Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions are also relatively small. Coulombic attraction is therefore much stronger in MgO, so its lattice enthalpy magnitude is substantially larger. A precise numerical comparison requires a thermochemical cycle or solid-state calculation, not just charge multiplication.

3.10 Born–Haber cycles and thermochemical reasoning

A Born–Haber cycle applies Hess's law to relate the enthalpy of formation of an ionic solid to atomisation or sublimation, bond dissociation, ionisation energy, electron affinity and lattice enthalpy. The cycle is not a physical mechanism in which the solid must literally form through that exact sequence. It is a thermochemical accounting route connecting initial and final states. Because enthalpy is a state function, the total enthalpy change is independent of the path.

For a generic MX solid formed from M(s) and one-half X₂(g), the cycle may include sublimation of M, dissociation of X₂, ionisation of M(g), electron addition to X(g), and formation of MX(s) from gaseous ions. For compounds with multivalent ions, successive ionisation energies and electron affinities must be included with appropriate stoichiometric factors. Electron affinity conventions require care: some tables report energy released as a positive magnitude, while thermodynamic enthalpy changes for exothermic electron attachment are negative.

Worked example 3.4: Born–Haber calculation for a hypothetical MX

Suppose ΔH°f[MX(s)] = −410 kJ mol⁻¹, metal sublimation = +108, one-half X₂ bond dissociation = +122, first ionisation energy = +496, and electron attachment enthalpy = −349 kJ mol⁻¹. Let lattice formation enthalpy be L.

Hess's law gives −410 = 108 + 122 + 496 − 349 + L. The sum of known terms is +377 kJ mol⁻¹, so L = −410 − 377 = −787 kJ mol⁻¹.

Answer: L = −787 kJ mol⁻¹ for the convention in which gaseous ions form the solid. The reverse lattice dissociation enthalpy is +787 kJ mol⁻¹.

3.11 Covalent bonding and orbital overlap

In a covalent bond, electron density is concentrated in a region that interacts favourably with more than one nucleus. In valence-bond theory, a bond is described in terms of overlap between atomic orbitals and pairing of electrons with opposite spin. Effective overlap requires suitable spatial orientation, comparable orbital energies and compatible symmetry. Greater overlap often corresponds to stronger bonding, although total bond energy also depends on electron repulsion, orbital energy and the rest of the molecular framework.

End-on overlap along the internuclear axis produces a sigma (σ) bond. Side-on overlap of appropriately oriented p orbitals produces a pi (π) bond. A single bond is normally one σ bond. A double bond contains one σ and one π bond; a triple bond contains one σ and two mutually perpendicular π bonds. Sigma electron density is cylindrically symmetric around the bond axis in the simplest diatomic description. Pi electron density occurs above and below, or on opposite sides of, the internuclear axis and has a nodal plane containing that axis.

3.12 Valence-bond theory and hybridisation

Hybridisation is a valence-bond model in which atomic orbitals on the same atom are combined mathematically to produce directional hybrid orbitals. It rationalises local geometry and equivalent bonds in many main-group molecules. Common labels include sp, sp² and sp³, associated in simple idealised cases with linear, trigonal-planar and tetrahedral arrangements. These labels are not directly observable objects; they are a model-dependent representation of the molecular wavefunction.

In an sp model, one s and one p orbital combine to form two oppositely directed hybrids. In an sp² model, one s and two p orbitals form three coplanar hybrids separated by approximately 120°, leaving one p orbital available for π bonding. In an sp³ model, one s and three p orbitals form four directional hybrids pointing approximately toward tetrahedral vertices. Actual bond angles can differ because lone pairs, substituent electronegativity, ring constraints and electron delocalisation alter the electron distribution. Hybridisation should not be used as a substitute for analysing geometry and bonding evidence.

Worked example 3.5: hybridisation and geometry in ethene

Each carbon in ethene, H₂C=CH₂, forms three σ bonds: two C–H and one C–C. Three electron domains around each carbon give a trigonal-planar arrangement. In the conventional valence-bond description, each carbon is sp² hybridised. The remaining unhybridised p orbital on each carbon overlaps side-on to form the π component of the C=C double bond. Rotation about the double bond would disrupt this π overlap, so the molecule does not rotate freely like an isolated single bond.

3.13 Molecular geometry: VSEPR theory

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory predicts molecular shape by considering repulsions among regions of electron density around a central atom. Electron domains arrange themselves to reduce repulsion. A multiple bond is counted as one domain for basic geometry prediction, although it may exert a somewhat stronger repulsive influence than a single bond. Lone pairs occupy more space near the central atom than bonding pairs in the simple model, leading to the qualitative order lone pair–lone pair greater than lone pair–bond pair greater than bond pair–bond pair.

Two domains give a linear arrangement; three give trigonal planar; four give tetrahedral; five give trigonal bipyramidal; and six give octahedral. Molecular shape is defined by nuclear positions, not lone-pair locations. Thus NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal even though its electron-domain geometry is tetrahedral, while H₂O is bent. In trigonal bipyramidal arrangements, lone pairs often occupy equatorial positions because this reduces 90° interactions. VSEPR is qualitative and less reliable for some transition-metal complexes, delocalised systems and molecules with strongly different ligand effects.

3.15 Bond angles and deviations from ideal geometry

Idealised angles are reference values, not guaranteed experimental results. In NH₃, one lone pair compresses the H–N–H angle relative to tetrahedral methane. In H₂O, two lone pairs produce a still smaller H–O–H angle. The exact angle reflects the complete electronic structure, not a simple count alone. Substituents with different electronegativities can also change electron density around the central atom. Ligand size, ring strain and multiple-bond character may cause further deviations.

For five-coordinate molecules, axial bonds in a trigonal bipyramid experience three 90° interactions, while equatorial bonds experience two. A lone pair often prefers an equatorial site because it has fewer 90° interactions. In six-coordinate octahedral arrangements, two lone pairs often occupy trans positions. These preferences explain common molecular shapes but should be treated as qualitative reasoning, not an exact energy calculation.

3.16 Molecular polarity and dipole moments

A dipole moment measures charge separation. For a simple pair of point charges, μ = qr. Molecular dipole moments are vector sums of bond dipoles and contributions from the full electron distribution. The SI unit is coulomb metre; the debye is a common molecular unit, where 1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. In a polar bond, the more electronegative atom generally carries partial negative charge and the less electronegative atom partial positive charge. The dipole arrow convention points toward the negative end, with a cross at the positive end in common chemistry notation.

Carbon dioxide contains polar C=O bonds but is linear, so the two equal bond dipoles cancel. Water has polar O–H bonds arranged in a bent geometry, so the dipoles do not cancel. BF₃ has polar B–F bonds but an ideal trigonal-planar arrangement with three equivalent bonds, giving zero net dipole. Molecular polarity therefore requires both bond polarity and three-dimensional shape.

Worked example 3.8: dipole moment of a simple diatomic molecule

A hypothetical diatomic molecule has an effective charge separation of 0.20e over 0.120 nm. Estimate its dipole moment in debye. μ = qr = (0.20 × 1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)(1.20 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) = 3.845 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. Convert to debye: μ = (3.845 × 10⁻³⁰)/(3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰) = 1.15 D. The effective charge is a model parameter and should not automatically be equated with an experimentally measured oxidation state.

3.17 Molecular orbital theory: the central idea

Molecular orbital (MO) theory treats electrons as occupying orbitals that extend over the molecule. Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals when their energies and symmetries are compatible and their overlap is significant. Combining two atomic orbitals produces two molecular orbitals: a bonding combination with increased electron density between nuclei and an antibonding combination with a node between nuclei. Antibonding orbitals are marked with an asterisk, such as σ* or π*.

Electrons occupy molecular orbitals according to the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle and Hund's rule. Lower-energy orbitals fill first; each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spin; and degenerate orbitals are singly occupied with parallel spins before pairing. Bond order is one-half the difference between the number of electrons in bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals. A positive bond order indicates net bonding in the elementary MO model. Larger bond order often corresponds to a shorter and stronger bond, although comparisons across different molecules require care.

3.18 Bonding and antibonding combinations

For two identical atomic orbitals, the in-phase combination often produces a bonding MO, while the out-of-phase combination produces an antibonding MO. The bonding orbital has constructive overlap and electron density between nuclei. The antibonding orbital has destructive interference and a nodal surface between nuclei. Occupation of a bonding orbital stabilises the molecule relative to the separated-atom reference, while occupation of an antibonding orbital reduces that stabilisation.

Not all combinations are equally important. Orbitals far apart in energy mix less strongly; orbitals with incompatible symmetry may not interact at all. In heteronuclear molecules, atomic orbitals differ in energy, and the bonding MO is usually weighted more toward the lower-energy atomic orbital, often associated with the more electronegative atom. This provides an MO explanation for polar covalent bonding.

3.19 Molecular orbital diagrams for second-period diatomics

For homonuclear second-period diatomics, the ordering of 2p-derived molecular orbitals changes across the series. For B₂, C₂ and N₂, s–p mixing makes the π(2p) orbitals lower in energy than σ(2p). For O₂, F₂ and Ne₂, the σ(2p) orbital is lower than the π(2p) set. The 2s-derived σ and σ* orbitals lie below the 2p-derived orbitals. The correct ordering should be selected for the region of the periodic sequence rather than memorising one universal diagram.

The MO model explains why O₂ is paramagnetic: its two highest-energy electrons occupy separate degenerate π* orbitals, leaving two unpaired electrons. A simple Lewis structure with a conventional O=O double bond does not predict this magnetic property. This is a classic demonstration of the explanatory power of molecular orbital theory. In contrast, N₂ has all electrons paired and a bond order of three in the elementary MO treatment.

Worked example 3.9: bond order and magnetism of O₂

O₂ has 12 valence electrons. In the standard valence MO filling, the bonding orbitals contain eight electrons and antibonding orbitals contain four. Bond order = (8 − 4)/2 = 2. The two electrons in degenerate π* orbitals are unpaired, so O₂ is paramagnetic. The result is consistent with an overall bond order of two but adds magnetic information unavailable from a simple localised Lewis drawing.

3.20 Bond order calculations for common diatomic species

In the elementary MO model, H₂ has two electrons in σ1s and none in σ*1s, giving bond order one. H₂⁺ has one electron in σ1s and bond order one-half. He₂ would have two electrons in σ1s and two in σ*1s, giving bond order zero; a stable ordinary covalent He₂ molecule is therefore not predicted by this simple ground-state model. For N₂, the valence MO count gives bond order three. For O₂, the bond order is two. Adding or removing electrons from frontier orbitals can strengthen or weaken a bond depending on whether the orbital is bonding or antibonding.

Worked example 3.10: comparing O₂, O₂⁺ and O₂⁻

O₂ has bond order 2. Removing one electron from O₂ to form O₂⁺ removes it from a π* antibonding orbital, so bond order increases by one-half to 2.5. Adding one electron to form O₂⁻ places it in a π* antibonding orbital, so bond order decreases by one-half to 1.5. The simple model therefore predicts O₂⁺ to have a shorter, stronger bond than O₂, and O₂⁻ to have a longer, weaker bond. Experimental bond lengths broadly support this trend.

3.21 Bond length, bond strength and bond order

Bond length is the internuclear distance at the minimum of the molecular potential-energy curve. Bond dissociation energy is the energy required for a defined dissociation process. In a series of related bonds between the same atoms, increasing bond order usually decreases bond length and increases bond strength: a typical C–C single bond is longer than a C=C double bond, which is longer than a C≡C triple bond. Bond length is also affected by atomic size, resonance, polarity, hybridisation, ring strain and the electronic environment.

Average bond enthalpies are useful for estimating reaction enthalpies. For a reaction, ΔH ≈ Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed). The estimate is approximate because tabulated values are averages over different chemical environments and usually refer to gas-phase molecules. Use measured formation enthalpies or more specific thermochemical data when precision is required.

Worked example 3.11: estimating a reaction enthalpy from bond enthalpies

For H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, use approximate bond enthalpies H–H = 436, Cl–Cl = 243 and H–Cl = 431 kJ mol⁻¹. Bonds broken: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ mol⁻¹. Bonds formed: 2 × 431 = 862 kJ mol⁻¹. ΔH ≈ 679 − 862 = −183 kJ mol⁻¹.

Answer: estimated exothermic enthalpy change −183 kJ mol⁻¹. The value is an estimate because bond enthalpies are average values; standard enthalpies of formation give a more precise value for specified conditions.

3.22 Metallic bonding and band theory

In a metal, valence electrons are not confined to individual two-centre bonds. They are delocalised through the solid and interact with the periodic array of atomic cores. This delocalisation helps explain electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability and ductility. When a metal is deformed, layers of atoms can move while the electron distribution continues to provide cohesion, unlike a brittle ionic lattice in which displacement can bring like-charged ions into close proximity.

Band theory extends molecular-orbital ideas to a very large number of atoms. As atoms approach to form a solid, many closely spaced molecular orbitals combine into bands. The valence band and conduction band may overlap, be partially filled or be separated by an energy gap. Metals have partially filled bands or overlapping bands that permit electrons to respond to an applied field. Insulators have a large band gap; semiconductors have a smaller gap and conductivity can be modified by temperature, light or doping.

3.23 Intermolecular forces: an overview

Intermolecular forces are interactions between separate molecules or molecular units. They are generally weaker than covalent or ionic bonds, but collectively they govern many bulk properties: boiling and melting points, viscosity, surface tension, solubility, crystal packing and biomolecular recognition. The distinction between intramolecular bonds and intermolecular forces is important when analysing phase changes. Boiling a molecular liquid usually separates molecules by overcoming intermolecular attractions; it does not normally break covalent bonds within each molecule.

The principal categories include London dispersion forces, permanent dipole–dipole interactions, dipole-induced dipole interactions and hydrogen bonding. Ion–dipole interactions are important in solutions of ionic compounds. These forces can operate simultaneously. A polar molecule also experiences dispersion forces, and a large nonpolar molecule may have substantial dispersion interactions. Relative importance depends on polarizability, molecular shape, dipole moment, temperature and distance.

3.24 London dispersion forces

London dispersion forces arise from instantaneous fluctuations in electron density that create temporary dipoles and induce correlated dipoles in neighbouring particles. They occur in all atoms and molecules, including nonpolar substances and noble gases. Dispersion interactions become stronger as electron clouds become more polarizable, often increasing with electron count and molecular size. Shape also matters: extended molecules can have greater contact area than compact, roughly spherical molecules of similar molar mass.

Dispersion forces are not simply weak in every context. In large molecules and condensed phases, many dispersion interactions act together and can make a major contribution to cohesion. They are important in boiling-point trends of noble gases and halogens, packing of hydrocarbons, adsorption on surfaces and stability of molecular crystals. Molecular polarizability is related to how readily its electron cloud is distorted by an electric field.

3.25 Dipole–dipole and dipole-induced dipole interactions

Permanent dipole–dipole interactions occur between polar molecules. Their orientation-dependent electrostatic interactions favour arrangements in which opposite partial charges are near one another. Thermal motion competes with this ordering, so the average interaction depends on temperature and molecular mobility. A polar molecule can also induce a dipole in a nonpolar but polarizable neighbour, producing a dipole-induced dipole attraction.

Simple point-dipole models are useful at distances large compared with molecular dimensions, but real molecules have distributed charge, anisotropic polarizability and multiple interaction sites. Therefore, boiling-point comparisons should not be based solely on whether a molecule is polar. Molecular mass, surface area, hydrogen bonding and packing may be equally or more important.

3.26 Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is an important directional interaction involving a hydrogen atom bonded to an electronegative donor atom and an acceptor with an available electron pair or suitable electron density. Common introductory examples involve O–H, N–H or F–H donors. Acceptors often contain lone pairs on oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine, although chemical context matters. Hydrogen bonds influence water's unusual properties, protein secondary structure, nucleic-acid base pairing, crystal structures and molecular recognition.

Hydrogen bonds vary in strength and geometry. They are often strongest when donor–hydrogen–acceptor atoms are close to linear, but bent interactions can still be significant. The term should not be used for every close contact involving hydrogen. The donor bond polarises hydrogen, while the acceptor provides an electron-rich region. Modern descriptions include electrostatic, induction, dispersion and partial covalent contributions. Hydrogen bonding is not a conventional covalent bond, but it can be strong enough to dominate molecular organisation.

Worked example 3.12: explaining boiling-point trends

Explain why water has a much higher boiling point than H₂S, although their molar masses are of the same order. Water molecules form an extensive network of hydrogen bonds because oxygen is highly electronegative and each molecule can participate as both donor and acceptor. Separating molecules into the gas phase requires substantial energy to disrupt this network. H₂S is less polar and does not form comparably strong conventional hydrogen bonds; its cohesion is dominated by dispersion and weaker dipolar interactions. The difference is therefore not explained by molar mass alone.

3.27 Ion–dipole interactions and solvation

Ion–dipole interactions occur between an ion and a polar molecule. They are important when ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water. A cation attracts the partially negative end of a solvent dipole; an anion attracts the partially positive end. Solvent molecules organise around the ion to form a solvation shell. In water, this is hydration. The stability of solvated ions competes with the energy required to separate ions in the crystal lattice.

Dissolution is favourable when the overall Gibbs energy change is negative, not merely when hydration enthalpy is large. Enthalpy and entropy both contribute. A salt can dissolve endothermically if the entropy contribution is sufficiently favourable, or dissolve exothermically but only modestly if other contributions oppose dissolution. Solubility also depends on temperature, pressure for gases, common ions, complex formation and solution composition.

3.28 Solubility and “like dissolves like”

The phrase “like dissolves like” is a rough guide: polar solvents often dissolve polar or ionic solutes, while nonpolar solvents often dissolve nonpolar substances. A deeper explanation compares free-energy changes associated with disrupting solute–solute and solvent–solvent interactions and forming solute–solvent interactions. Entropy of mixing and structural changes in the solvent are also important. Thus, polarity alone does not fully predict solubility.

Hydrocarbons are generally poorly soluble in water because they cannot compensate favourably for disruption of water's hydrogen-bond network and organisation of water around nonpolar surfaces. Amphiphilic molecules contain both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions and can assemble into micelles or bilayers. These structures are central to detergency, membranes and biological chemistry. Solubility is a thermodynamic property under specified conditions, not a simple permanent label attached to a substance.

3.29 Crystal lattices and coordination environments

Many ionic solids adopt repeating crystal structures in which each ion has a characteristic coordination number and geometry. Common structures include rock-salt (NaCl), caesium chloride (CsCl) and zinc blende (ZnS). In rock-salt, each ion has six nearest neighbours of opposite charge. In CsCl, each ion has eight nearest neighbours of opposite charge. In zinc blende, each ion is tetrahedrally coordinated by four oppositely charged ions. These descriptions concern the local environment, not a discrete molecule.

The preferred structure depends on ionic size, charge, polarisation, covalent contribution, pressure and temperature. Radius-ratio rules provide a historical qualitative guide, but they are not universal predictors. Real crystal stability is determined by total lattice energy and entropy, including long-range electrostatics, short-range repulsion and structural relaxation. Crystal defects, non-stoichiometry and polymorphism can also affect properties.

3.30 Structure and physical properties

Bonding and structure influence physical properties, but trends should be explained rather than memorised. Ionic solids are often hard and brittle because displacement can bring like charges into unfavourable proximity. Metals conduct because electrons can move through partially occupied or overlapping bands. Molecular solids often have lower melting points than extended ionic or covalent-network solids because melting mainly disrupts intermolecular forces rather than an entire covalent network. Network covalent materials such as diamond and silicon dioxide have extended bonding frameworks and can have high melting or sublimation temperatures.

Graphite illustrates anisotropy: strong covalent bonding holds carbon atoms in sheets, while weaker interactions act between sheets. Electrons are delocalised within the layers, supporting electrical conductivity parallel to the sheets. Diamond has a three-dimensional tetrahedral covalent network and is extremely hard. Both are forms of elemental carbon, but their structures and properties differ dramatically. This is an example of allotropy and the importance of bonding topology.

3.31 Bonding in coordination compounds

Coordination compounds contain a central metal atom or ion bonded to surrounding ligands. A ligand donates electron density to the metal through a donor atom, producing a coordinate interaction. Common ligands include H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻ and ethane-1,2-diamine. Coordination number describes the number of donor atoms directly attached to the metal, not necessarily the number of ligand molecules. A bidentate ligand binds through two donor atoms; a polydentate ligand binds through several.

Coordination geometry commonly includes octahedral, tetrahedral and square-planar arrangements. Geometry depends on metal identity, oxidation state, d-electron configuration, ligand properties, steric effects and solvent. Coordination compounds can exhibit isomerism, colour, magnetism and catalytic activity. A simple Lewis acid–base description treats the metal ion as an electron-pair acceptor and ligand as donor, while detailed bonding models examine metal–ligand orbital overlap and ligand-field splitting.

3.32 Ligand-field and crystal-field overview

In a free gaseous transition-metal ion, the five d orbitals are degenerate in the absence of external fields and relativistic effects. When ligands approach, electrostatic and covalent interactions remove this degeneracy. In an octahedral field, d orbitals split into a lower-energy t₂g set and a higher-energy e_g set in the conventional crystal-field model. The energy separation is commonly represented by Δₒ. In a tetrahedral field, the ordering is reversed and the splitting magnitude is often smaller than in an analogous octahedral environment.

Whether electrons pair in lower orbitals or occupy higher orbitals depends on the competition between ligand-field splitting and pairing energy. Strong-field ligands often favour low-spin configurations in suitable d-electron counts; weak-field ligands often favour high-spin arrangements. The spectrochemical series is an empirical ordering of ligand-field strengths. Crystal-field theory is electrostatic and simplified; ligand-field and molecular-orbital approaches include covalency more explicitly.

Worked example 3.13: d-electron count and unpaired electrons

Determine the d-electron count of Fe³⁺. Neutral iron has configuration [Ar]3d⁶4s². When transition-metal cations form, 4s electrons are removed before 3d electrons in this bookkeeping convention. Removing three electrons gives [Ar]3d⁵. In a weak-field octahedral complex, a d⁵ ion is often high spin with five unpaired electrons; in a sufficiently strong-field octahedral environment it can be low spin with one unpaired electron. The ligand field, not oxidation state alone, determines spin state.

3.33 Bonding models: strengths and limitations

Lewis theory is fast and useful for electron counting, connectivity and formal charge. Its weaknesses include inability to represent delocalisation fully, magnetic properties and quantitative bond energies. VSEPR is useful for predicting many main-group shapes but is qualitative and less reliable for transition-metal complexes and unusual bonding. Valence-bond theory explains localised overlap, σ and π bonds, and directional bonding, but hybridisation labels can be model-dependent and may obscure delocalised electron structure. Molecular-orbital theory explains delocalisation, bond order, spectra and magnetism but can require substantial mathematics and computational treatment for complex molecules.

Crystal-field theory rationalises d-orbital splitting and many magnetic and spectroscopic trends in coordination compounds, but its purely electrostatic picture neglects covalent mixing. Ligand-field and MO approaches improve this description. Band theory is powerful for solids but a simple band diagram cannot capture every feature of strongly correlated materials, defects or complex electronic phases. The best chemist selects a model suited to the question and states its assumptions.

3.34 Quantitative problem-solving strategy

For bonding questions, begin by defining the species, charge and physical state. Count electrons carefully. Draw a Lewis structure and check that total formal charge matches the species. Identify resonance if more than one valid electron arrangement exists. Predict electron-domain geometry, then molecular shape. Assess bond polarity from electronegativity and determine the net dipole by vector addition. If magnetic behaviour or bond order is requested, construct an MO diagram with the correct orbital ordering and electron count. For ionic solids, state the lattice-enthalpy convention and use Hess's law with signs and stoichiometric coefficients. For intermolecular forces, compare all relevant interactions rather than assigning one force to a whole molecule.

Numerical answers should show units at every stage. Convert nanometres to metres before using SI electrostatic equations. Convert per-particle energies to molar quantities using Avogadro's constant. Use joules consistently in equations involving SI constants, and convert to kJ mol⁻¹ at the end. Retain guard digits during calculations and round only the final answer to a sensible number of significant figures. A physically meaningful interpretation is part of the answer.

3.35 Worked example: formal charge in carbonate

Worked example 3.14: carbonate ion

CO₃²⁻ has 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24 valence electrons. In one common contributor, carbon is central, one C=O bond is present and two C–O single bonds are present. Carbon has formal charge 0; the double-bonded oxygen has 0; each singly bonded oxygen has −1. The total is −2. There are three equivalent contributors, so the actual ion has three equivalent C–O bonds in the idealised resonance hybrid, with average bond order 4/3 in this simple resonance picture. This does not mean each bond alternates between a single and a double bond.

3.36 Worked example: formal charge in ammonium

Worked example 3.15: NH₄⁺

Count valence electrons: nitrogen contributes five, four hydrogens contribute four, and the +1 charge means subtract one, giving eight electrons. Four N–H bonds use all eight electrons. Nitrogen has four bonds and no lone pair, so its formal charge is 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Each hydrogen has one bond and formal charge zero. The overall formal charge is +1. Formation can be described as donation of the ammonia lone pair to H⁺, but after formation the four N–H bonds are equivalent in the tetrahedral ion.

3.37 Worked example: geometry and polarity of SF₄

Worked example 3.16: SF₄

Sulfur has six valence electrons and four fluorines contribute 28, giving 34 electrons. Four S–F bonds use eight electrons; completing fluorine octets uses 24, leaving two electrons as one lone pair on sulfur. There are five electron domains, giving trigonal-bipyramidal electron-domain geometry. The lone pair occupies an equatorial position in the usual VSEPR model, producing a seesaw molecular shape. The S–F bonds are polar and the shape is asymmetric, so molecular dipoles do not cancel; SF₄ is polar.

3.38 Worked example: molecular shape and polarity of BF₃

Worked example 3.17: BF₃

Boron has three valence electrons and each fluorine has seven, giving 24 valence electrons. Three B–F bonds use six electrons, and the remaining 18 complete the fluorine octets. Boron has three bonding domains and no lone pair, giving trigonal-planar geometry with bond angles near 120°. Each B–F bond is polar, but the three equal bond dipoles cancel by symmetry. The ideal molecule has zero net dipole moment. Boron is electron-deficient in the simple Lewis structure and can act as a Lewis acid.

3.39 Worked example: ionic versus covalent contribution

Worked example 3.18: comparing MgCl₂ and AlCl₃

Both compounds contain metal and chloride components, but Al³⁺ is smaller and has a higher charge than Mg²⁺. Its charge density and polarising power are greater, so it distorts the chloride electron cloud more strongly. This increases covalent contribution in aluminium chloride relative to a simple purely ionic picture. The comparison illustrates Fajans' rules: small, highly charged cations and large, polarizable anions favour greater covalent character. The rules are qualitative and actual structures depend on phase and conditions; anhydrous AlCl₃, for example, has molecular dimeric forms under common conditions.

3.40 Worked example: bond order of a diatomic ion

Worked example 3.19: electron addition to a bonding orbital

Suppose a diatomic species has six bonding electrons and two antibonding electrons in the relevant MO set. Its bond order is (6 − 2)/2 = 2. If one electron is added to a higher-energy antibonding orbital, the bond order becomes (6 − 3)/2 = 1.5. The added electron weakens the net bond in this model. If instead the electron were added to an available bonding orbital, the bond order would rise by one-half. Always identify the orbital receiving or losing the electron before predicting the trend.

3.41 Worked example: lattice energy and ionic size

Worked example 3.20: comparing LiF and CsI

Both salts contain monovalent ions, so the charge product is the same in a simple Coulomb model. Li⁺ and F⁻ are substantially smaller than Cs⁺ and I⁻, giving a shorter interionic distance in LiF. Electrostatic attraction is therefore stronger and the magnitude of lattice enthalpy is expected to be greater for LiF. A complete comparison uses measured thermochemical data or a lattice model that accounts for crystal structure and short-range repulsion.

3.42 Worked example: dipole cancellation in a tetrahedral molecule

Worked example 3.21: CCl₄ versus CH₃Cl

CCl₄ has four polar C–Cl bonds arranged symmetrically in a tetrahedral geometry. The vector sum of the four equal bond dipoles is zero, so the molecule is nonpolar. CH₃Cl also has tetrahedral geometry, but the substituents are not all identical. The C–Cl bond dipole is not cancelled by three equivalent C–H bond dipoles, so CH₃Cl has a net dipole moment. Molecular symmetry is essential to the conclusion.

3.43 Worked example: intermolecular forces and viscosity

Worked example 3.22: qualitative viscosity comparison

Consider two liquids of similar molar mass, one composed of compact nonpolar molecules and the other of elongated molecules with polar functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding. The second liquid may have greater viscosity because it has stronger directional intermolecular attractions and more opportunities for molecular contact and temporary association. However, viscosity also depends on temperature, molecular flexibility and shape. A sound comparison identifies relevant structural evidence and avoids attributing every bulk property to one force alone.

3.44 Worked example: coordination number and ligand denticity

Worked example 3.23: [Co(en)₃]³⁺

The ligand en (ethane-1,2-diamine) contains two nitrogen donor atoms and is bidentate. Three en ligands bind through six donor atoms in total. The coordination number of cobalt is therefore six, not three. The common geometry is octahedral. This distinction between number of ligand molecules and number of donor atoms is essential when naming and interpreting coordination compounds.

3.45 Worked example: crystal-field absorption energy

Worked example 3.24: photon energy for a d–d transition

An octahedral complex absorbs light at 500 nm for a simplified transition associated with an energy gap Δ. Estimate the energy per mole of photons. E = hc/λ = (6.6261 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸)/(5.00 × 10⁻⁷) = 3.973 × 10⁻¹⁹ J photon⁻¹. Multiplying by Nₐ gives 239 kJ mol⁻¹. This is an illustrative conversion; real spectra may contain multiple transitions, vibronic structure and solvent effects, and the observed absorption maximum is not always identical to a single pure crystal-field splitting.

3.46 Bonding, colour and spectroscopy

Many coordination compounds are coloured because visible light can promote electronic transitions between levels separated by energies comparable to visible photon energies. In a simple octahedral d–d picture, absorption may promote an electron from t₂g to e_g. The colour observed is related to wavelengths not absorbed, so it is often complementary to the dominant absorbed colour. Charge-transfer transitions, ligand-centred transitions and mixed electronic states can also contribute, and many intense colours are not adequately explained by a simple d–d transition alone.

Infrared spectroscopy provides information about vibrational motions and bond force constants. A diatomic harmonic oscillator approximation gives vibrational frequency proportional to the square root of force constant divided by reduced mass. Stronger bonds often have higher stretching frequencies for the same pair of atoms. Isotopic substitution changes reduced mass and shifts vibrational frequencies, providing evidence that observed bands correspond to molecular vibrations. Real molecules have coupled normal modes, anharmonicity and selection rules.

3.47 Chemical bonding and reaction mechanisms

Bonding concepts underpin reaction mechanisms. Heterolytic bond cleavage produces ions, with both bonding electrons assigned to one fragment; homolytic cleavage produces radicals, with one electron assigned to each fragment. Curved-arrow notation in polar mechanisms tracks electron-pair movement, while single-headed fishhook arrows track single-electron movement in radical mechanisms. Arrows begin at an electron source such as a lone pair or bond and point toward an electron-deficient atom or bond-forming region.

In organic chemistry, nucleophiles donate electron pairs and electrophiles accept them. Lewis acid–base theory generalises this language beyond proton transfer. A Lewis base donates an electron pair; a Lewis acid accepts it. Coordination chemistry, catalysis and many inorganic reactions can be understood through this framework. Formal charges, oxidation states and actual electron density are related but distinct bookkeeping ideas; do not use them interchangeably.

3.48 Bonding in solids: covalent networks and molecular crystals

In a covalent-network solid, atoms are connected by an extended network of covalent bonds. Diamond, silicon and silicon carbide are important examples. Their mechanical and thermal properties depend on network topology and bond strength. In molecular crystals, discrete molecules occupy lattice sites and are held together by intermolecular forces. Iodine, dry ice and many organic solids are examples. Their melting points and mechanical properties depend on molecular size, shape, packing and intermolecular interactions.

Layered solids can combine strong bonding within sheets and weaker interactions between sheets. Graphite is the familiar carbon example. Some layered materials can be exfoliated into thin sheets, with properties that differ from their bulk forms. The structure–property relationship is multiscale: local bond geometry influences the lattice, the lattice determines electronic and mechanical behaviour, and defects or surfaces can modify performance.

3.49 Defects, non-stoichiometry and ionic transport

Real crystals are not perfectly periodic. Point defects include vacancies, interstitials and substitutional defects. In ionic solids, charge neutrality must be maintained overall, so defects often occur in coupled combinations. Schottky defects involve paired vacancies in an ionic lattice; Frenkel defects involve an ion leaving its normal site and occupying an interstitial site. Defects can influence density, colour, diffusion and ionic conductivity.

Some solids conduct ions because defects permit ions to move through the lattice. Such materials are important in batteries, fuel cells and sensors. Ionic conductivity depends on carrier concentration and mobility, both affected by temperature and crystal structure. The presence of an ionic lattice does not automatically imply high conductivity in the solid state; mobile charge carriers and accessible migration pathways are required.

3.50 A unified structure–bonding workflow

When faced with an unfamiliar substance, begin with its composition and likely oxidation states. Ask whether it consists of discrete molecules, an extended ionic lattice, a metallic solid, a covalent network or a coordination framework. Use Lewis structures and electron counting for molecular species; use lattice and band models for extended solids; use ligand-field and MO ideas for transition-metal complexes. Then connect the proposed structure to observable properties such as conductivity, magnetism, solubility, melting point, colour and spectroscopy. The model should explain several observations consistently, not just one feature.

Compare predictions with experimental evidence. Bond lengths can be measured by diffraction or spectroscopy; dipole moments by microwave spectroscopy; magnetic susceptibility by magnetic measurements; vibrational frequencies by infrared or Raman spectroscopy; and crystal structures by X-ray or neutron diffraction. Each method has assumptions and limitations. Chemical bonding is not merely a set of diagrams: it is a set of models tested against physical measurements.

3.51 Common misconceptions to avoid

  • “Atoms bond to complete octets.” Many stable compounds are electron-deficient, odd-electron, hypervalent or metallic. Energy and electron distribution are the deeper explanation.
  • “Ionic compounds are made of molecules.” Most simple ionic solids are extended lattices; formula units express ratios.
  • “A polar bond means a polar molecule.” Molecular symmetry can cancel bond dipoles.
  • “Resonance structures interconvert.” They are contributors to one delocalised state, not rapidly switching molecules.
  • “Hybrid orbitals are directly observable objects.” Hybridisation is a model-dependent representation.
  • “A double bond is two identical bonds.” In the usual σ/π description, it contains one sigma and one pi component.
  • “All intermolecular forces are negligible.” Their collective effects dominate many physical and biological properties.
  • “A high lattice enthalpy guarantees high solubility.” Dissolution depends on the full Gibbs energy balance.
  • “O₂ is diamagnetic because its Lewis structure has paired electrons.” O₂ is paramagnetic; MO theory explains its unpaired electrons.
  • “Coordination number equals number of ligands.” Denticity means one ligand may bind through multiple donor atoms.

3.52 Chapter summary

Chemical bonding is the outcome of a balance among attractive and repulsive interactions and the quantum-mechanical distribution of electrons. Lewis structures provide a starting point for electron counting, formal charge and resonance. VSEPR predicts many main-group geometries; valence-bond theory describes directional overlap and hybridisation; molecular-orbital theory explains delocalisation, bond order and magnetic behaviour. Ionic solids are stabilised by long-range electrostatics and short-range repulsion, while Born–Haber cycles connect lattice energetics to measurable thermochemical quantities. Metallic bonding and band theory explain electrical behaviour in solids. Intermolecular forces govern many phase and solution properties, while coordination and ligand-field models describe transition-metal complexes. No model is universally sufficient: select the simplest model that explains the evidence and recognise where a more advanced description is required.

Chapter 4: Molecular Structure, Symmetry and Group Theory

4.0 Chapter map and prerequisites

This chapter develops the mathematical language used to connect molecular geometry to spectroscopy and electronic structure. Students should be comfortable with vectors, matrices, molecular geometry, normal modes and elementary quantum-mechanical ideas. The chapter is written as a general undergraduate resource; follow the notation and point-group conventions prescribed by your own department when they differ.

4.1 Learning outcomes and scope

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to identify symmetry operations and elements; classify molecules into point groups using a systematic decision tree; explain the mathematical structure of groups; construct simple representations; use character tables; determine whether vibrations are infrared- or Raman-active; interpret symmetry labels; and apply symmetry arguments to bonding, spectroscopy, chirality and selection rules. The chapter assumes familiarity with Lewis structures, molecular geometry, atomic orbitals, molecular orbital theory and elementary linear algebra. The emphasis is on using symmetry as a predictive tool rather than memorising a catalogue of molecule names.

Molecular symmetry describes transformations that leave a molecule indistinguishable from its original configuration. The atoms may move during a transformation, but after the operation the same kinds of atoms occupy equivalent positions and the molecular arrangement is unchanged. Symmetry is therefore a property of the complete nuclear framework, not merely a visually attractive shape. Isotopic substitution can lower symmetry because two isotopes of the same element are distinguishable by mass in spectroscopic experiments. A molecule that appears symmetric in a ball-and-stick model may have lower effective symmetry when isotopes, ligands, a crystal environment or a solvent field are considered.

Symmetry is useful because it reduces complex problems. It can tell us whether a molecule has a permanent dipole, whether it is chiral, which vibrational modes can absorb infrared light, which modes can scatter Raman light, and which atomic orbitals can combine to form molecular orbitals. It can also explain degeneracy: two or more independent quantum states may have the same energy because the Hamiltonian is invariant under the symmetry operations of the system.

4.2 Symmetry operations and symmetry elements

A symmetry operation is an action that carries a molecule into an indistinguishable configuration. A symmetry element is the geometrical entity about which or through which the operation occurs. The distinction matters: rotation is an operation; the rotation axis is the element. Reflection is an operation; the mirror plane is the element. In a molecular point group, all operations leave at least one point fixed. This is why translations are not included in ordinary molecular point-group classification, although translations are central to the symmetry of crystals.

The identity operation E leaves every point unchanged. It may appear trivial, but it is essential mathematically because every group contains an identity. The identity operation also provides the reference against which all other transformations are defined. Every molecule has E, including molecules with no other symmetry.

A proper rotation Cₙ rotates a molecule through an angle 360°/n about an axis. C₂ is a half-turn; C₃ is a rotation by 120°; C₄ is 90°; and C₆ is 60°. The principal axis is the rotation axis with the largest value of n. If several axes have the same order, the point-group classification procedure considers the full arrangement, not an arbitrary choice. Repeating Cₙ n times gives E. For example, C₄² is a 180° rotation and is equivalent to C₂ about the same axis.

A reflection σ maps each point to its mirror image through a plane. A horizontal plane σₕ is perpendicular to the principal rotation axis. A vertical plane σᵥ contains the principal axis. A dihedral plane σ_d also contains the principal axis but bisects the angle between two adjacent C₂ axes perpendicular to the principal axis in groups where that description applies. These labels are defined relative to the selected principal axis; they are not absolute directions in space.

An inversion i maps a point with coordinates (x,y,z) to (−x,−y,−z) through a centre of inversion. The inversion centre is a point, not a plane. A molecule with an inversion centre has an atom or equivalent molecular environment opposite every position through that centre. Inversion is important in spectroscopy because centrosymmetric molecules obey the mutual exclusion rule: in the electric-dipole approximation, vibrations that are IR-active are Raman-inactive and vice versa.

An improper rotation Sₙ consists of a proper rotation Cₙ followed by reflection through a plane perpendicular to the rotation axis. The order of the operations is conventionally specified in the definition, and in this case the rotation and perpendicular reflection commute. S₁ is equivalent to a mirror reflection; S₂ is equivalent to inversion. Higher improper axes occur in several molecular point groups and are particularly important for identifying non-chiral structures. A molecule with any Sₙ axis is achiral.

4.3 Identity and closure: why operations form groups

The set of symmetry operations of a molecule forms a mathematical group. A group is a set of elements together with a binary operation—in this setting, composition of symmetry operations—that satisfies four requirements. Closure means that combining any two operations in the set gives another operation in the set. Associativity means that the order in which three operations are grouped does not change the final transformation. An identity operation exists. Every operation has an inverse that reverses its effect.

For example, the symmetry operations of a water molecule belong to C₂ᵥ: E, C₂ and two vertical mirror reflections. Applying C₂ twice returns the original arrangement, so C₂ is its own inverse. Applying either mirror operation twice gives E. The product of the two distinct mirror reflections is equivalent to the C₂ rotation. Thus, combinations remain within the set. The order of a finite group is the number of operations; C₂ᵥ has order four.

Composition is not always commutative. In general, AB need not equal BA, although many small molecular point groups are abelian and their operations commute. The distinction becomes important for groups with several non-parallel rotation axes. A multiplication table records the product of every pair of operations and is a direct way to check closure and inverses. The rows and columns are labelled by operations; the entry is their composition according to a stated convention.

The group axioms are not abstract decoration. They ensure that symmetry operations can be represented consistently by matrices, that representations can be decomposed into irreducible components, and that character tables obey orthogonality relationships. The mathematical framework gives reliable selection rules even when the molecule is too complicated for a direct visual prediction.

4.4 Point groups and Schoenflies notation

A point group is the complete set of molecular symmetry operations that leave at least one point fixed. Schoenflies notation is widely used in molecular spectroscopy. The simplest groups are C₁, containing only E, and Cₛ, containing E and one mirror plane. Cᵢ contains E and inversion. Cₙ contains rotations about one principal axis; Cₙᵥ adds n vertical mirror planes; Cₙₕ adds a horizontal mirror plane. Dₙ contains a principal Cₙ axis and n perpendicular C₂ axes. Dₙₕ and Dₙd add further mirror or improper-rotation symmetry.

The polyhedral groups include T, T_d and T_h for tetrahedral arrangements; O and O_h for octahedral and cubic arrangements; and I and I_h for icosahedral arrangements. These groups have several non-equivalent rotation axes. Linear molecules require special groups: C∞ᵥ for a heteronuclear linear molecule such as CO, and D∞ₕ for a homonuclear linear molecule such as N₂. The infinite rotation axis means infinitely many possible rotation angles about the molecular axis.

The point group is determined by the complete set of operations, not by a molecule's formula alone. CO₂ is linear and centrosymmetric, so it belongs to D∞ₕ. HCN is linear but has different ends and no inversion centre, so it belongs to C∞ᵥ. BF₃ is trigonal planar with a C₃ axis, three C₂ axes perpendicular to it, a horizontal plane and vertical planes, and belongs to D₃ₕ. Ammonia is trigonal pyramidal and belongs to C₃ᵥ. Methane has tetrahedral symmetry T_d. Benzene in its ideal planar equilibrium geometry belongs to D₆ₕ.

Real molecular structures can be distorted by vibrations, crystal packing, solvent interactions, substitution and external fields. Point-group classification generally refers to the equilibrium geometry of an isolated molecule unless another condition is stated. A dynamic molecule may sample multiple conformations; its time-averaged experimental symmetry can differ from the instantaneous symmetry of one conformation.

4.5 A reliable point-group decision procedure

Start by asking whether the molecule is linear. If it is, decide whether the two ends are equivalent. Equivalent ends lead to D∞ₕ; different ends lead to C∞ᵥ. For a non-linear molecule, identify all proper rotation axes and choose the principal axis with the highest n. Next determine whether there are n C₂ axes perpendicular to the principal axis. If so, the molecule belongs to a D family; if not, it is generally in a C or S family.

Then look for a horizontal mirror plane perpendicular to the principal axis. In a Cₙ family this may give Cₙₕ; in a Dₙ family it may give Dₙₕ. If there is no horizontal plane, check for vertical planes containing the principal axis. If present, a Cₙ molecule may be Cₙᵥ. In a Dₙ family, check for dihedral planes and improper axes; the relevant group may be Dₙd. If no mirror plane is found, check for inversion or an improper rotation axis before assigning a low-symmetry group. Finally verify that no symmetry operation has been overlooked.

The decision tree is a guide, not a substitute for inspecting the geometry. A common error is to identify a C₃ axis and immediately assign C₃ᵥ without checking whether perpendicular C₂ axes exist. Another is to call a molecule D₃ₕ merely because it is trigonal planar. A planar trigonal molecule with equivalent substituents has the additional C₂ axes that distinguish D₃ₕ from C₃ᵥ. A substituted trigonal molecule may lose those axes and belong to a lower group.

When assigning symmetry, state the assumed geometry and list the decisive operations. A strong answer might say: “The ideal trigonal-pyramidal structure has one C₃ axis and three vertical mirror planes, but no horizontal plane or perpendicular C₂ axes; therefore it belongs to C₃ᵥ.” This demonstrates reasoning rather than giving an unsupported label.

4.6 Worked point-group classifications

Water is bent. A C₂ rotation through 180° about the axis that bisects the H–O–H angle exchanges the two hydrogen atoms. Two mirror planes contain this axis: the molecular plane and a plane perpendicular to the molecular plane that also contains the C₂ axis. The operations are E, C₂ and two reflections. The point group is C₂ᵥ.

Ammonia is trigonal pyramidal. A C₃ rotation about the axis through nitrogen and the centre of the hydrogen triangle cycles the three hydrogen atoms. There are three vertical mirror planes, each containing nitrogen and one N–H bond. There is no horizontal mirror plane because reflection through a plane parallel to the hydrogen triangle would not preserve the pyramidal arrangement. The group is C₃ᵥ.

Boron trifluoride is planar trigonal. It has a C₃ principal axis perpendicular to the molecular plane, three C₂ axes in the plane, a horizontal molecular plane and three vertical planes. These operations give D₃ₕ. The molecular plane is σₕ relative to the principal C₃ axis.

Methane has four equivalent hydrogen positions arranged tetrahedrally. It has four C₃ axes through a carbon–hydrogen direction, three C₂ axes through opposite edge midpoints, six mirror planes and improper axes. Its point group is T_d. It is not O_h because it lacks a centre of inversion and the full octahedral set of operations.

Carbon dioxide is linear and has equivalent oxygen ends. It has an infinite-fold rotation axis, infinitely many vertical planes and a centre of inversion, so it belongs to D∞ₕ. Carbon monoxide is linear but has unlike ends, so it has C∞ᵥ symmetry and no inversion centre. These examples show why the molecular ends matter.

4.7 Symmetry and molecular polarity

A permanent electric dipole moment is a vector. Under a symmetry operation, a molecular dipole must transform into a vector that is compatible with the unchanged molecular arrangement. If a symmetry operation reverses a proposed dipole direction while leaving the molecule indistinguishable, the only allowed permanent dipole is zero. A centre of inversion forces the dipole to vanish because a polar vector changes sign under inversion. A molecule with multiple high-order rotation axes also cannot have a non-zero permanent dipole unless the vector lies along a direction preserved by every operation.

For a molecule with a single principal axis and vertical planes, a dipole may be allowed along the principal axis. Thus, water in C₂ᵥ has a dipole along the C₂ axis; ammonia in C₃ᵥ can have a dipole along its C₃ axis; and HCN in C∞ᵥ can have a dipole along the molecular axis. BF₃ in D₃ₕ has no permanent dipole because its in-plane bond dipoles cancel and its symmetry excludes an out-of-plane dipole. CO₂ in D∞ₕ is nonpolar.

A point group can therefore establish whether a dipole is symmetry-allowed, but it does not by itself calculate the magnitude. The magnitude depends on electronic structure and nuclear positions. A symmetry-allowed dipole may be small or even accidentally zero due to cancellation. Conversely, a symmetry-forbidden permanent dipole must be zero for the ideal structure.

4.8 Chirality and optical activity

A molecule is chiral if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image by any proper rotation and translation. In point-group terms, a molecule is chiral if it has no improper rotation axis Sₙ of any order. Because a mirror plane is S₁ and inversion is S₂, a molecule with a mirror plane or inversion centre is necessarily achiral. The absence of a mirror plane alone is not enough to prove chirality: an improper axis of higher order can also make a molecule achiral.

Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror-image forms of a chiral molecule. In an achiral environment, enantiomers have identical ordinary physical properties such as melting point and boiling point, but they rotate plane-polarised light by equal magnitudes and opposite directions under the same conditions. They can behave differently in chiral environments, such as enzymes, chiral solvents or chiral stationary phases. Optical rotation is an experimental property; the sign of rotation cannot be inferred simply from R or S configuration.

A tetrahedral carbon with four different substituents is a common source of chirality, but chirality is not restricted to tetrahedral stereocentres. Axial chirality occurs in appropriately substituted allenes and hindered biaryl systems. Helical molecules can be chiral because their left- and right-handed forms are not superimposable. Conversely, a molecule may contain stereogenic centres and still be achiral if internal symmetry produces a meso form. Point-group analysis helps identify the relevant symmetry restrictions.

The point groups compatible with chirality are groups containing only proper rotations, such as C₁, Cₙ, Dₙ, T, O and I. The presence of any mirror plane, inversion centre or improper rotation makes the structure achiral. In crystals, the corresponding restrictions lead to the 11 Sohncke space-group families that can contain chiral arrangements, a subject developed in crystallography.

4.9 Symmetry operations as matrices

A vector in three-dimensional Cartesian space can be transformed by a 3 × 3 matrix. A rotation about the z-axis by angle θ is represented by a matrix with cosθ and sinθ terms in the x–y plane while leaving z unchanged. Reflection in the xy plane changes the sign of z but leaves x and y unchanged. Inversion changes the signs of all three coordinates. Matrix multiplication represents successive symmetry operations.

The matrix representation of a symmetry operation acting on Cartesian coordinates is called a representation of the group. For a particular group, each operation is assigned a matrix such that the product of matrices corresponds to the product of operations. The trace of a matrix is its character. Characters are useful because they are unchanged by a change of basis and can be used to decompose a representation into irreducible representations.

A representation may be reducible: it may contain several independent symmetry types. The three Cartesian displacement coordinates x, y and z form a representation for the motion of a point in space. In a molecule, the 3N Cartesian displacement coordinates of N atoms form the 3N-dimensional mechanical representation. This can be reduced to identify translations, rotations and vibrational normal modes.

A character is not an energy and does not measure how much an operation physically moves an atom. It is a mathematical trace associated with a chosen representation. In a Cartesian representation, the character for an operation is the sum of the diagonal matrix elements. For the identity in three dimensions the character is 3; for inversion it is −3. For a molecular displacement representation, the character also depends on how many atoms remain in place and how their local displacement vectors transform.

4.10 Character tables: how to read them

A character table summarises the irreducible representations of a point group. Columns are symmetry operations or classes of operations. Rows are irreducible representations. The entries are characters. The final columns commonly identify how Cartesian coordinates, rotations, quadratic functions and other basis functions transform. Mulliken symbols label irreducible representations: A and B are one-dimensional representations, E denotes a two-dimensional representation, and T (or F in some conventions) denotes a three-dimensional representation. Subscripts and primes distinguish symmetry behaviour under selected operations.

For C₂ᵥ, the irreducible representations are A₁, A₂, B₁ and B₂. In a common axis convention with the C₂ axis along z and the molecular plane xz, z transforms as A₁, x as B₁ and y as B₂. The labels depend on the axis convention, so students must check the character table used in the course. A₁ is symmetric under all operations; A₂ is symmetric under C₂ but antisymmetric under the two mirror planes; B representations are antisymmetric under C₂ with differing mirror behaviour.

The totally symmetric representation is the one whose characters are all +1. It is often labelled A₁, A or Γ₁ depending on the group and convention. Totally symmetric functions remain unchanged under every symmetry operation. Many molecular properties and totally symmetric vibrational modes transform according to this representation.

Character tables also list rotational functions Rₓ, Rᵧ and R_z. These are axial-vector components, not polar-vector coordinates. A polar vector such as a dipole transforms as x, y and z; an axial vector such as angular momentum transforms differently under reflections and inversion. Confusing the two can lead to incorrect assignments of rotational and spectroscopic selection rules.

4.11 Classes and equivalent operations

Two group operations belong to the same class if one can be transformed into the other by conjugation: an operation A is conjugate to B if there is a group operation X such that B = XAX⁻¹. Operations in the same class have the same character in every representation. Character tables therefore list classes rather than necessarily listing every operation separately. The number of operations in a class is often shown as a heading or implied by the notation.

In C₃ᵥ, the classes are E, 2C₃ and 3σᵥ. The two C₃ operations are rotations by +120° and −120°; they are conjugate and belong to one class. The three vertical mirror planes form another class. The group has six operations in total: one identity, two rotations and three reflections. This class structure is useful when applying the reduction formula because each class contributes its character multiplied by the number of operations in that class.

The number of irreducible representations equals the number of classes for a finite group. The sum of the squares of the dimensions of all irreducible representations equals the order of the group. For C₃ᵥ, the dimensions are 1, 1 and 2, and 1² + 1² + 2² = 6. This provides a useful check on a character table.

4.12 Orthogonality and the reduction formula

Irreducible representations of a finite group obey orthogonality relations. These relations make it possible to determine how many times each irreducible representation occurs in a reducible representation. If Γ is a reducible representation, the number of times irreducible representation i occurs is nᵢ = (1/h) Σ_R n_R χΓ(R) χᵢ(R)*, where h is the group order, n_R is the number of operations in class R, χΓ is the character of the reducible representation, and χᵢ is the character of the irreducible representation. For the real character tables commonly used in elementary molecular applications, the complex conjugate symbol may be omitted.

The reduction formula is a weighted inner product of character rows. The weights are the class sizes. Omitting class sizes is a frequent source of mistakes. The result must be a non-negative integer for a valid representation. If fractional or negative coefficients appear, check the character calculation, class multiplicities, group order and coordinate convention.

The reduction formula is not restricted to vibrational analysis. It can reduce sets of atomic orbitals, ligand group orbitals, molecular displacement coordinates, or basis functions used in quantum calculations. It is the bridge between the symmetry of a whole molecule and the symmetry of its constituent functions.

4.13 Building the 3N mechanical representation

For a molecule with N atoms, there are 3N Cartesian displacement coordinates. Each atom has three possible infinitesimal displacement directions. The full mechanical representation Γ₃N describes how these displacements transform under every symmetry operation. To obtain the character for a particular operation, first identify which atoms remain unmoved by that operation. Atoms that are moved to different positions do not contribute to the trace in the usual atom-by-atom basis. For each unmoved atom, determine the trace of the 3 × 3 transformation matrix acting on its x, y and z displacement components. Sum those traces over the unmoved atoms.

The identity leaves all N atoms in place and leaves all three displacement directions unchanged, so χ(E) = 3N. For a rotation, the trace of the Cartesian rotation matrix is 1 + 2cosθ when the axis is z. For a reflection in a plane, the trace is 1 because two in-plane components remain unchanged and the perpendicular component changes sign. For inversion, the trace is −3. The total molecular character is obtained by multiplying the single-atom trace by the number of atoms that remain fixed under that operation.

Once Γ₃N has been reduced, translations are removed by subtracting the representation for x, y and z. Rotations are removed by subtracting the representation for Rₓ, Rᵧ and R_z. The remainder is the vibrational representation Γvib. For a non-linear molecule, there are 3N − 6 vibrational normal modes. For a linear molecule, there are 3N − 5 because rotation about the molecular axis does not correspond to an independent overall rotational degree of freedom.

4.14 Vibrational normal modes

A normal mode is a collective pattern of atomic motion in which all atoms oscillate at the same frequency and maintain fixed relative phase and amplitude relationships. A molecule with N atoms has 3N Cartesian degrees of freedom. Six correspond to overall translation and rotation for a non-linear molecule, leaving 3N − 6 vibrations. A linear molecule has only two independent overall rotations, leaving 3N − 5 vibrations.

The normal modes are independent in the harmonic approximation. Each mode has a characteristic frequency determined by the molecular masses and the curvature of the potential-energy surface along that normal coordinate. The normal coordinates are linear combinations of atomic displacements. Symmetry assigns each normal coordinate to an irreducible representation. Modes belonging to different irreducible representations cannot mix in a symmetry-preserving harmonic force field, while modes of the same symmetry may interact.

A molecule can have degenerate vibrations. Degeneracy occurs when two or more independent normal coordinates transform together as a multidimensional irreducible representation. In an ideal tetrahedral molecule, certain vibrations are triply degenerate. Distortion or an external field that lowers the symmetry can split the degeneracy. Observed spectral bands may also show splitting due to isotopic substitution, crystal fields or coupling between molecules in a condensed phase.

4.15 Infrared activity and the dipole-moment derivative

A vibration is infrared-active if it produces a change in the molecular dipole moment. More precisely, the derivative of at least one component of the dipole moment with respect to the normal coordinate must be non-zero at the equilibrium geometry. The dipole moment is a vector, so its x, y and z components transform according to the corresponding irreducible representations. A normal mode is IR-active if its symmetry species matches one of the irreducible representations associated with x, y or z.

This symmetry criterion is a selection rule. It tells us whether a transition is allowed in the electric-dipole approximation; it does not guarantee that the band will be intense. The intensity depends on the magnitude of the dipole derivative, population of the initial state, transition frequency, linewidth and experimental conditions. A symmetry-allowed transition can be weak, while a forbidden transition may gain weak intensity through anharmonicity, vibronic coupling, collisions or symmetry-breaking environments.

For C₂ᵥ with z as the principal axis, x, y and z belong to different irreducible representations. Therefore any normal mode transforming as one of these species is potentially IR-active. A mode transforming as a species that contains none of x, y or z is IR-inactive in the ideal electric-dipole approximation.

4.16 Raman activity and polarizability

A vibration is Raman-active if it changes the molecular polarisability tensor. The induced dipole in an applied electric field is μind = αE, where α is the polarisability tensor. A vibrational mode is Raman-active if at least one component of the derivative of α with respect to the normal coordinate is non-zero. The six independent components of a symmetric polarisability tensor transform like quadratic functions: x², y², z², xy, xz and yz.

Character tables list these quadratic functions in their right-hand columns. If the symmetry species of a vibration appears beside one or more quadratic functions, that mode is Raman-active. Raman selection rules are therefore different from IR selection rules. A vibration can be active in both methods in a non-centrosymmetric molecule. For centrosymmetric molecules, the mutual exclusion rule applies in the electric-dipole and ordinary Raman approximations: gerade modes are Raman-active and ungerade modes are IR-active, while the opposite activities are forbidden by inversion symmetry.

Raman scattering can be measured with visible, near-infrared or ultraviolet lasers. Stokes scattering leaves the molecule in a higher vibrational state and the scattered photon has lower energy than the incident photon. Anti-Stokes scattering begins from a thermally populated excited vibrational state and leaves the molecule at lower energy, so the scattered photon has higher energy. The relative Stokes and anti-Stokes intensities depend on temperature and the population distribution.

4.17 Worked example: mode count for water

Water has N = 3 atoms and is non-linear. The number of normal modes is 3N − 6 = 9 − 6 = 3. They are conventionally described as the symmetric O–H stretch, the H–O–H bend and the asymmetric O–H stretch. In C₂ᵥ symmetry these modes transform as three one-dimensional species. With the conventional axis assignment, the symmetric stretch and bend are A₁, while the asymmetric stretch is B₂ or B₁ depending on which perpendicular axis is designated as y. All three species correspond to Cartesian coordinates in C₂ᵥ, so all three modes are IR-active. The modes are also Raman-active because their symmetry species appear among the quadratic functions in the C₂ᵥ character table.

4.18 Worked example: mode count for carbon dioxide

CO₂ has N = 3 atoms and is linear, so the number of vibrational modes is 3N − 5 = 4. These correspond to the symmetric stretch, the asymmetric stretch and two perpendicular bending coordinates that are degenerate in the ideal linear molecule. The symmetric stretch does not change the dipole moment and is IR-inactive but Raman-active. The asymmetric stretch and bending modes change the dipole moment and are IR-active. The two bending coordinates are degenerate in an isolated linear molecule; environmental or isotopic effects can alter the observed degeneracy.

4.19 Worked example: symmetry of a dipole

For a molecule with C₂ᵥ symmetry and the C₂ axis along z, a permanent dipole can only lie along a direction that is unchanged by all operations. The z component is unchanged by C₂ and by both mirror planes in the conventional orientation, so z transforms as A₁ and a dipole along z is allowed. The x and y components change sign under at least one operation and cannot represent a permanent dipole for the unchanged structure. Thus, C₂ᵥ permits a dipole along the principal axis. The result is a symmetry allowance, not a numerical prediction of dipole magnitude.

4.20 Worked example: group order and classes in C₃ᵥ

C₃ᵥ has the operations E, C₃, C₃², σᵥ1, σᵥ2 and σᵥ3. The classes are E, 2C₃ and 3σᵥ, with class sizes 1, 2 and 3. The order is 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. Its irreducible representation dimensions are 1, 1 and 2. The sum of squared dimensions is 1 + 1 + 4 = 6, which agrees with the group order and checks the completeness of the irreducible representations.

4.21 Worked example: reduction of a simple representation

Consider a reducible representation of C₂ᵥ with characters Γ = (3, 1, 1, 1) for the operation order E, C₂, σᵥ(xz), σᵥ(yz). The group order is h = 4. The C₂ᵥ irreducible characters are A₁ = (1,1,1,1), A₂ = (1,1,−1,−1), B₁ = (1,−1,1,−1) and B₂ = (1,−1,−1,1). The coefficient of A₁ is (1/4)[3 + 1 + 1 + 1] = 1. The coefficient of A₂ is (1/4)[3 + 1 − 1 − 1] = 1/2, which is not an integer. This tells us the proposed character set is not a valid representation for this group and is a useful warning: do not force an invalid reduction. For a valid example use Γ = (3,1,1,1) only if the characters and class conventions have been independently verified; here the fractional result demonstrates the diagnostic value of orthogonality.

4.22 Worked example: valid C₂ᵥ reduction

Take Γ = (3, −1, 1, 1) in the same operation order. The coefficient for A₁ is (3 − 1 + 1 + 1)/4 = 1. For A₂ it is (3 − 1 − 1 − 1)/4 = 0. For B₁ it is (3 + 1 + 1 − 1)/4 = 1. For B₂ it is (3 + 1 − 1 + 1)/4 = 1. Therefore Γ = A₁ + B₁ + B₂. This is a valid reducible representation because all coefficients are non-negative integers. Always state the operation order and class sizes when presenting a reduction.

4.23 Worked example: vibrational mode count in ammonia

NH₃ has four atoms and is non-linear, so it has 3(4) − 6 = 6 normal modes. In C₃ᵥ these reduce to 2A₁ + 2E, where each E species is doubly degenerate. The total number of coordinates is 2(1) + 2(2) = 6. The two A₁ modes are the symmetric stretch and symmetric deformation; the two E sets describe doubly degenerate asymmetric stretching and deformation patterns. The Cartesian coordinates z and (x,y) transform as A₁ and E, respectively, so both species are IR-active. Quadratic functions also include A₁ and E, so both are Raman-active.

4.24 Worked example: mutual exclusion in a centrosymmetric molecule

In CO₂, inversion symmetry is present. The symmetric stretch is gerade because inversion returns the same displacement pattern; the asymmetric stretch is ungerade because inversion changes its sign. In the electric-dipole approximation, dipole components transform as ungerade functions, so ungerade vibrations may be IR-active. The polarisability components are quadratic and gerade, so gerade vibrations may be Raman-active. Thus the symmetric stretch is Raman-active and IR-inactive, while the asymmetric stretch is IR-active. This is an example of the mutual exclusion rule for centrosymmetric molecules.

4.25 Symmetry-adapted linear combinations

A symmetry-adapted linear combination (SALC) is a linear combination of basis functions that transforms as one irreducible representation of the molecular point group. SALCs are central to molecular orbital theory. When ligand orbitals surround a central atom, their combinations can be classified by symmetry. Only central-atom orbitals of compatible symmetry can mix with a given ligand SALC, subject also to energy and spatial-overlap requirements.

The projection operator can generate a SALC from a chosen basis function. In a common form, the projection operator for irreducible representation i is proportional to the sum over group operations of the character of that operation multiplied by the operation acting on the basis function. The result is a function with the desired symmetry. The function is then normalised and linearly independent combinations are retained. The method is more systematic than guessing bonding combinations from a drawing.

For a simple linear triatomic molecule, terminal ligand orbitals can combine in-phase and out-of-phase. The in-phase combination may have the same symmetry as a central s orbital, while another combination may match a central p orbital. Which combinations can interact depends on the chosen coordinate system and the point group. The symmetry analysis predicts allowed mixing; the actual energy ordering and degree of mixing require a quantum-mechanical calculation or a more detailed orbital model.

4.26 Worked example: ligand combinations in a linear triatomic

Consider a symmetric linear X–A–X molecule along the z-axis and one σ-type ligand orbital on each terminal X atom. The in-phase combination adds the two terminal functions and is symmetric with respect to inversion when the phases are defined consistently; the out-of-phase combination changes sign under inversion. The central s orbital is gerade and can mix with a ligand combination of gerade symmetry. The central pz orbital is ungerade and can mix with a ligand combination of ungerade symmetry. The conclusion is not that every symmetry-matched pair must mix strongly: orbital energies and overlap must also be favourable.

4.27 Symmetry and molecular orbital degeneracy

If a molecule has a multidimensional irreducible representation, orbitals transforming according to that representation are symmetry-related and may be degenerate in the absence of symmetry-breaking perturbations. For example, the three p orbitals of an isolated atom are degenerate in a spherically symmetric environment. In a molecule, the local point group can split these levels. In a tetrahedral or octahedral ligand environment, d orbitals split into sets whose degeneracy is predicted by the point group.

Degeneracy can be lifted by distortion, substitution, an applied electric or magnetic field, crystal packing or vibronic coupling. The Jahn–Teller theorem states, in its common molecular form, that a non-linear molecule in an orbitally degenerate electronic state is unstable with respect to at least one symmetry-lowering distortion that removes the degeneracy, subject to the assumptions of the theorem. This is important in coordination chemistry, especially for certain transition-metal electron configurations.

Symmetry does not determine the magnitude of a splitting. It predicts which degeneracies are permitted and which couplings are forbidden. Energetic calculations and experimental data are needed to quantify the effects.

4.28 Symmetry and electronic spectroscopy

Electronic transitions are governed by transition moments. In the electric-dipole approximation, a transition from an initial state ψᵢ to a final state ψf is allowed when the integral of ψf* μ ψᵢ over all coordinates is non-zero. In group-theory language, the direct product of the symmetry species of the initial state, the dipole operator and the final state must contain the totally symmetric representation. This is the general symmetry selection rule.

For centrosymmetric molecules, electric-dipole transitions generally require a change in parity: g to u or u to g. A g-to-g or u-to-u transition is electric-dipole forbidden in the ideal approximation. Spin selection rules add another condition: transitions that change total spin are generally forbidden in the non-relativistic electric-dipole approximation, although spin–orbit coupling can give them weak intensity. Vibronic coupling can also lend intensity to electronically forbidden transitions.

Selection rules are not absolute statements that a transition can never be observed. They describe the idealised dominant interaction. Real molecules vibrate, rotate and interact with their environment; perturbations can relax the rules. When interpreting a weak spectral band, consider whether it is intrinsically weak, symmetry-forbidden but activated, or due to another species or transition.

4.29 Raman tensors and polarisation

The Raman tensor for a normal mode describes how the polarisability tensor changes as the molecule vibrates. Its non-zero components determine the polarisation properties of the scattered light. Symmetry constrains which tensor components can be non-zero. Polarised Raman experiments can therefore distinguish vibrational symmetry species, particularly in crystals and highly symmetric molecules.

In an isotropic sample of randomly oriented molecules, orientation averaging affects the measured intensities and depolarisation ratios. In single crystals, the scattering geometry and crystal orientation are controlled, so Raman tensor elements can be measured more directly. The distinction between parallel and perpendicular polarisations provides information about the symmetry of the vibration. Quantitative Raman intensity analysis requires careful treatment of instrument response, laser polarisation, sample orientation and resonance effects.

4.30 Symmetry in crystallography: molecular versus crystal symmetry

Molecular point groups describe finite molecules and include only operations that leave at least one point fixed. Crystal symmetry also includes translations and combinations of translations with point operations. A space group describes the full symmetry of a periodic crystal lattice. It may contain screw axes, which combine rotation with translation, and glide planes, which combine reflection with translation. These operations cannot be represented as ordinary molecular point-group operations because they do not leave every point fixed.

A crystal's point group is obtained by considering the rotational and reflectional parts of its space-group operations while omitting translations. Crystallographic point groups are restricted by translational periodicity to rotational orders 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. This restriction differs from isolated molecular point groups, where axes of other orders are possible. The 32 crystallographic point groups classify macroscopic symmetry and underpin crystal morphology, optical properties and tensor behaviour.

Diffraction is sensitive to periodic electron density. Systematic absences in diffraction patterns can reveal screw axes and glide planes. The full space group is therefore determined using lattice metrics, systematic absences and intensity symmetry, not merely by looking at the external shape of a crystal.

4.31 Symmetry and physical properties

Symmetry constrains physical property tensors. A polar vector such as electric polarisation, a dipole moment or a linear displacement must transform as x, y and z. An axial vector such as magnetisation or angular momentum transforms differently under inversion and reflection. A second-rank tensor such as polarisability, dielectric susceptibility, conductivity or thermal conductivity must remain invariant under all operations of the point group. The symmetry restrictions determine which tensor components may be non-zero or equal.

For example, an isotropic fluid has the same linear response in every direction, so its second-rank response tensor is proportional to the identity matrix. A uniaxial crystal can have different response parallel and perpendicular to its principal axis. A low-symmetry crystal can have off-diagonal tensor components, depending on its point group and coordinate axes. These restrictions are important in optics, electrochemistry, magnetism and materials science.

Piezoelectricity requires the absence of a centre of inversion. Ferroelectricity requires a spontaneous polarisation that can be reversed by an applied electric field and is compatible with the crystal symmetry. Not every non-centrosymmetric crystal is piezoelectric, and not every polar crystal is ferroelectric. Symmetry provides necessary restrictions, while domain structure, energetics and switching behaviour determine the observed material response.

4.32 Dynamic symmetry and conformational averaging

A molecule may rotate about single bonds and interconvert between conformations. Each instantaneous conformation has its own point group, but rapid interconversion can produce an averaged structure with higher apparent symmetry in some experiments. Nuclear magnetic resonance often observes time-averaged environments when exchange is fast compared with the NMR timescale. Vibrational spectroscopy may probe a different timescale and can reveal features of the instantaneous potential-energy surface.

A classic conceptual example is a molecule with hindered internal rotation. At low temperature, distinct conformers may be populated unequally or interconvert slowly, producing multiple signals. At higher temperature, exchange may become fast and signals coalesce. The observed symmetry is therefore linked to both molecular structure and experimental timescale. Isotopic labelling can break equivalence and reveal pathways that are hidden in the unlabelled molecule.

When assigning a point group for a problem, use the equilibrium geometry specified by the question. When interpreting an experiment, state whether the measurement probes a static geometry, a vibrational average, or a time-averaged ensemble.

4.33 Isotopic substitution and symmetry lowering

Replacing an atom by one of its isotopes does not substantially change its nuclear charge or the leading electronic potential, but it changes its mass. In vibrational spectroscopy, this changes the kinetic-energy matrix and can split modes that were degenerate by permutation symmetry of identical nuclei. A molecule that is geometrically symmetric may therefore have lower mass symmetry after isotopic substitution. The isotope-substituted molecule should be classified using the actual nuclear labels relevant to the physical measurement.

For a diatomic harmonic oscillator, the vibrational wavenumber is proportional to the square root of the force constant divided by the reduced mass. Increasing reduced mass lowers the vibrational frequency if the force constant is nearly unchanged. In polyatomic molecules, isotopic substitution changes normal-mode eigenvectors and can mix modes of compatible symmetry. It can also remove symmetry equivalences and activate otherwise forbidden transitions weakly.

4.34 Common mistakes in symmetry and group theory

Do not confuse an operation with its element: C₃ is the rotation; the C₃ axis is the line. Do not assume a molecule has a horizontal plane merely because it is flat; the plane must be perpendicular to the selected principal axis and preserve the structure. Do not infer chirality from the absence of a mirror plane alone; inversion or an improper axis also makes a structure achiral. Do not forget the perpendicular C₂ axes when distinguishing Cₙ and Dₙ groups.

In character reductions, include the number of operations in each class and divide by the group order. Keep the coordinate convention consistent with the character table. Distinguish polar-vector functions x, y, z from axial-vector functions Rₓ, Rᵧ, R_z. Remember that a mode can be symmetry-allowed but weak, and a mode forbidden in an ideal molecule may gain intensity through perturbations. Finally, do not treat point-group symmetry as a complete description of a crystal; translational symmetry requires space groups.

4.35 A practical workflow for a symmetry problem

First draw or inspect the equilibrium geometry and label equivalent atoms. Choose coordinate axes and state them. Identify the principal proper rotation axis. Search for perpendicular C₂ axes, mirror planes, inversion and improper axes. Assign the point group only after checking the complete set. For a property question, identify the transformation behaviour of the relevant vector or tensor. For a vibrational problem, calculate 3N, subtract translations and rotations, reduce the mechanical representation, and apply IR and Raman selection rules. For an orbital-mixing problem, identify the symmetry species of the basis functions and combine only compatible functions.

Show intermediate reasoning. A diagram with labelled axes and mirror planes is often clearer than a paragraph. When using a character table, state its convention. When calculating a mode count, check that the sum of degeneracies equals 3N − 6 or 3N − 5. When reducing a representation, check that all coefficients are non-negative integers. These checks catch many errors before the final interpretation.

4.36 Chapter synthesis

Molecular symmetry is the bridge between geometry and measurable properties. The symmetry operations of a molecule form a point group. Point-group classification provides a compact description of its ideal equilibrium geometry. Group representations describe how vectors, orbitals, displacements and polarizability components transform. Character tables organise the irreducible representations and supply the symmetry information needed to derive selection rules. The reduction formula decomposes a reducible representation into irreducible components.

In vibrational spectroscopy, the number of normal modes follows from the degrees of freedom, while symmetry determines their degeneracies and spectroscopic activity. IR activity requires a change in dipole moment; Raman activity requires a change in polarisability. In centrosymmetric molecules, the mutual exclusion rule separates IR and Raman active modes in the ideal approximation. In electronic spectroscopy, the direct-product selection rule identifies transitions allowed by symmetry. In molecular orbital theory, SALCs show which ligand and central-atom orbitals can mix. In materials chemistry, symmetry restricts tensor properties and distinguishes molecular point groups from crystal space groups.

The strongest approach is to combine geometry, algebra and experiment. Symmetry predicts what is permitted; quantum mechanics and energetics determine what occurs and how strongly; spectroscopy and diffraction test the predictions.

4.37 Worked examples

Worked example 4.1: water point group

Choose the C₂ axis that bisects the H–O–H angle. A half-turn exchanges the hydrogen atoms. The molecular plane and a perpendicular plane containing the C₂ axis are mirror planes. The operations are E, C₂ and two σᵥ operations. The point group is C₂ᵥ.

Worked example 4.2: ammonia point group

The line through N and the centre of the H₃ triangle is a C₃ axis. Three vertical planes each contain one N–H bond. There is no horizontal plane and no set of perpendicular C₂ axes. The point group is C₃ᵥ.

Worked example 4.3: distinguish CO₂ and HCN

Both molecules are linear. CO₂ has identical ends and an inversion centre, so it belongs to D∞ₕ. HCN has unlike ends, so it lacks inversion and belongs to C∞ᵥ.

Worked example 4.4: methane point group

The tetrahedral arrangement has four C₃ axes and three C₂ axes, along with six mirror planes and improper axes. Its point group is T_d. The absence of an inversion centre distinguishes it from O_h.

Worked example 4.5: mode count for NH₃

N = 4 and the molecule is non-linear. The number of normal modes is 3N − 6 = 12 − 6 = 6. The symmetry decomposition is 2A₁ + 2E; since each E mode is doubly degenerate, the total is 2 + 4 = 6.

Worked example 4.6: mode count for CO₂

N = 3 and the molecule is linear. The number of vibrations is 3N − 5 = 9 − 5 = 4. These are one symmetric stretch, one asymmetric stretch and two degenerate bending coordinates.

Worked example 4.7: group order of C₃ᵥ

C₃ᵥ contains E, C₃, C₃² and three σᵥ operations. Its order is 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. Its irreducible dimensions are 1, 1 and 2; their squared sum is 1 + 1 + 4 = 6.

Worked example 4.8: C₂ᵥ dipole direction

With z along the C₂ axis, z is unchanged by all C₂ᵥ operations and transforms as A₁. x and y change sign under at least one operation. A permanent dipole is therefore allowed only along z.

Worked example 4.9: valid representation reduction

For C₂ᵥ with operation order E, C₂, σᵥ(xz), σᵥ(yz), let Γ have characters (3, −1, 1, 1). The reduction coefficients are A₁ = (3−1+1+1)/4 = 1; A₂ = (3−1−1−1)/4 = 0; B₁ = (3+1+1−1)/4 = 1; B₂ = (3+1−1+1)/4 = 1. Hence Γ = A₁ + B₁ + B₂.

Worked example 4.10: detect an invalid character set

For Γ = (3,1,1,1) in the same C₂ᵥ operation order, the A₂ coefficient is (3+1−1−1)/4 = 1/2. A fractional coefficient is impossible for a valid finite-dimensional representation. Recheck the proposed characters and conventions rather than accepting the decomposition.

Worked example 4.11: infrared selection rule

A mode is IR-active when it transforms like x, y or z. In C₂ᵥ with z as the principal axis, z transforms as A₁, x as B₁ and y as B₂. Modes of A₁, B₁ or B₂ symmetry are potentially IR-active; A₂ is not IR-active in the ideal electric-dipole approximation.

Worked example 4.12: Raman selection rule

Raman activity requires a symmetry species that occurs among x², y², z², xy, xz or yz. For C₂ᵥ, the quadratic functions span A₁, A₂, B₁ and B₂, so all four one-dimensional species are Raman-allowed in principle.

Worked example 4.13: mutual exclusion

A centrosymmetric molecule has g and u parity labels. Dipole components are u and polarizability components are g. Thus g vibrations can be Raman-active and u vibrations can be IR-active; the same mode cannot be both under the ideal mutual-exclusion rule.

Worked example 4.14: water vibrational activity

Water has three normal modes. In C₂ᵥ they transform as 2A₁ + B₂ or 2A₁ + B₁ depending on the chosen in-plane axis convention. Each species transforms like a Cartesian coordinate or quadratic function, so the modes are IR- and Raman-active.

Worked example 4.15: determine chirality from symmetry

A candidate structure has an S₄ axis. Since any improper rotation axis makes a molecule achiral, the structure is achiral even if no mirror plane is visible. The mirror image can be superimposed through a sequence involving the improper symmetry.

Worked example 4.16: isotopic substitution

Replacing one H in H₂O by D makes the two terminal nuclei inequivalent. The mass-labelled isotopologue HDO has lower effective permutation symmetry than H₂O. Vibrational degeneracies or equivalences relying on interchange of identical hydrogen nuclei are removed.

Worked example 4.17: count Cartesian degrees of freedom

For a non-linear molecule with N = 7 atoms, there are 3N = 21 Cartesian degrees of freedom. Six are overall translation and rotation, leaving 15 vibrational modes.

Worked example 4.18: count linear-molecule vibrations

A linear molecule with N = 5 atoms has 15 Cartesian degrees of freedom. Subtract three translations and two independent rotations to obtain 10 normal modes.

Worked example 4.19: character of identity

For a molecule with N = 6 atoms, the identity operation leaves all atoms and all three Cartesian displacement directions unchanged. The character of Γ₃N under E is 3N = 18.

Worked example 4.20: polar versus axial vector

Under inversion, a polar vector (x,y,z) changes sign, while an axial vector such as angular momentum does not. Therefore, dipole components and rotational functions must be read from different parts of a character table.

Worked example 4.21: linear molecule symmetry

A heteronuclear diatomic molecule has an infinite rotation axis and infinitely many vertical planes but no inversion centre, so it belongs to C∞ᵥ. A homonuclear diatomic molecule has the additional inversion centre and belongs to D∞ₕ.

Worked example 4.22: symmetry-allowed orbital mixing

A central s orbital is totally symmetric under rotations and inversion for a centrosymmetric linear triatomic arrangement. It can mix only with ligand combinations of matching symmetry. A ligand combination of opposite parity cannot mix with that s orbital in the ideal symmetric Hamiltonian.

Worked example 4.23: symmetry and degeneracy

If two components transform together as a two-dimensional E representation, the point group permits them to be degenerate. A distortion that lowers the symmetry can split the pair into two non-degenerate modes.

Worked example 4.24: crystal versus molecular symmetry

A molecule may possess a C₅ axis, but a periodic crystal lattice cannot have an ordinary five-fold rotational symmetry compatible with translational periodicity. Crystal symmetry is described by crystallographic point groups and space groups.

Answer 14. Construct Γ₃N using the characters of the displacement transformations; reduce it; subtract translations and rotations; the remainder is Γvib.
Answer 15. Polar vectors reverse under inversion; axial vectors do not. Coordinates x,y,z describe polar vectors, while Rₓ,Rᵧ,R_z describe axial rotations.
Answer 16. Isotopes change nuclear masses and can make formerly equivalent atoms distinguishable. This can lower effective symmetry, split degenerate modes and shift vibrational frequencies.
Answer 17. Combine ligand orbitals into SALCs and assign each a symmetry species. A central orbital can mix only with a SALC of the same symmetry, provided energy and overlap are suitable.
Answer 18. Multidimensional irreducible representations permit degenerate states. A distortion can lower the point group and split the representation into lower-dimensional components.
Answer 19. Point groups contain operations leaving a point fixed and describe isolated molecular geometry. Space groups include translations, screw axes and glide planes for periodic crystals.
Answer 20. Selection rules state whether the transition moment is symmetry-allowed. Intensity also depends on the magnitude of the transition moment, populations, coupling and experimental conditions.

4.40 Further academic practice

For deeper study, work through the character tables for C₂ᵥ, C₃ᵥ, D₂ₕ, D₃ₕ and T_d until the transformation properties of Cartesian coordinates and quadratic functions become familiar. Practise point-group assignment from three-dimensional molecular drawings rather than flat formulae. When learning a new point group, construct its operation classes and verify the group order before attempting a reduction. Use the same coordinate convention throughout a calculation and explicitly label the principal axis.

For spectroscopy, compare a predicted vibrational activity pattern with an experimental IR or Raman spectrum. Record the phase, solvent, temperature, resolution and isotopic composition where available. Assign bands cautiously: a peak may contain overlapping modes, combination bands, overtones or contributions from impurities. Selection rules are powerful constraints but do not replace spectral interpretation.

For molecular orbital applications, practise constructing ligand SALCs and matching their symmetry to central-atom orbitals. Begin with simple linear triatomic and tetrahedral examples before moving to octahedral complexes. Remember that symmetry determines allowed interactions, while orbital energies, overlap and electron occupancy determine the resulting molecular orbitals and their properties.

Recommended university-level follow-up topics include symmetry in quantum mechanics, projection operators, direct products, electronic term symbols, ligand-field theory, vibronic coupling, Raman tensors, crystallographic point groups and space-group determination. Consult the current course reading list and laboratory instructions used by your department.

4.41 Direct products and transition selection rules

When two functions are multiplied, their product transforms according to the direct product of their symmetry species. This is a compact way to test whether an integral can be non-zero. For an electronic transition, the integrand contains the initial-state function, the dipole operator and the final-state function. The integral over all molecular coordinates can be non-zero only if their direct product contains the totally symmetric representation. In a real character table, direct products can be obtained by multiplying the characters operation by operation and reducing the resulting representation. This method generalises the parity rule for centrosymmetric molecules and applies to non-centrosymmetric point groups as well.

For a one-dimensional representation, multiplying a function by itself often gives the totally symmetric representation, because its characters are ±1 and their squares are +1. For multidimensional representations, the direct product may contain several irreducible components. The result helps determine whether two states can be coupled by a perturbation, whether a transition is allowed, or whether two vibrational coordinates can interact through a particular term in the potential-energy expansion. Always use the direct-product rule together with the correct operator symmetry; a transition is not allowed simply because the initial and final states have similar-looking orbital pictures.

4.42 Magnetic properties and symmetry

Magnetic response is described by axial vectors and higher-rank response tensors. Under inversion, an axial vector such as magnetic moment remains unchanged, whereas an electric dipole changes sign. This distinction is central to the symmetry classification of magnetic interactions and optical effects. In a molecule, the point group restricts the possible directions of permanent magnetic moments and the components of magnetic susceptibility. In a solid, the magnetic point group may include operations combined with time reversal, because magnetic order can break ordinary time-reversal symmetry.

Symmetry arguments also appear in circular dichroism and optical rotation. Chiral structures can interact differently with left- and right-circularly polarised light. The magnitude and sign of the response depend on electronic structure and wavelength; the presence of chirality makes such effects possible but does not determine their numerical size. Spectroscopic interpretation therefore combines point-group restrictions, transition moments, state populations and experimental conditions.

Essential constants: R = 8.3144626 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; F = 96485 C mol⁻¹; kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹; 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa; 1 L bar = 100 J. Use kelvin for thermodynamic equations and maintain consistent units throughout.

5.1 Thermodynamics: scope, language and chemical questions

Chemical thermodynamics studies the relationships among heat, work, energy, temperature, entropy and the direction of change. It does not normally describe the detailed path taken by each molecule. Instead, it relates measurable macroscopic properties to the behaviour of very large collections of particles. This distinction is useful: kinetics asks how fast a process occurs, whereas thermodynamics asks whether a process is energetically and entropically favourable under specified conditions. A reaction may be thermodynamically favourable yet experimentally slow because its activation barrier is large. Conversely, a rapid reaction may stop at equilibrium before all reactants are consumed.

Thermodynamic conclusions are conditional. A statement such as “the reaction is spontaneous” is incomplete unless the temperature, pressure, composition and relevant constraints are understood. Spontaneity means that a process can proceed in the stated direction without continuous external driving under the specified conditions; it does not mean that the process is fast, violent, complete or useful. Thermodynamic state functions describe the state, while heat and work describe energy transfer during a process. Keeping these categories separate prevents many common errors.

5.2 Systems, surroundings and boundaries

A thermodynamic system is the portion of the universe selected for study; everything outside it is the surroundings. The boundary may be real, such as the wall of a calorimeter, or imaginary, such as a surface drawn around a reacting solution. An open system exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings. A closed system exchanges energy but not matter. An isolated system exchanges neither matter nor energy. These are idealised classifications: a well-insulated sealed vessel approximates an isolated system for a limited period, but no practical apparatus is perfectly isolated.

The choice of system changes the bookkeeping, not the underlying physics. In a reaction calorimeter, the reacting mixture may be the system and the calorimeter plus laboratory the surroundings. If the system releases heat, the surroundings gain energy. A control volume around a flowing reactor is usually open because feed and product streams cross its boundary. Always state what is inside the system before assigning signs to heat, work or mass transfer.

5.3 State variables, state functions and path functions

A thermodynamic state is specified by a sufficient set of independent properties, for example temperature, pressure, volume and composition. A state function depends only on the state, not on the route used to reach it. Internal energy U, enthalpy H, entropy S, Gibbs energy G and Helmholtz energy A are state functions. Their changes around a closed cycle are zero. Heat q and work w are path functions: their values depend on how the process is carried out. The differential of a state function is exact, commonly written dU, whereas heat and work are inexact differentials, often written δq and δw.

A useful analogy is elevation. The change in height between two points is independent of the route, but the distance walked depends on the path. Likewise, ΔU is fixed by initial and final states, but q and w can vary with the process. For a cycle returning to its starting state, ΔU = 0 even though heat and work may each be non-zero. The first law requires their net contributions to balance.

5.4 Temperature, thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law

The zeroth law establishes the basis of temperature measurement: if system A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and C are in thermal equilibrium with one another. A thermometer works because it reaches thermal equilibrium with the object being measured and reports a property calibrated against temperature. Temperature is an intensive property; it does not scale with the amount of material. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference, not a substance stored inside a body.

Thermal equilibrium means there is no net heat flow between bodies in thermal contact. It does not imply equal internal energies, equal heat capacities or equal masses. A small metal sample and a large water bath can have the same temperature while containing very different amounts of internal energy. Temperature is related to the distribution of molecular energies, but for a macroscopic system it is a well-defined measurable state variable.

5.5 The first law and conservation of energy

The first law is the conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems. With the chemistry sign convention, q is positive when heat enters the system and w is positive when work is done on the system. For a closed system, ΔU = q + w. If a reaction mixture absorbs 12 kJ of heat and the surroundings perform 3 kJ of work on it, its internal energy rises by 15 kJ. If the system expands against the surroundings, it performs work and w is negative under this convention. Other disciplines sometimes use a different sign convention, so the convention must be stated.

Internal energy includes microscopic kinetic and potential contributions: translational, rotational and vibrational motion, electronic energy, intermolecular interactions and chemical bonding. It excludes the macroscopic kinetic and potential energy of the whole apparatus unless those terms are explicitly included. Absolute internal energy is generally not measured directly; experiments determine changes. Chemical reactions alter internal energy because the electronic and nuclear arrangements and interactions of the particles change.

5.6 Pressure–volume work and expansion

For a system expanding against a constant external pressure, pressure–volume work is w = −PextΔV. Expansion has ΔV greater than zero and therefore w less than zero: the system transfers energy to the surroundings by doing work. Compression has ΔV less than zero, so w is positive. In SI units, pressure in pascals multiplied by volume in cubic metres gives joules. A frequent examination error is to use litres with pascals without converting units. One litre atmosphere is approximately 101.325 J.

For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, the external pressure differs only infinitesimally from the gas pressure at each stage. Integration gives wrev = −nRT ln(V2/V1), or equivalently nRT ln(P2/P1) for fixed temperature and amount. Reversible expansion produces the maximum magnitude of work for the specified endpoints. Free expansion into a vacuum has Pext = 0 and therefore no pressure–volume work, even though the gas volume changes.

5.7 Heat capacity and calorimetry

Heat capacity is the heat required to raise the temperature of a body by one kelvin. The molar heat capacity is expressed per mole and the specific heat capacity per unit mass. For a temperature-independent heat capacity over a limited range, q = CΔT, or q = mcΔT for a sample of mass m and specific heat capacity c. If heat capacity varies significantly with temperature, the correct relation is q = ∫C(T)dT. The approximation should be used only over a range where it is reasonable.

A calorimeter measures heat transfer indirectly through a temperature change. In a simple coffee-cup calorimeter at approximately constant pressure, qsolution = m c ΔT and the reaction heat is approximately the negative of the solution heat, after allowing for the cup's heat capacity if significant. In a bomb calorimeter, combustion occurs at constant volume; the measured heat is related to ΔU rather than directly to ΔH. Calibration, heat loss, incomplete combustion, stirring and thermometer response are important sources of uncertainty.

5.8 Enthalpy and constant-pressure processes

Enthalpy is defined as H = U + PV. It is a state function with units of energy. For a process at constant pressure where only pressure–volume work occurs, qp = ΔH. This is why many solution reactions and reactions carried out in open vessels are reported using enthalpy changes. Enthalpy is not “heat stored in a substance”; it is a thermodynamic state function whose change equals heat transferred under the stated constant-pressure conditions.

For ideal gases, PV = nRT, so ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT for a reaction when gaseous species are ideal and the temperature is specified. Here Δng is the change in moles of gaseous products minus gaseous reactants, using stoichiometric coefficients. If Δng is zero, ΔH and ΔU are equal under these assumptions. The correction may be important for gas-phase reactions with substantial changes in gas mole number.

5.9 Exothermic and endothermic reactions

An exothermic process has ΔH less than zero: the system's enthalpy decreases and heat is released to the surroundings at constant pressure. An endothermic process has ΔH greater than zero and absorbs heat. The sign describes the system, not the surroundings. Dissolving a salt can be endothermic even if the beaker feels cold; the system absorbs heat from the surroundings. Combustion is typically strongly exothermic because the products have lower enthalpy than the reactants under the specified conditions.

The sign of ΔH alone does not determine spontaneity. Melting ice above its melting point is endothermic but spontaneous at that temperature because the entropy contribution is favourable. Some exothermic processes are not spontaneous under all conditions because the entropy change may be sufficiently negative. Gibbs energy combines enthalpy and entropy at constant temperature and pressure to provide the appropriate criterion.

5.10 Thermochemical equations and reaction enthalpy

A thermochemical equation is a balanced chemical equation accompanied by a specified enthalpy change. The physical states matter because enthalpy depends on phase. For example, H2O(l) and H2O(g) have different standard enthalpies of formation. If an equation is reversed, the sign of ΔH reverses. If all coefficients are multiplied by a factor, ΔH is multiplied by the same factor. Enthalpy is extensive: it scales with the amount of material reacting.

Thermochemical equations should identify temperature, pressure and standard-state conventions where relevant. The standard pressure commonly used in modern thermodynamic tables is 1 bar. A standard state does not mean a concentration of exactly 1 mol L−1 in every context; the standard state for a solute is defined through an agreed reference convention. Always consult the definitions attached to the data table being used.

5.11 Hess's law and enthalpy cycles

Hess's law follows from enthalpy being a state function. The enthalpy change for an overall reaction is the same whether the reaction occurs in one step or several hypothetical or real steps, provided the initial and final states are identical. This permits enthalpy changes that are difficult to measure directly to be calculated from other reactions. Equations are manipulated algebraically: reverse a reaction and reverse its enthalpy sign; multiply an equation and multiply its enthalpy; add equations and add their enthalpy changes.

A Hess cycle is a bookkeeping diagram connecting reactants and products through a common reference state. It is not necessarily a proposed mechanism. In combustion cycles, formation cycles or solution cycles, arrows must be labelled consistently. A reliable method is to write the target equation first, then arrange known equations so that unwanted species cancel. Check atom balance and charge balance after adding the equations.

5.12 Standard enthalpies of formation

The standard enthalpy of formation ΔfH° of a substance is the enthalpy change when one mole of that substance forms from its constituent elements in their reference states under standard conditions. By convention, the standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its most stable reference form is zero at the specified temperature and standard pressure. The value for a compound is not generally zero. Formation reactions must produce exactly one mole of the compound as written.

Reaction enthalpy can be calculated using ΔrH° = ΣνΔfH°(products) − ΣνΔfH°(reactants). Stoichiometric coefficients ν multiply the tabulated values. The formula applies to the balanced equation and the selected standard states. A common mistake is to omit coefficients or to use the value for a different phase. If water is formed as liquid rather than gas, the corresponding formation enthalpy must be selected.

5.13 Bond enthalpies and estimating reaction energies

A mean bond enthalpy is the average enthalpy required to break one mole of a specified type of bond in gaseous molecules. Reaction enthalpies can be estimated from ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed). Breaking bonds requires energy and is endothermic; forming bonds releases energy and is exothermic. The estimate is approximate because a bond's strength depends on its molecular environment. Mean bond enthalpies average over different compounds and are most useful for gas-phase estimates.

Use the molecular structures to count bonds carefully. For a reaction, list every bond type and the number broken and formed, multiply by the relevant bond enthalpies, then subtract the formation total from the breaking total. Do not count a bond merely because an atom changes its position in a structural drawing. The method is less accurate than using reliable standard enthalpies of formation when those data are available.

5.14 Lattice enthalpy and ionic solids

Lattice enthalpy describes the energetic change associated with forming an ionic solid from gaseous ions, or the reverse process of separating the solid into gaseous ions. Two conventions are common: lattice formation enthalpy is negative, while lattice dissociation enthalpy is positive. State which definition is being used. Lattice energy depends strongly on ionic charges, ionic sizes and crystal structure. Greater charge magnitudes generally increase electrostatic attraction; smaller ions can approach more closely and also tend to increase lattice strength.

Born–Haber cycles apply Hess's law to ionic-solid formation. The cycle combines atomisation or sublimation, bond dissociation, ionisation energies, electron affinities and lattice formation. The result allows an unknown lattice enthalpy to be estimated. The cycle is a thermodynamic construction, not a literal sequence of microscopic steps followed by a crystal during formation.

5.15 Kirchhoff's law and temperature dependence of enthalpy

Reaction enthalpy changes with temperature because reactants and products have different heat capacities. At constant pressure, Kirchhoff's law gives d(ΔrH)/dT = ΔrCp. If ΔrCp is approximately constant over a temperature interval, ΔrH(T2) = ΔrH(T1) + ΔrCp(T2 − T1). If heat capacities vary appreciably, integrate ΔrCp(T) over the interval. This correction matters when data measured at one temperature are applied at another.

The heat-capacity difference is calculated from products minus reactants, with stoichiometric coefficients. A positive ΔrCp means reaction enthalpy increases with temperature under the approximation; a negative value means it decreases. Phase transitions require additional treatment because latent enthalpies must be included when a reactant or product changes phase within the temperature interval.

5.16 The second law and entropy

The second law introduces entropy, a state function that measures the dispersal of energy among accessible microscopic arrangements. For a reversible transfer of heat, dS = δqrev/T. For an isolated system, a spontaneous change increases the total entropy of the system plus surroundings; at equilibrium, the total entropy is at a maximum subject to the constraints. Entropy is not simply disorder. It is connected to the number and probability of accessible microstates, and structural descriptions such as “more disorder” are only qualitative guides.

The entropy change of a system can be negative if the surroundings gain enough entropy for the total to increase. Freezing a liquid reduces the entropy of the material, but heat released to the surroundings can compensate. The second law concerns the combined entropy change, not the system alone. For reversible processes the total entropy generation is zero; for irreversible processes it is positive.

5.17 Statistical interpretation: Boltzmann equation

Boltzmann's relation S = kB ln W connects entropy with the number W of accessible microstates, where kB is Boltzmann's constant. A macrostate that can be realised in many more microscopic ways has greater statistical weight and, correspondingly, greater entropy. The logarithm makes entropy additive for independent systems: if Wtotal = W1W2, then Stotal = S1 + S2. This microscopic interpretation explains why energy and matter tend to spread among available states.

The number of microstates depends on constraints and the energy levels available to particles. Increasing volume often increases the number of translational states accessible to a gas. Heating generally makes more energy levels accessible. Mixing distinguishable gases increases the number of arrangements, whereas mixing identical gases does not produce the same physical entropy of mixing when the correct indistinguishability is considered. Statistical mechanics gives a deeper foundation for thermodynamic entropy.

5.18 Entropy changes in heating, phase transitions and expansion

For heating at constant pressure with constant heat capacity, ΔS = nCp,m ln(T2/T1). At constant volume, replace Cp,m with Cv,m. For a reversible phase transition at its transition temperature, ΔStrans = ΔHtrans/Ttrans. For an ideal gas undergoing an isothermal change, ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) = −nR ln(P2/P1). These expressions are derived from reversible paths even when the actual process is irreversible, because entropy is a state function.

When a process crosses a phase transition and also changes temperature, calculate the entropy change in separate stages: heat within the first phase, carry out the phase transition at its equilibrium temperature, then heat or cool in the second phase. Sum the contributions. Using a single heat-capacity expression across a phase change omits the latent-heat contribution and gives an incorrect result.

5.19 Third law and absolute entropies

The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystalline substance approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero, provided the crystal has a unique ground state. This provides a reference that allows absolute molar entropies to be tabulated. Real crystals may possess residual entropy if disorder remains at very low temperature, and glassy materials may not reach perfect equilibrium. The third law is therefore a precise limiting statement with assumptions, not a claim that all materials become motionless in a simple classical sense.

Standard molar entropy values are usually positive at ordinary temperatures and depend on temperature, phase and molecular structure. Reaction entropy is calculated as ΔrS° = ΣνS°(products) − ΣνS°(reactants). Unlike standard enthalpies of formation, standard molar entropies of elements in their reference states are not generally set to zero at 298 K.

5.20 Entropy of mixing and solution

Mixing often increases entropy because particles gain access to a larger number of arrangements. For ideal mixing, ΔmixS = −RΣni ln xi, where ni is the amount of component i and xi its mole fraction. Each mole-fraction term is non-positive because xi lies between zero and one, so the ideal entropy of mixing is non-negative. The expression assumes ideal behaviour and is especially useful for gases and ideal solutions.

Real solutions can show non-ideal interactions. Enthalpy of mixing may be positive or negative, and volume may change on mixing. A favourable entropy of mixing does not by itself guarantee that every pair of liquids is completely miscible at all temperatures; the Gibbs energy of mixing must be considered. Phase separation can occur when enthalpic penalties outweigh the entropy benefit in a particular composition and temperature range.

5.21 Gibbs energy and spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure

Gibbs energy is defined as G = H − TS. For a process at constant temperature and pressure, ΔG < 0 indicates a thermodynamically spontaneous direction, ΔG = 0 indicates equilibrium, and ΔG > 0 indicates that the forward direction is non-spontaneous under the stated conditions. The criterion incorporates both enthalpy and entropy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS when temperature is constant. Use kelvin and consistent energy units.

A negative ΔG does not mean that a reaction releases heat; it means that the combined enthalpic and entropic terms favour the process under the specified constraints. A reaction with positive ΔH can be spontaneous at sufficiently high temperature if ΔS is positive. A reaction with negative ΔH and negative ΔS may be spontaneous only at sufficiently low temperature. These conclusions assume the signs remain approximately constant over the temperature range.

5.22 Temperature dependence and spontaneity maps

The sign combinations of ΔH and ΔS give a useful first prediction. If ΔH is negative and ΔS positive, ΔG is negative at all positive temperatures within the range where the signs remain valid. If ΔH is positive and ΔS negative, ΔG is positive at all positive temperatures. If both are negative, the enthalpy term favours the process while the −TΔS term opposes it, so lower temperature is favourable. If both are positive, higher temperature can make the entropy term dominate.

The crossover temperature for ΔG = 0 is T = ΔH/ΔS when ΔH and ΔS are expressed in compatible units and treated as temperature-independent. This is an estimate, not a universal exact transition temperature. Heat capacities, phase changes and non-ideal effects can alter the temperature dependence. A phase transition at a specified pressure occurs when the Gibbs energies of the two phases are equal.

5.23 Standard Gibbs energies and formation data

Standard reaction Gibbs energy can be calculated from standard Gibbs energies of formation: ΔrG° = ΣνΔfG°(products) − ΣνΔfG°(reactants). The standard Gibbs energy of formation of an element in its reference state is zero by convention. Alternatively, if standard reaction enthalpy and entropy are known at the same temperature, use ΔrG° = ΔrH° − TΔrS°. Do not mix data at different temperatures without appropriate corrections.

Standard-state values describe reference conditions, not necessarily the actual mixture in a reactor. A reaction with positive ΔrG° can proceed forward if the actual composition makes the reaction quotient sufficiently small. Conversely, a reaction with negative ΔrG° may proceed in reverse if products accumulate enough. The non-standard Gibbs energy is needed to describe the direction at the current composition.

5.24 Chemical potential and partial molar properties

The chemical potential μi is the partial molar Gibbs energy of component i: μi = (∂G/∂ni)T,P,nj. It measures how Gibbs energy changes when a small amount of component i is added while temperature, pressure and the amounts of other components are held fixed. In a mixture, chemical potential depends on composition as well as temperature and pressure. At equilibrium, the chemical potential conditions for all permitted transfers and reactions are satisfied.

For a pure substance, chemical potential is its molar Gibbs energy. For an ideal gas, μi = μi° + RT ln(pi/p°). For a component in a solution, activity replaces the simple concentration or mole fraction when non-ideality matters. Chemical potential is central to phase equilibrium, diffusion, osmosis and chemical reaction equilibrium because matter tends to redistribute in ways that lower the appropriate thermodynamic potential.

5.25 Reaction Gibbs energy and reaction quotient

For a reaction at fixed temperature, ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Q, where Q is the dimensionless reaction quotient constructed from activities raised to their stoichiometric powers. For ideal gases, activities are approximated by partial pressures relative to the standard pressure; for dilute ideal solutions, activities may be approximated using concentration relative to the standard concentration. Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from Q because their activities are taken as unity in the standard treatment.

The sign of ΔrG predicts the instantaneous direction under the current composition. If ΔrG is negative, forward reaction lowers G; if positive, reverse reaction lowers G; at equilibrium ΔrG is zero. Q is not the same as K except at equilibrium. As a reaction proceeds, Q changes until it reaches K, assuming the system can attain equilibrium and no external constraints prevent it.

5.26 Thermodynamic equilibrium constant

At equilibrium, ΔrG = 0 and Q = K. Substitution gives ΔrG° = −RT ln K. A large K corresponds to a negative standard reaction Gibbs energy and product-favoured equilibrium under standard-state reference conditions; a small K corresponds to a positive value and reactant-favoured equilibrium. K is dimensionless when defined using dimensionless activities. Equilibrium constants depend on temperature but not on the initial amounts of reactants and products at a fixed temperature.

The magnitude of K does not indicate reaction rate. A reaction can have a very large equilibrium constant yet proceed slowly because of a high activation barrier. Catalysts accelerate the approach to equilibrium but do not change K or the equilibrium composition at fixed temperature. This distinction between thermodynamic favourability and kinetics is essential in industrial process design.

5.27 van ’t Hoff equation and temperature effects

The temperature dependence of an equilibrium constant is described by the van ’t Hoff equation: d ln K/dT = ΔrH°/(RT²). If ΔrH° is approximately constant over a temperature interval, integration gives ln(K2/K1) = −(ΔrH°/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1). Endothermic reactions generally have larger K at higher temperature, while exothermic reactions generally have smaller K at higher temperature. The relation provides a quantitative basis for temperature effects on equilibrium.

The approximation assumes the reaction enthalpy does not change substantially over the interval. If heat capacities differ significantly, ΔrH° varies with temperature and a more complete integration is required. Use kelvin, ensure the enthalpy is in joules per mole when R is in joules per mole kelvin, and take natural logarithms.

5.28 Helmholtz energy and other thermodynamic potentials

Helmholtz energy A = U − TS is the useful thermodynamic potential for a closed system at constant temperature and volume. Gibbs energy G = H − TS is especially useful at constant temperature and pressure. Internal energy is natural for isolated systems, while enthalpy is convenient for constant-pressure processes. These potentials are not competing definitions of energy; they are transformations of state functions that incorporate environmental constraints.

The differential forms show the natural variables: for a simple compressible system of fixed composition, dU = TdS − PdV, dH = TdS + VdP, dA = −SdT − PdV, and dG = −SdT + VdP. Chemical composition adds chemical-potential terms. These relations explain why A decreases spontaneously at constant T,V and G decreases spontaneously at constant T,P for the appropriate closed-system conditions.

5.29 Reversible and irreversible processes

A reversible process is an ideal limiting path that can be reversed by an infinitesimal change in conditions, leaving no net change in system and surroundings. It proceeds through a succession of equilibrium states and produces the maximum useful work for specified endpoints. Real processes are irreversible because of finite temperature or pressure gradients, friction, viscosity, mixing, diffusion, electrical resistance and chemical reaction away from equilibrium. Reversibility is a theoretical benchmark, not a description of ordinary laboratory operations.

For an irreversible process, entropy is generated. The entropy balance for a closed system can be written ΔSsystem = ∫δq/Tboundary + Sgen, where Sgen is non-negative. The exact form depends on how heat crosses the boundary and on the chosen control volume. Entropy generation is zero in the reversible limit and positive for irreversible processes. This provides a quantitative measure of thermodynamic inefficiency.

5.30 Thermodynamic cycles and engines

A cyclic engine returns its working substance to its initial state after absorbing heat from a hot reservoir, producing work and rejecting some heat to a colder reservoir. Since the working substance completes a cycle, its net change in internal energy is zero. The first law therefore relates the net work to the net heat. The second law prevents complete conversion of heat from a single reservoir into work in a cyclic process. Some heat must be rejected to a colder reservoir.

The Carnot engine is an ideal reversible engine operating between two reservoirs at temperatures Th and Tc. Its maximum efficiency is ηmax = 1 − Tc/Th, with temperatures in kelvin. No real engine operating between the same reservoirs can exceed this limit. The Carnot cycle consists of two isothermal and two adiabatic reversible steps; it establishes a universal upper bound independent of the working substance.

5.31 Chemical thermodynamics in batteries and electrochemical cells

For a reversible electrochemical cell, the maximum non-expansion electrical work is related to Gibbs energy by ΔG = −nFEcell, where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred per reaction as written, F is Faraday's constant and Ecell is the reversible cell potential. A positive cell potential corresponds to a negative Gibbs energy for the cell reaction under the specified conditions. This relation links measurable voltage to chemical driving force.

The standard relation is ΔrG° = −nFE°cell. Combining it with ΔrG° = −RT ln K gives ln K = nFE°cell/(RT). The Nernst equation follows from ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q: E = E° − (RT/nF)ln Q. At 298 K, the base-10 form is often written E = E° − (0.05916 V/n)log10 Q. Check the reaction direction and electron count before using it.

5.32 Non-ideal gases, fugacity and real mixtures

Ideal-gas thermodynamic expressions use pressure as a proxy for activity. Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature because molecules occupy finite volume and interact. Fugacity f is an effective pressure that accounts for non-ideality; chemical potential can be written μ = μ° + RT ln(f/f°). The fugacity coefficient φ = f/p approaches one in the ideal-gas limit.

For solutions, activity ai = γi xi or an equivalent concentration-based convention, where γi is an activity coefficient. At infinite dilution under the selected standard state, the activity coefficient approaches its limiting value. Activities are dimensionless. Thermodynamic equilibrium constants defined in terms of activities are fundamental; concentration-based constants are conditional approximations that may depend on ionic strength and solvent.

5.33 Phase equilibria and phase-transition conditions

At phase equilibrium, the chemical potential of a substance is equal in every phase that can exchange matter. For a pure substance at a given pressure, solid and liquid coexist at the melting temperature; liquid and vapour coexist at the boiling temperature. A phase transition occurs when the Gibbs energies of the phases become equal. The enthalpy and entropy changes of transition are related by ΔHtrans = TtransΔStrans at equilibrium.

The Clapeyron equation describes the slope of a phase boundary: dP/dT = ΔHtrans/(TΔVtrans). For liquid–vapour equilibrium, the Clausius–Clapeyron approximation can be integrated when vapour behaves ideally and liquid volume is negligible relative to vapour volume. This gives ln(P2/P1) = −ΔHvap/R(1/T2 − 1/T1) when ΔHvap is treated as constant.

5.34 Thermodynamic stability and metastability

A thermodynamically stable state has the lowest relevant thermodynamic potential under the imposed constraints. A metastable state is locally stable but not the global minimum; it can persist because a barrier separates it from a more stable state. Supercooled water is a familiar example: liquid water below its normal freezing point can remain liquid if nucleation is inhibited. The metastable state is not the equilibrium state, but it may persist for an observable time.

Thermodynamic stability must be distinguished from mechanical, kinetic and operational stability. A material may be thermodynamically unstable but kinetically persistent. Conversely, a thermodynamically favourable transformation may be prevented by diffusion limitations or a large nucleation barrier. Phase diagrams and free-energy curves help visualise stable and metastable regions.

5.35 Experimental thermochemistry: uncertainty and good practice

Reliable thermochemical work requires calibrated instruments, known sample purity, controlled pressure and temperature, accurate mass and volume measurements, and an explicit reaction equation. Heat loss to the environment, incomplete reaction, evaporation, side reactions and unmeasured calorimeter heat capacity can bias results. Replicate measurements help estimate precision. Report uncertainty with appropriate significant figures and distinguish random scatter from systematic bias.

When comparing experimental and tabulated values, verify that the same reaction, physical states, temperature, pressure and standard-state conventions are used. A discrepancy does not automatically mean the table is wrong: the experiment may measure a different process or include unaccounted heat transfers. A good report explains assumptions and identifies which corrections are likely to be most important.

5.36 Worked numerical and conceptual examples

The examples below illustrate the choice of equation, unit conversion, sign convention and interpretation. In an examination, show the governing relation before substituting values, retain units through the calculation, and state what the sign means physically.

Worked Example 5.1: Calorimetric heat

Question. A 100.0 g solution has c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and warms from 22.0 °C to 28.5 °C. Calculate the heat absorbed by the solution.

Solution. q = mcΔT = (100.0)(4.18)(6.5) = 2717 J = 2.72 kJ. The solution absorbs 2.72 kJ. If the reaction is the only other heat source and the calorimeter heat capacity is negligible, qreaction = −2.72 kJ.

Worked Example 5.2: Expansion work

Question. A gas expands from 2.00 L to 5.50 L against a constant external pressure of 1.20 bar. Calculate w.

Solution. ΔV = 3.50 L. w = −PextΔV = −(1.20 bar)(3.50 L) = −4.20 L bar. Since 1 L bar = 100 J, w = −420 J. The negative sign means the system performs work on the surroundings.

Worked Example 5.3: First-law bookkeeping

Question. A system absorbs 8.0 kJ of heat and has 2.5 kJ of work done on it. Find ΔU.

Solution. Using ΔU = q + w, q = +8.0 kJ and w = +2.5 kJ. Therefore ΔU = +10.5 kJ.

Worked Example 5.4: Enthalpy from formation data

Question. For a balanced reaction, ΣνΔfH° of products is −850 kJ mol⁻¹ and the corresponding reactant sum is −620 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔrH°.

Solution. ΔrH° = products − reactants = −850 − (−620) = −230 kJ mol⁻¹. The reaction is exothermic as written.

Worked Example 5.5: Ideal-gas ΔH and ΔU

Question. At 298 K a gas reaction has Δng = +2.00. Estimate ΔH − ΔU.

Solution. ΔH − ΔU = ΔngRT = (2.00)(8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(298 K) = 4.95 kJ mol⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.6: Entropy of heating

Question. One mole of an ideal gas is heated reversibly from 300 K to 450 K at constant pressure. Cp,m = 29.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find ΔS.

Solution. ΔS = nCp,m ln(T2/T1) = (1)(29.1)ln(450/300) = 11.8 J K⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.7: Entropy of melting

Question. A solid melts reversibly at 273.15 K with ΔfusH = 6.01 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔfusS.

Solution. ΔfusS = ΔfusH/T = 6010/273.15 = 22.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.8: Gibbs energy

Question. At 298 K, ΔH = −40.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −80.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate ΔG.

Solution. Convert entropy to kJ: −0.0800 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. ΔG = −40.0 − 298(−0.0800) = −16.2 kJ mol⁻¹. The forward direction is favourable at 298 K under constant T,P.

Worked Example 5.9: Crossover temperature

Question. A process has ΔH = +25.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +75.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Estimate the temperature above which it is favourable.

Solution. T = ΔH/ΔS = 25000/75.0 = 333 K. Above about 333 K, ΔG is negative, assuming ΔH and ΔS are approximately constant.

Worked Example 5.10: Equilibrium constant from ΔG°

Question. At 298 K, ΔrG° = −5.70 kJ mol⁻¹. Estimate K.

Solution. ln K = −ΔG°/(RT) = 5700/(8.314×298) = 2.30. K = e²·³⁰ ≈ 9.97. The equilibrium constant is about 10.

Worked Example 5.11: Reaction direction from Q

Question. At 298 K, ΔrG° = +4.00 kJ mol⁻¹ and Q = 0.10. Find ΔrG.

Solution. ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Q = 4000 + (8.314)(298)ln(0.10) = −1.70 kJ mol⁻¹. Although ΔrG° is positive, the current mixture favours the forward direction.

Worked Example 5.12: van ’t Hoff estimate

Question. An endothermic reaction has ΔH° = +30.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Estimate ln(K2/K1) when temperature rises from 300 K to 350 K.

Solution. ln(K2/K1) = −ΔH°/R(1/T2 − 1/T1) = −30000/8.314(1/350 − 1/300) = 1.72. Thus K2/K1 = e¹·⁷² ≈ 5.6.

Worked Example 5.13: Electrical work and Gibbs energy

Question. A cell reaction transfers 2 mol electrons and has Ecell = 1.10 V. Calculate ΔG.

Solution. ΔG = −nFE = −(2)(96485 C mol⁻¹)(1.10 J C⁻¹) = −212 kJ mol⁻¹. The negative value indicates a favourable cell reaction as written.

Worked Example 5.14: Carnot efficiency

Question. An ideal engine operates between 600 K and 300 K. Find its maximum efficiency.

Solution. η = 1 − Tc/Th = 1 − 300/600 = 0.50, or 50%. A real engine must be less efficient.

Worked Example 5.15: Ideal entropy of mixing

Question. Mix 1.0 mol A and 1.0 mol B ideally. Calculate ΔmixS.

Solution. Each mole fraction is 0.5. ΔmixS = −RΣni ln xi = −R[ln(0.5)+ln(0.5)] = 11.53 J K⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.16: Heat-capacity correction

Question. ΔrCp = +20.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. If ΔrH at 298 K is −100.0 kJ mol⁻¹, estimate it at 350 K.

Solution. ΔrH(350) = ΔrH(298) + ΔrCpΔT = −100.0 + (0.0200)(52) = −98.96 kJ mol⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.17: Bomb calorimeter

Question. A bomb calorimeter has total heat capacity 9.50 kJ K⁻¹. Combustion raises its temperature by 1.25 K. Find qcal and qrxn.

Solution. qcal = CΔT = (9.50)(1.25) = +11.9 kJ. The reaction releases this heat, so qrxn = −11.9 kJ at constant volume, subject to calibration and correction assumptions.

Worked Example 5.18: Clausius–Clapeyron

Question. A liquid has ΔHvap = 40.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Estimate ln(P2/P1) from 350 K to 370 K.

Solution. ln(P2/P1) = −ΔHvap/R(1/T2 − 1/T1) = −40000/8.314(1/370 − 1/350) = 0.744. Therefore P2/P1 ≈ 2.10.

Worked Example 5.19: Chemical potential change

Question. For an ideal gas at 298 K, its partial pressure changes from 1.0 bar to 0.10 bar. Calculate Δμ.

Solution. Δμ = RT ln(p2/p1) = (8.314)(298)ln(0.10) = −5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. Lowering the partial pressure lowers the chemical potential.

Worked Example 5.20: Nernst equation

Question. At 298 K, E° = 0.80 V, n = 2 and Q = 100. Calculate E.

Solution. E = E° − (0.05916/n)log Q = 0.80 − (0.05916/2)(2) = 0.7408 V.

Worked Example 5.21: Entropy of ideal-gas expansion

Question. One mole of an ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly from 5.0 L to 20.0 L. Calculate ΔS.

Solution. ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) = (1)(8.314)ln(4) = 11.53 J K⁻¹.

Worked Example 5.22: Formation enthalpy via Hess’s law

Question. A target reaction is obtained by adding two steps with enthalpies +45 kJ mol⁻¹ and −120 kJ mol⁻¹. Find the overall enthalpy.

Solution. By Hess’s law, ΔHoverall = +45 − 120 = −75 kJ mol⁻¹. The algebra is valid only if the summed equations produce the target balanced reaction.

Worked Example 5.23: Equilibrium composition interpretation

Question. A reaction has K = 2.0×10⁵ at a fixed temperature. Does this prove the reaction is fast?

Solution. No. K indicates the equilibrium position is product-favoured under the defined standard states. It gives no direct information about activation energy or reaction rate. Kinetics is needed to assess speed.

Worked Example 5.24: Distinguish ΔH and ΔG

Question. A process has ΔH = +10 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔG = −2 kJ mol⁻¹ at a stated temperature and pressure. Interpret.

Solution. The process is endothermic at constant pressure, but thermodynamically spontaneous in the stated forward direction because ΔG is negative. The entropy contribution is sufficiently favourable to overcome the positive enthalpy term.

5.37 Conceptual revision questions with suggested answers

1. Explain why heat and work are not state functions, whereas internal energy is.

Suggested answer: Heat and work describe energy transferred along a path and depend on how a change occurs. Internal energy is a property of the state, so its change depends only on initial and final states.

2. A gas is compressed against a constant external pressure. What is the sign of PV work using the chemistry convention?

Suggested answer: Compression gives ΔV < 0, so w = −PextΔV is positive: work is done on the system.

3. Why does a bomb calorimeter measure ΔU rather than directly measuring ΔH?

Suggested answer: The reaction occurs at constant volume, so pressure–volume work is zero and qv = ΔU for the usual closed-system assumptions.

4. State Hess's law and one practical use.

Suggested answer: Enthalpy change is independent of the route between the same initial and final states. It allows inaccessible or difficult reaction enthalpies to be calculated from measurable steps.

5. Why must physical states be included in thermochemical equations?

Suggested answer: Different phases have different enthalpies; for example, liquid water and water vapour have different standard formation enthalpies.

6. Explain why breaking a chemical bond is endothermic.

Suggested answer: Energy must be supplied to overcome the attractive interactions holding the bonded atoms together.

7. Does a negative ΔH guarantee spontaneity? Explain.

Suggested answer: No. Spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure depends on ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, not on enthalpy alone.

8. A reaction has ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0. How does temperature affect its favourability?

Suggested answer: It becomes favourable at sufficiently high temperature, where the positive TΔS term can outweigh ΔH.

9. What is the physical meaning of ΔG = 0 for a reaction at fixed T and P?

Suggested answer: The system is at chemical equilibrium with no net thermodynamic driving force in either reaction direction.

10. Why does a catalyst not change the equilibrium constant?

Suggested answer: A catalyst lowers kinetic barriers for forward and reverse reactions but does not change the thermodynamic state functions of reactants and products.

11. Distinguish standard Gibbs energy from Gibbs energy under actual conditions.

Suggested answer: ΔrG° refers to standard-state activities. ΔrG includes the actual reaction quotient through ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Q.

12. Why are activities preferred over concentrations in rigorous equilibrium expressions?

Suggested answer: Activities account for non-ideal interactions; concentrations alone do not generally represent effective thermodynamic behaviour.

13. What does the second law say about entropy generation?

Suggested answer: Entropy generation is non-negative; it is zero for a reversible process and positive for an irreversible process.

14. Why is entropy of mixing positive for an ideal mixture?

Suggested answer: Mixing increases the number of accessible arrangements, and −ΣniR ln xi is positive for mole fractions between zero and one.

15. How is the maximum efficiency of a heat engine determined?

Suggested answer: The Carnot limit η = 1 − Tc/Th, with absolute temperatures, sets the maximum efficiency between two reservoirs.

16. What does a positive standard cell potential imply for the cell reaction as written?

Suggested answer: ΔG° = −nFE° is negative, so the reaction is thermodynamically favourable under standard conditions.

17. Explain the difference between thermodynamic stability and kinetic persistence.

Suggested answer: Thermodynamic stability concerns the lowest relevant free energy; kinetic persistence concerns barriers that may prevent a system from reaching that state quickly.

18. List four experimental sources of error in calorimetry.

Suggested answer: Heat exchange with surroundings, incomplete reaction, inaccurate mass or temperature measurement, and unaccounted calorimeter heat capacity are examples.

5.39 Common misconceptions and examination pitfalls

  • Do not call heat a state function or describe a body as “containing heat”; a body has internal energy and heat is energy transfer.
  • Do not reverse the chemistry sign convention halfway through a calculation. Expansion work is negative when the system expands against positive external pressure.
  • Do not assume q = ΔH unless the process is at constant pressure and only pressure–volume work is relevant.
  • Do not infer spontaneity from ΔH alone. Use ΔG under constant temperature and pressure, or total entropy for an isolated universe.
  • Do not confuse ΔrG° with ΔrG. Actual composition enters through RT ln Q.
  • Do not interpret K as a rate constant. Equilibrium thermodynamics and kinetics answer different questions.
  • Do not use Celsius in logarithmic temperature expressions or in RT terms.
  • Do not omit phase labels, stoichiometric coefficients or activity conventions from thermochemical calculations.
  • Do not assume all real mixtures are ideal. Use activity coefficients or fugacity where non-ideality is significant.
