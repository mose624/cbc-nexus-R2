1. Introduction to medical microbiology

Microbiology studies microorganisms and their interactions with hosts and environments. Organisms relevant to human health include bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa and helminths. Not every microorganism causes disease: normal microbiota contribute to colonisation resistance, metabolism and immune development. Infection occurs when an organism enters, survives or multiplies in a host and produces a response; disease depends on organism factors and host susceptibility.

Distinguish contamination, colonisation and infection. Contamination is the unintended presence of microorganisms in a specimen or on a surface; colonisation means organisms are present and multiplying without necessarily causing tissue damage or symptoms; infection involves host invasion and response and may or may not produce overt clinical illness. This distinction is important when interpreting cultures and avoiding unnecessary antimicrobial use.

2. Bacterial structure and classification

Bacteria are prokaryotic cells without a membrane-bound nucleus. Typical structures include a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and a peptidoglycan cell wall; some have capsules, flagella, pili or endospores. Bacteria are commonly described by shape (cocci, bacilli, spirals), arrangement, staining characteristics, oxygen requirements and biochemical properties.

Gram-positive bacteria retain the primary stain and appear purple in Gram staining because of their thick peptidoglycan layer; Gram-negative bacteria appear pink after counterstaining and have an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide. These are broad laboratory categories, not complete clinical diagnoses. Culture and susceptibility results must be interpreted with specimen quality, symptoms and local laboratory guidance.

3. Viruses, fungi and parasites

Viruses are acellular infectious agents containing DNA or RNA within a protein capsid; some have a lipid envelope. They depend on host cells for replication. Antiviral medicines target selected stages of viral replication and are organism-specific. Fungi are eukaryotes and include yeasts and moulds; fungal disease ranges from superficial infection to invasive illness, particularly in people with impaired immunity.

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic parasites; helminths are multicellular parasitic worms. Transmission may occur through contaminated food or water, vectors, soil, direct contact or other routes. Prevention depends on the organism's life cycle and route of spread, and may include sanitation, safe water, food hygiene, vector control, vaccination where available and appropriate treatment.

4. Normal microbiota and host-microbe relationships

Normal microbiota occupy skin, oral cavity, respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and other body sites. They compete with pathogens, produce metabolites and interact with immune defences. Composition varies with age, diet, environment, medicines and health. Disruption by antibiotics or illness can permit opportunistic organisms to overgrow.

Microbiota are site-specific. A microorganism found in a non-sterile site may represent normal colonisation, contamination or disease depending on the context. Nurses should avoid interpreting a positive culture in isolation and should communicate clinical signs, specimen source and collection method to the responsible team.

5. Chain of infection and transmission

The chain of infection is commonly represented by an infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host. Breaking any link can reduce spread. Reservoirs include people, animals, water, food and contaminated environments. Transmission may be contact, droplet, airborne, vehicle-borne or vector-borne, depending on the pathogen.

In healthcare, hands, shared equipment, surfaces, respiratory particles, invasive devices and contaminated materials can contribute to transmission. Apply standard precautions consistently and additional transmission-based precautions when indicated. Use local signage and infection-prevention advice because required isolation measures differ by pathogen and setting.

6. Innate immunity

Innate immunity provides rapid, non-specific defence. Physical and chemical barriers include skin, mucous membranes, cilia, gastric acidity and antimicrobial substances. Cellular components include neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells and mast cells. Pattern-recognition receptors detect conserved microbial or damage-associated signals and activate inflammatory pathways.

Inflammation helps recruit immune cells and plasma proteins to affected tissues. Cardinal features are redness, heat, swelling, pain and altered function, although not all are present in every case. Fever is a regulated rise in body temperature mediated by inflammatory signalling. Excessive or dysregulated inflammation can damage host tissues.

7. Adaptive immunity and antibodies

Adaptive immunity is antigen-specific and develops immunological memory. B lymphocytes can differentiate into plasma cells that produce antibodies. T lymphocytes include helper cells that coordinate responses and cytotoxic cells that kill infected or abnormal cells. Antigen presentation by major histocompatibility complex molecules helps activate T cells.

Antibodies bind antigens and can neutralise pathogens or toxins, promote phagocytosis and activate complement. Primary responses are generally slower than secondary responses; memory cells support faster responses after re-exposure. Vaccination safely presents antigen or antigen instructions to train immune memory without requiring the person to experience the target disease in its full form.

8. Immunisation, hypersensitivity and immunodeficiency

Vaccines reduce the risk of selected infections and severe outcomes. Vaccine types include live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, toxoid, viral-vector and nucleic-acid platforms. Schedules, contraindications and catch-up recommendations vary by country and change over time; use current national guidance. Cold-chain management protects vaccine potency.

Hypersensitivity reactions are inappropriate or excessive immune responses and are classified into several mechanisms. Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic reaction requiring immediate emergency response under local protocol. Immunodeficiency may be primary or acquired and can increase susceptibility to unusual, recurrent or severe infections.

9. Healthcare-associated infections and device safety

Healthcare-associated infections develop during or following healthcare and were not necessarily present or incubating at the time of admission. Common categories include surgical-site infections, catheter-associated urinary tract infections, central-line-associated bloodstream infections and ventilator-associated events. Risk is influenced by patient factors, device exposure, duration, aseptic practice and environmental conditions.

Prevention includes avoiding unnecessary devices, reviewing ongoing need, using aseptic insertion and maintenance practices, hand hygiene, environmental cleaning, appropriate surveillance and timely removal under approved protocols. Nurses play a key role in noticing early signs, maintaining device care and communicating concerns.

10. Specimen collection and laboratory principles

Reliable laboratory results begin with the correct patient, correct specimen, correct container, correct timing and correct labelling. Follow the laboratory's current instructions for collection, transport, storage and special precautions. Use aseptic technique where required, collect adequate volume and avoid contamination. Label specimens at the point of collection according to policy, with required identifiers, source, date and time.

A culture identifies organisms that grow under specified laboratory conditions; susceptibility testing estimates response to selected antimicrobial agents. A negative culture does not always exclude infection, and a positive result does not always prove invasive disease. Results should be interpreted with symptoms, specimen quality, prior treatment and clinical assessment.

11. Antimicrobial stewardship and resistance

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microorganisms survive medicines that would previously have inhibited or killed them. Resistance is selected and spread through genetic mutation, gene transfer, antimicrobial exposure and transmission. Misuse includes unnecessary treatment, incorrect drug choice, dose or duration and sharing leftover medicines.

Antimicrobial stewardship promotes the right antimicrobial, for the right patient, at the right dose, route and duration, with review when results become available. Nurses support stewardship by obtaining specimens correctly when ordered, administering prescribed treatment safely, monitoring response and adverse effects, documenting allergies and educating patients not to share or self-adjust medicines.

12. Occupational exposure, outbreak response and prevention culture

Needlestick and sharps injuries require immediate first aid and prompt reporting under institutional policy. Do not recap used needles unless an approved safety device or procedure specifically requires it. Follow local exposure-assessment pathways, including confidential evaluation, testing and time-sensitive post-exposure measures when indicated. Never delay reporting while trying to determine personal risk alone.

An outbreak is an occurrence of cases above the expected level in a defined place and period. Response may include case identification, line listing, testing, isolation or cohorting, environmental review, communication and public-health notification. Students should report clusters or unusual patterns promptly and follow the infection-prevention team's directions.

Integrated case-based learning

Case: A student nurse is assigned to observe an adult patient during a supervised learning placement. The patient has a change from their usual condition and expresses concern about the proposed care. The student must gather relevant information, listen respectfully, check identity and consent, consider immediate safety, communicate findings to the supervising nurse and document objectively.

Discussion: Identify the information that is subjective and objective. Which findings require prompt escalation? What additional context would help interpretation? How can the student preserve dignity and confidentiality? Which actions are within the student's authorised competence, and which require direct supervision? Explain how the chapter's concepts support safe decisions without attempting to diagnose or prescribe independently.

Learning point: Sound nursing practice combines scientific knowledge, careful observation, communication, ethical conduct, teamwork and timely escalation. A textbook scenario is not a substitute for local protocols or direct clinical supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For longer questions, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and relate the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define nursing and identify four major purposes of nursing practice.

    Show answer guidance

    Nursing is a professional discipline that promotes health, prevents illness, supports recovery, relieves suffering and protects dignity. Examples include health promotion, prevention, care coordination and rehabilitation.

  2. Outline the five stages of the nursing process.

    Show answer guidance

    Assessment; analysis or nursing diagnosis; planning; implementation; evaluation. The process is iterative and should be revised when new information emerges.

  3. Differentiate autonomy and beneficence.

    Show answer guidance

    Autonomy concerns respect for a person's informed choices; beneficence concerns acting to promote wellbeing. Ethical decisions may require balancing these and other principles.

  4. State three features of effective therapeutic communication.

    Show answer guidance

    Active listening, open questions, clarification, empathy, summarising and checking understanding are examples.

  5. Explain why accurate documentation is important.

    Show answer guidance

    It supports continuity, communication, accountability, evaluation, legal record-keeping and patient safety.

  6. Differentiate anatomy from physiology.

    Show answer guidance

    Anatomy studies body structures and their relationships; physiology studies how those structures function.

  7. Name the four primary tissue classes.

    Show answer guidance

    Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

  8. Describe the role of the plasma membrane.

    Show answer guidance

    It forms a selectively permeable boundary, regulates transport, supports signalling and contributes to cell recognition.

  9. State two functions of the integumentary system.

    Show answer guidance

    Barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, limiting water loss and vitamin D synthesis.

  10. Explain the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems.

    Show answer guidance

    The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS.

  11. Trace the normal flow of blood through the heart.

    Show answer guidance

    Venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  12. What is cardiac output?

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute; it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.

  13. State the main function of alveoli.

    Show answer guidance

    Alveoli provide a large, thin surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

  14. Name the three basic processes of urine formation.

    Show answer guidance

    Glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion.

  15. List the links in the chain of infection.

    Show answer guidance

    Infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host.
