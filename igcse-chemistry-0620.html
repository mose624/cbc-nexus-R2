UNIT 1 • CORE + SUPPLEMENT

1. States of matter

Learning outcomes: distinguish solids, liquids and gases; describe particle separation, arrangement and motion; explain changes of state; relate gas volume to temperature and pressure; and explain diffusion using kinetic particle theory.

1.1 The particle model

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions). The particles are in constant motion. Their spacing, arrangement, speed and attractive forces help explain the properties of each state.

SolidFixed shape and volume. Particles are close together in a regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions.
LiquidFixed volume but no fixed shape. Particles are close together, irregularly arranged and slide past one another.
GasNo fixed shape or volume. Particles are far apart, move rapidly and randomly, and fill the container.
PropertySolidLiquidGas
ShapeFixedTakes container shapeFills container
VolumeFixedFixed (approximately)Not fixed; compressible
Particle spacingVery closeCloseFar apart
Particle motionVibrate about fixed positionsMove/slide past each otherRapid, random motion
CompressibilityVery lowVery lowHigh

1.2 Changes of state

Melting is the change from solid to liquid; freezing is liquid to solid; boiling and evaporation are liquid to gas; condensation is gas to liquid; sublimation is solid directly to gas. During a change of state, energy is transferred but the temperature of a pure substance remains constant while the change is taking place at a fixed pressure.

1.3 Kinetic particle theory and heating curves

When a substance is heated, its particles gain energy. In a single state, the average kinetic energy and temperature increase. At the melting or boiling point, energy is used to overcome attractive forces and separate particles rather than increase their average kinetic energy; therefore the temperature stays constant until the change is complete. During cooling, the reverse occurs and energy is released to the surroundings.

Exam distinction: Boiling occurs throughout a liquid at its boiling point. Evaporation occurs only at the surface and can happen below the boiling point. Evaporation causes cooling because the higher-energy particles escape, lowering the average energy of those left behind.

1.4 Temperature and pressure effects on gas volume

Temperature: at constant pressure, heating a fixed amount of gas increases its volume. Gas particles move faster and collide more frequently/forcefully; the gas expands until its pressure balances the external pressure. Cooling reduces the volume.

Pressure: at constant temperature, increasing external pressure compresses a gas and decreases its volume. Decreasing pressure allows the gas to expand. Gases are compressible because there is substantial empty space between their particles.

1.5 Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration due to their random motion, until the particles become more evenly distributed. It occurs in gases and liquids and is generally faster in gases because gas particles move rapidly and are far apart.

Effect of relative molecular mass (Supplement): at the same temperature, lighter gas molecules generally diffuse faster than heavier molecules because their average speeds are higher. For example, ammonia (Mᵣ = 17) diffuses faster than hydrogen chloride (Mᵣ ≈ 36.5). In a tube, the white ammonium chloride ring forms closer to the HCl end because NH₃ travels farther in the same time.

Worked example

Question: A sealed flexible balloon is warmed while the external pressure remains approximately constant. Explain why its volume increases.

Solution: Heating transfers energy to the gas particles. Their average kinetic energy and speed increase. They collide more frequently and forcefully with the balloon walls. The balloon expands, increasing its volume, until the internal pressure again balances the external pressure.

1.6 Evaporation: factors that affect its rate

Evaporation happens when particles at the surface of a liquid have enough energy to escape into the gas state. It can occur at temperatures below the boiling point. The rate increases when:

  • Temperature increases: a greater proportion of particles have enough kinetic energy to escape.
  • Surface area increases: more particles are exposed at the surface at the same time.
  • Air movement increases: moving air carries vapour away from the surface, reducing the amount of vapour close to the liquid.
  • Humidity decreases: drier air can accept more water vapour before becoming saturated.
Application: Wet clothes dry faster on a warm, windy day than on a cool, still, humid day. Drying is evaporation, not boiling.

1.7 Gas pressure explained by collisions

Gas particles move randomly and collide with the walls of their container. Each collision changes the particles' momentum and exerts a force on the wall. The combined force per unit area is the gas pressure. At a fixed temperature, compressing a gas into a smaller volume makes wall collisions more frequent, so pressure increases.

1.8 Diffusion investigation: ammonia and hydrogen chloride

When cotton wool soaked in concentrated aqueous ammonia and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a long glass tube, the gases diffuse towards each other. A white ring of solid ammonium chloride forms where they meet.

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

Practical safety: Concentrated ammonia and hydrochloric acid are corrosive/irritant and release harmful vapours. This demonstration must be performed by a trained teacher in a fume cupboard, using appropriate eye protection and laboratory controls. Learners should use a teacher demonstration or approved simulation, not attempt it unsupervised.

1.9 Exam-style application questions

6. A student spreads the same volume of water over a wide dish and a narrow dish. The wide dish dries first. Explain why.
Marking points (2): the wide dish has a larger surface area; more water particles are at the surface and can escape per unit time, increasing the rate of evaporation.
7. Explain, using particles, why the pressure of a fixed mass of gas increases when its volume is reduced at constant temperature.
Marking points (3): particles have the same average kinetic energy at constant temperature; they have less distance to travel between wall collisions / collide with walls more frequently; force per unit area increases, so pressure rises.
8. In the NH₃/HCl diffusion experiment, state where the white ring forms and explain why.
Marking points (2): nearer the HCl end; ammonia has a lower relative molecular mass and diffuses faster/further in the same time.

Common misconceptions to avoid

  • Particles themselves do not expand significantly when heated; the average separation changes.
  • Particles in a solid are not stationary; they vibrate about fixed positions.
  • During melting or boiling of a pure substance at fixed pressure, temperature does not rise until the change is complete.
  • Diffusion is not the same as bulk movement caused by stirring or wind; it results from random particle motion.

Quick knowledge check

1. Which state has particles that are close together but arranged irregularly and able to slide past one another?
A Solid   B Liquid   C Gas   D Plasma
Answer: B — Liquid. Liquids have close, irregularly arranged particles that can move past each other.
2. Explain why a gas is easily compressed.
Marking points: gas particles are far apart; there is a large amount of empty space; pressure can force particles closer together.
3. State what happens to temperature while a pure liquid boils at a fixed pressure, and explain why.
Marking points: temperature remains constant; supplied energy is used to overcome attractive forces/separate particles rather than increase average kinetic energy.
4. Define diffusion.
Answer: the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration due to random motion.
5. A gas sample is compressed at constant temperature. Describe the change in volume and particle spacing.
Answer: volume decreases; particles become closer together. Their average kinetic energy remains constant because temperature is unchanged.

Self-assessment: explain each answer aloud, draw the particle diagrams from memory, and practise applying the model to unfamiliar situations.

2. Atoms, elements and compounds

Learning outcomes: distinguish elements, compounds and mixtures; describe the structure of atoms; use proton number and nucleon number; interpret isotopes; write electronic configurations for the first 20 elements; explain ion formation and ionic bonding; describe covalent and metallic bonding; and relate bonding and structure to physical properties.

2.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures

An element is a substance consisting of only one type of atom. A compound contains two or more different elements chemically bonded together in fixed proportions. A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically bonded and can be present in any proportion.

FeatureElementCompoundMixture
CompositionOne type of atomDifferent elements chemically combinedTwo or more substances physically combined

ExampleIron, oxygenWater, sodium chlorideAir, seawater
Exam tip: In a mixture, substances are not chemically bonded. In a compound, atoms are joined by chemical bonds and the elements are present in a fixed ratio. Do not describe air as a compound: it is a mixture of gases.

2.2 Inside the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons. Negatively charged electrons occupy shells (energy levels) around the nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

2.3 Proton number and nucleon number

Proton number (atomic number, Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus. It identifies the element. Nucleon number (mass number, A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Number of neutrons = A − Z

For a neutral atom: number of electrons = number of protons. For an ion, the proton number remains unchanged but the number of electrons differs.

Worked example: sodium-23Sodium has proton number 11 and nucleon number 23. Protons = 11; neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12; electrons in a neutral atom = 11. Its electronic configuration is 2,8,1.

2.4 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same proton number and different nucleon numbers. Neutral isotopes have the same number of electrons and therefore very similar chemical properties, because chemical reactions depend mainly on electron arrangement. Their physical properties, such as mass, can differ.

2.5 Electronic configuration and the Periodic Table

Electrons occupy shells around the nucleus. For the first 20 elements at this level, the shell capacities used in simple configurations are 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second, 8 in the third, then 2 in the fourth. Electrons fill the lower-energy shells first. The number of occupied shells indicates the period; for main-group elements, the number of outer-shell electrons helps identify the group.

Exam method: For a neutral atom, start with its proton number (also its electron number). Fill shells from the inside out. Check that the shell numbers add up to the total electrons.

2.6 Ions and ionic bonding

An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons. Metals usually lose electrons to form positive ions (cations); non-metals usually gain electrons to form negative ions (anions). The nucleus does not change during ordinary ion formation.

Ionic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It commonly forms when electrons transfer from a metal atom to a non-metal atom. The resulting ionic compound is electrically neutral overall.

Common ion charges: Group I metals form 1+ ions; Group II metals form 2+ ions; Group III metals commonly form 3+ ions; Group VI non-metals commonly form 2− ions; Group VII non-metals form 1− ions. Learn the ions specified by your syllabus and practise balancing total charge.

Worked formula example: magnesium chloride. Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine forms Cl⁻. Two chloride ions are needed to balance one magnesium ion: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0. The formula is MgCl₂.

2.7 Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points: strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in a giant ionic lattice require much energy to overcome.
  • Conduct electricity when molten or in aqueous solution: ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Do not conduct when solid: ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move through the lattice.
  • Often brittle: shifting layers can bring like charges next to each other; repulsion causes the crystal to split.

2.8 Covalent bonding

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. It forms mainly between non-metal atoms. Each atom contributes one electron to each shared pair in a simple covalent bond. Atoms share electrons to achieve a more stable outer-shell arrangement.

Simple molecular substances: molecules contain strong covalent bonds within each molecule, but the forces between molecules are usually much weaker. Therefore many simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points and do not conduct electricity because they have no mobile ions or delocalised electrons.

Worked example: methane, CH₄. Carbon has four outer-shell electrons. It shares one pair with each of four hydrogen atoms, forming four single covalent bonds. Each hydrogen achieves two electrons in its first shell; carbon achieves eight in its outer shell.

2.9 Giant covalent structures

In giant covalent structures, many atoms are joined by a continuous network of covalent bonds. A large amount of energy is needed to break these bonds, so they generally have very high melting points.

StructureBonding and arrangementProperties and uses
DiamondEach carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds in a rigid three-dimensional network.Very hard; very high melting point; does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile charged particles. Used in cutting tools and jewellery.
GraphiteEach carbon forms three covalent bonds in layers of hexagonal rings. One electron per carbon is delocalised.Layers slide over one another, so graphite is soft/slippery. Delocalised electrons allow electrical conduction. Used in electrodes and lubricants.
Silicon dioxideGiant three-dimensional network of silicon and oxygen atoms joined by strong covalent bonds.Hard, high melting point and does not conduct electricity. Found in sand/quartz and used in glass manufacture.

2.10 Metallic bonding

Metals consist of a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons. Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

Metals usually conduct electricity because delocalised electrons can move through the structure and carry charge. They conduct heat because mobile electrons transfer energy. They are malleable and ductile because layers of ions can slide while attraction to the electron sea maintains the metallic bond.

2.12 Exam technique: how to earn explanation marks

  • For ionic bonding, use the words electron transfer, oppositely charged ions and electrostatic attraction.
  • For covalent bonding, state that atoms share pairs of electrons.
  • For metallic bonding, mention positive ions, delocalised electrons and electrostatic attraction.
  • When explaining melting points, identify the structure and the forces/bonds that must be overcome.
  • When explaining conductivity, identify the mobile charged particles (ions or electrons).

Worked examples

Example 1: particle numbers in an ion An aluminium ion is Al³⁺. Aluminium has proton number 13 and nucleon number 27. Protons = 13; neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14; electrons = 13 − 3 = 10. The positive charge means three electrons have been lost.
Example 2: formula from ionic charges Calcium forms Ca²⁺ and oxide forms O²⁻. A 1:1 ratio balances the charges: (+2) + (−2) = 0. The formula is CaO. Always simplify to the smallest whole-number ratio.
Example 3: explaining graphite conductivity Graphite conducts electricity because each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, leaving one electron per carbon delocalised. These electrons are free to move along the layers and carry charge.

Common misconceptions

  • Isotopes are not different elements: they have the same proton number.
  • When an atom forms an ion, electrons change; the number of protons does not.
  • Ionic compounds are not made of separate molecules; they form giant ionic lattices.
  • Strong covalent bonds within molecules are not the same as the weaker forces between simple molecules.
  • Graphite conducts because of delocalised electrons, not because its layers slide.
  • Diamond does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile charged particles.

Topic 2 knowledge and exam check

1. Define an isotope.
Marking points (2): atoms of the same element / same number of protons; different numbers of neutrons.
2. An atom has proton number 16 and nucleon number 32. State its numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Answer (3): protons = 16; neutrons = 32 − 16 = 16; electrons = 16 for a neutral atom.
3. Explain why sodium forms Na⁺ ions.
Marking points (2): sodium loses one electron; it forms a positively charged ion with a stable outer-shell arrangement.
4. Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but molten sodium chloride does.
Marking points (3): in the solid, ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move; when molten, ions are mobile; moving ions carry charge.
5. Describe covalent bonding in a hydrogen molecule.
Marking points (2): each hydrogen atom contributes one electron; the atoms share a pair of electrons.
6. Explain why diamond is hard but graphite is soft and slippery.
Marking points (4): diamond has a rigid 3D network; each carbon forms four strong covalent bonds; graphite has layers; weak forces between layers allow them to slide.
7. Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine forms Cl⁻. Deduce the formula of magnesium chloride and show the charge balance.
Answer (2): MgCl₂; one Mg²⁺ is balanced by two Cl⁻ ions: +2 + 2(−1) = 0.
8. Explain why metals conduct electricity and can be hammered into shape.
Marking points (3): delocalised electrons move and carry charge; layers of metal ions can slide; attraction between ions and delocalised electrons remains, so the metal does not shatter easily.
9. A substance has a low boiling point and does not conduct electricity. Suggest its structure and explain the observations.
Marking points (3): simple molecular structure; weak intermolecular forces require little energy to overcome; no mobile ions or delocalised electrons to carry charge.
10. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but silicon dioxide does not.
Marking points (3): graphite has delocalised electrons that can move and carry charge; silicon dioxide has electrons localised in covalent bonds; it has no mobile charged particles.
