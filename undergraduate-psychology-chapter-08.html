BACHELOR OF PSYCHOLOGY · CHAPTER 8

Abnormal Psychology and Mental Health

A critical and culturally responsive introduction to psychological distress, classification, assessment, interventions, prevention and recovery. Educational notes; not a diagnostic instrument or substitute for professional care.

Learning outcomes: Explain models of abnormality; evaluate classification systems; describe major diagnostic groups without stereotyping; outline assessment and case formulation; compare interventions; and discuss ethics, culture and public health.

1. Meaning and scope of abnormal psychology

Abnormal psychology is the scientific study of patterns of thought, emotion and behaviour associated with clinically significant distress, impairment, risk or difficulty adapting to a context. The field asks how such patterns arise, what maintains them, how they differ across people and cultures, and which forms of support reduce suffering. The term abnormal is descriptive within a discipline; it is not a judgement about a person's worth, character or social value. People are always more than their diagnoses.

No single feature separates normal from abnormal experience. Statistical rarity may describe an uncommon trait, but rarity alone does not make it a disorder. Violation of social norms is culturally dependent and can reflect unjust norms. Subjective distress is important, although some people with serious difficulties may have limited insight. Functional impairment examines effects on education, work, relationships, self-care and participation. Duration, severity, developmental stage and safety also matter. Clinical judgement therefore combines several dimensions with the person's own account and the circumstances in which the experience occurs.

For example, intense grief after a death can involve sleep disturbance, reduced concentration and sadness without necessarily indicating a mental disorder. The assessor explores the person's cultural expectations, course of grief, functioning, support and any signs of additional risk. Conversely, a person may minimise symptoms because of stigma or fear of consequences. The task is not to label every painful emotion, but to recognise when support is needed and to avoid overlooking serious impairment.

2. Historical development of explanations

Historical explanations of mental distress have reflected scientific knowledge, spiritual beliefs, institutions and social power. Communities have interpreted distress through spiritual, supernatural, moral and bodily frameworks. These interpretations sometimes offered meaning and social support, but they could also lead to fear, exclusion and harmful practices. Medical traditions gradually investigated bodily processes, while the growth of psychiatric institutions created systems of observation and care that also included coercion and neglect. The history is therefore neither a simple tale of progress nor a reason to reject scientific inquiry.

Psychodynamic approaches emphasised unconscious conflict and early relationships; behavioural approaches studied conditioning and reinforcement; cognitive approaches examined interpretation, attention and memory; humanistic approaches highlighted agency, empathy and meaning; biological approaches investigated genes, brain systems and physical health; and family or systemic approaches studied interaction patterns. Each tradition introduced useful concepts, but each has limitations when treated as a complete explanation. Contemporary practice tests mechanisms and interventions through research and integrates approaches according to the needs of the individual.

Historical awareness requires asking who has had the power to define disorder and whose behaviour has been pathologised. Gender, disability, race, sexuality, class, colonial history and cultural difference have shaped psychiatric classification and treatment. Modern practice must learn from past abuses by protecting human rights, informed consent, privacy and least-restrictive care. People with lived experience should help shape research and services. Scientific progress is meaningful only when it improves wellbeing without repeating patterns of exclusion or dehumanisation.

3. Biological, psychological and social models

The biological model examines genetic influences, brain development, neural circuits, hormones, sleep, physical illness and substance effects. Biological factors may influence vulnerability, but they do not determine a person's future. Genes operate within environments, and similar predispositions can lead to different outcomes depending on stress exposure, relationships, development and access to support. Biological explanations can reduce blame when evidence supports them, but become misleading when they imply that every difficulty is fixed or caused by a single chemical imbalance.

The psychological model considers learning histories, cognition, emotion regulation, attachment, personality, coping and trauma-related processes. Avoidance may reduce anxiety in the short term while preventing new learning; rumination can prolong low mood; and expectations can shape attention and behaviour. The social model examines family relationships, housing, employment, education, community safety, discrimination, conflict, cultural meanings and service availability. Social conditions influence exposure to stress and determine whether treatment, safety and practical support are accessible.

The biopsychosocial framework integrates these levels and explains how they influence one another over time. Sleep disruption may increase emotional reactivity; worry may then delay sleep; poor concentration may create academic difficulties; and those difficulties may intensify worry. A useful formulation identifies feedback loops and intervention points. It does not merely list three categories. Support may therefore include medical assessment, psychological skills, family or peer support, environmental adjustment and action on material stressors.

4. Diathesis–stress and developmental psychopathology

The diathesis–stress perspective proposes that vulnerability interacts with stress to increase the probability of a difficulty. A diathesis may include temperament, genetic susceptibility, early adversity, a medical condition or a learned response pattern. Stressors may be acute, such as bereavement, or chronic, such as insecurity, caregiving burden or discrimination. Neither vulnerability nor stress alone guarantees a disorder. The model is probabilistic and helps explain why people exposed to similar events can have different outcomes.

Developmental psychopathology studies how risk and protection change across the lifespan. Early attachment, language, peer experiences, puberty, education, work, parenthood and ageing can alter vulnerability and opportunity. Risk factors may accumulate, interact or have different effects at different developmental stages. Childhood symptoms do not inevitably predict adult outcomes: some patterns persist, some remit and others change as capacities and environments change. Assessment should therefore be developmentally informed and avoid assuming that one snapshot explains a person's entire life.

Resilience is not invulnerability or an expectation that people should endure harmful conditions without help. It refers to processes that support adaptation and recovery under adversity. Secure relationships, practical resources, emotional skills, cultural connection, accessible treatment and fair institutions can all be protective. Resilience is relational and structural as well as individual. Prevention should reduce avoidable adversity and strengthen support systems rather than simply telling people to become tougher.

5. Classification systems and diagnosis

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, text revision (DSM-5-TR), is published by the American Psychiatric Association. The International Classification of Diseases, eleventh revision (ICD-11), is maintained by the World Health Organization and includes mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders within a broader international health classification. These systems provide shared terminology for communication, research, service planning and clinical documentation. Their structures and purposes are related but not identical.

Diagnosis requires attention to symptom type, number, duration, severity, distress or impairment, developmental context and exclusions. Clinicians consider whether symptoms are better explained by substances, medication, physical illness, another condition or a culturally expected response. Reliability concerns consistency among assessors; validity concerns whether a category meaningfully captures the phenomenon it claims to describe. Clear criteria can improve reliability without resolving overlap, uncertain boundaries or cultural bias.

Many symptoms occur across diagnostic categories, and a person may meet criteria for more than one condition. Categorical thresholds can support decisions, but distress often varies dimensionally. A diagnosis should communicate a clinically useful pattern, not imply that everyone with the same label has identical experiences or prognosis. Classification is a tool, not a substitute for formulation, longitudinal observation or the person's own goals. Students should consult current manuals and local professional guidance because criteria and service pathways can change.

6. Anxiety, fear and panic

Fear is an adaptive response to immediate threat, whereas anxiety commonly concerns anticipated threat. Both can prepare the body for action. They become clinically significant when persistent, difficult to regulate, disproportionate to context or associated with substantial avoidance and impairment. Anxiety can involve worry and threat predictions, dread, muscle tension, sweating, a racing heart, restlessness and reassurance seeking. Realistic responses to unsafe environments must not be misclassified as pathological.

Specific phobia involves marked fear of a particular object or situation, such as heights, animals or injections. Social anxiety disorder centres on fear of scrutiny, embarrassment or negative evaluation. Generalised anxiety disorder involves persistent, difficult-to-control worry across multiple areas, often accompanied by tension, fatigue, irritability and sleep problems. Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks and ongoing concern or behavioural change related to further attacks. A panic attack is an episode of intense fear or discomfort and can occur in several conditions.

Assessment explores triggers, avoidance, safety behaviours, physical health, substance use, cultural context and the effect on daily life. Avoidance can reduce anxiety immediately, which reinforces the behaviour, but it prevents the person from learning that a situation may be manageable. Evidence-based support may include cognitive behavioural methods, carefully planned exposure, relaxation or attention strategies, and treatment of co-occurring conditions. Interventions should be collaborative, paced and reviewed against the person's goals.

7. Obsessive-compulsive and related processes

Obsessive-compulsive disorder involves obsessions, compulsions or both. Obsessions are intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that cause distress. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to an obsession or rigid rule, often to reduce distress or prevent a feared event. Examples may include repeated checking, washing, ordering or mental reviewing. The content of an obsession does not necessarily represent a person's wishes, values or intentions.

The cycle is maintained because a ritual can provide temporary relief. Relief negatively reinforces the compulsion, making it more likely to recur when uncertainty or distress returns. People may spend substantial time completing rituals, avoid triggers or seek reassurance from others. Assessment examines time consumed, distress, impairment, insight, cultural context and alternative explanations. It should distinguish a clinical pattern from ordinary routines, carefulness or culturally meaningful practices.

Evidence-based care can include cognitive behavioural therapy with exposure and response prevention, in which the person gradually approaches relevant triggers while reducing ritualised responses. The process should be planned with a trained clinician and should never involve humiliating or coercive exposure. Family members may need guidance to reduce inadvertent reinforcement while maintaining compassion. Progress is measured through improved functioning and reduced interference, not by demanding that a person never experience an intrusive thought.

8. Trauma-related conditions and PTSD

Trauma can involve events that threaten safety or overwhelm a person's capacity to cope, including violence, abuse, accidents, disasters, war and displacement. Not everyone exposed to trauma develops a disorder. Responses may include intrusive memories, nightmares, avoidance, changes in beliefs or mood, guilt, emotional numbing, heightened arousal, sleep disturbance and difficulties trusting others. The meaning and impact of an event depend on the person, context, history and available support.

Post-traumatic stress disorder involves a pattern following traumatic exposure that may include re-experiencing, avoidance, persistent negative changes in cognition or mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity, with clinically significant distress or impairment. Complex trauma presentations may also involve difficulties in emotion regulation, self-concept and relationships. Differential assessment considers depression, anxiety, grief, acute stress responses, substance effects, physical illness and traumatic brain injury. Diagnostic requirements vary across classification systems.

Trauma-informed care prioritises safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment and cultural sensitivity. It avoids unnecessary retelling and does not assume every difficulty is caused by trauma. Support may include practical assistance, stabilisation, coping skills and evidence-based trauma-focused therapies. When danger is ongoing, improving safety and access to resources may be as important as symptom-focused work. Clinicians explain options, obtain consent and monitor benefit, distress and unintended effects.

9. Depressive disorders

Depressive disorders involve persistent changes in mood, interest, motivation, thinking, bodily rhythms and functioning. A major depressive episode may include depressed mood or loss of interest and pleasure, together with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, movement, self-worth or thoughts about death. Assessment considers duration, frequency, severity, impairment, bereavement, medical conditions, medication and substances. Depression is not simply sadness, laziness or a failure to appreciate positive events.

Presentations vary. Some people report prominent physical symptoms; others experience irritability, anxiety, cognitive slowing or emotional numbness. Persistent depressive patterns involve long-lasting low mood and associated symptoms. Perinatal depression can occur during pregnancy or after birth and requires timely, compassionate support. Depression can co-occur with anxiety, substance use, chronic illness and social adversity. Assessment identifies protective relationships, strengths and changes from the person's usual functioning as well as symptoms.

Psychological interventions may include cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation and interpersonal approaches. Treatment planning considers severity, preferences, previous response, access and co-occurring conditions. Medication may be considered by qualified prescribers after discussion of benefits, risks and monitoring; educational notes are not prescribing advice. Restoring routines, addressing sleep, reducing isolation, solving practical problems and strengthening support can complement therapy. Recovery may be gradual, and setbacks do not mean that treatment has failed.

10. Bipolar and related disorders

Bipolar disorders involve episodes of mood and energy change that include mania or hypomania and, for many people, depressive episodes. Mania is more than happiness or productivity: it is a distinct period of elevated, expansive or irritable mood and increased energy or activity, with features such as reduced need for sleep, increased speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, heightened goal-directed activity or risky decisions. The episode can seriously disrupt judgement, relationships, finances, work or safety.

Hypomania involves a similar pattern but differs in duration, severity and functional consequences under diagnostic criteria. Accurate assessment requires a longitudinal history, not a judgement from one appointment. Clinicians ask about previous periods of unusual energy, sleep changes, impulsivity, family history, substances, medication and medical conditions. Differential diagnosis includes attention-related conditions, substance effects, sleep disorders, trauma-related arousal and other explanations.

Management is individualised and may combine clinical monitoring, psychoeducation, psychological support, sleep and routine planning, family involvement when appropriate and medication prescribed by a qualified clinician. Early recognition of changes in sleep, energy, activity and judgement can support timely review. Stigma can falsely suggest that a person cannot study, work, parent or maintain relationships. The person's goals, preferences and early-warning plan should remain central.

11. Schizophrenia spectrum and psychosis

Psychosis describes experiences in which reality testing may be significantly altered, including delusions, hallucinations and disorganised thinking or behaviour. A hallucination is a perception-like experience without a corresponding external stimulus. A delusion is a strongly held belief not readily changed by contrary evidence and not explained by the person's cultural or religious context. Disorganised speech can reflect difficulty organising thoughts. Negative symptoms may include reduced emotional expression, motivation or speech; cognitive difficulties may affect attention and executive functioning.

Schizophrenia is one diagnosis within a wider spectrum. Assessment considers symptom pattern, duration, functional change and exclusions. Psychotic experiences can also occur in mood disorders, substance-induced states, neurological or medical conditions, severe sleep deprivation and some trauma-related presentations. A single unusual belief or perceptual experience is not enough to establish schizophrenia. Cultural meanings and spiritual practices must be explored respectfully. People experiencing psychosis are not inherently dangerous and may be especially vulnerable to victimisation and exclusion.

Support may include early intervention, medication prescribed and monitored by qualified clinicians, psychological therapies, family education, supported education or employment and physical healthcare. Side effects and physical health require attention. Recovery-oriented care recognises that symptom reduction is only one dimension: autonomy, relationships, identity, hope and meaningful roles matter. Professionals can acknowledge distress without reinforcing a belief they do not share, and should explain decisions transparently.

12. Personality disorders and dimensional models

Personality refers to relatively enduring patterns of perceiving, relating and responding. Personality disorder frameworks describe persistent patterns associated with significant difficulties in self-functioning, relationships or adaptation. These patterns are not moral defects, and people should not be reduced to a label. Categorical systems identify named disorders, while dimensional approaches describe traits and severity along continua. Dimensional models may better represent variation and overlap, although they can be more complex to communicate.

Assessment considers developmental history, stability across settings, culture, trauma, current stress, substance use, neurodevelopmental factors and the person's own understanding. A behaviour that appears rigid in one context may be adaptive in another, especially where safety is uncertain. Clinicians should avoid diagnosing from brief encounters or online descriptions. Stigma can lead professionals to dismiss distress or assume manipulation; good practice focuses on specific needs, strengths, risks and collaborative goals.

Psychological interventions may address emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness, mentalisation, cognitive patterns and long-standing relational difficulties. Dialectical behaviour therapy teaches mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness skills for particular presentations. Treatment may be sustained and requires a clear therapeutic framework. Crisis planning, continuity and respectful boundaries can support safety without punishment or abandonment. Progress should be evaluated against goals that matter to the person.

13. Neurodevelopmental conditions and neurodiversity

Neurodevelopmental conditions begin during the developmental period and can influence learning, attention, communication, behaviour or adaptive functioning. ADHD involves persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity inconsistent with developmental level and associated with impairment. Assessment gathers developmental history and information from relevant settings, while considering sleep, anxiety, learning differences, trauma, sensory needs and environmental mismatch. ADHD is not equivalent to poor discipline or low intelligence.

Autism spectrum conditions involve differences in social communication and interaction together with restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or sensory experience. Autistic people are diverse: communication styles, abilities and support needs vary widely. A neurodiversity-informed approach recognises neurological variation while taking disability, distress and barriers seriously. The goal is not to make every person appear non-autistic, but to support communication, autonomy, access, safety and quality of life.

Support is individualised and may include accessible teaching, predictable routines, environmental adjustments, coaching, communication supports and family partnership. Medication may be appropriate for some ADHD-related difficulties under professional supervision, but it does not replace educational or environmental support. Clinicians seek the person's preferences and developmentally appropriate consent or assent. Inclusive practice avoids deficit-only descriptions and recognises that difficulties can arise from the interaction between a person's needs and an inaccessible environment.

14. Eating, somatic symptom and dissociative disorders

Eating disorders involve persistent disturbances in eating-related behaviour, body image or weight-related concerns that affect physical health or psychological functioning. Anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder are distinct presentations, and not every person fits a stereotyped appearance. Medical risk can occur at different body sizes. Assessment requires sensitivity, attention to physical complications and coordination with qualified healthcare professionals. Shame and secrecy can delay help, so non-judgemental communication is essential.

Somatic symptom and related disorders involve distressing physical symptoms and/or excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours related to health concerns. Symptoms should never be dismissed as imaginary. Physical conditions and psychological processes can coexist. Assessment should explain uncertainty honestly, avoid unnecessary repeated investigations and ensure new or changing symptoms receive appropriate medical review. A consistent care relationship can reduce fragmentation and support coping, function and quality of life.

Dissociative disorders involve disruptions in the integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception or sense of self. Experiences range from transient detachment to persistent impairment. Trauma may be relevant, but assessment also considers neurological, sleep-related, substance-related and other explanations. Clinicians should not lead or pressure people to produce memories. Care emphasises safety, grounding, stabilisation, consent and careful coordination, avoiding sensationalised accounts.

15. Substance use and behavioural addictions

Substance use disorders involve patterns of alcohol or drug use associated with impaired control, risky use, social or occupational consequences and sometimes tolerance or withdrawal. The substance, amount, frequency, route, context and co-occurring health conditions matter. A continuum perspective recognises a range from occasional use to severe dependence. Screening should be confidential and non-stigmatising. Moral condemnation and punitive responses can discourage help-seeking and worsen social harms.

Assessment considers the function of use, triggers, craving, withdrawal risk, previous attempts to change, mental and physical health, family context and the person's readiness and goals. Some withdrawal states can be medically dangerous, so professional assessment is important rather than unsupervised detoxification. Treatment may include motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioural approaches, relapse-prevention planning, peer support, family work and medication when clinically indicated. Harm reduction seeks to reduce injury, disease and social harm even when immediate abstinence is not the chosen goal.

High engagement in gaming, shopping, gambling or online activity is not automatically a behavioural addiction. Assessment asks whether there is impaired control, persistence despite significant harm and disruption to important areas of life. Cultural, economic and technological contexts matter. Interventions focus on function, triggers, alternative sources of reward, safeguards and co-occurring difficulties. Clinicians should avoid treating ordinary enthusiasm or culturally valued activity as pathology.

16. Psychological assessment

Psychological assessment is a structured process for understanding concerns, strengths, functioning and support needs. It may involve interviews, observation, validated questionnaires, behavioural measures, collateral information with consent, record review and consultation. Assessment begins by clarifying the referral question and the person's own goals. A measure is useful only when appropriate for the population, language, purpose and decision. Scores must be interpreted in context rather than treated as objective truths.

Interviews explore onset, duration, frequency, intensity, triggers, consequences and previous coping. A comprehensive history may include development, physical health, medication, substance use, relationships, education, work, culture, spirituality, trauma where relevant and previous support. The assessor explains confidentiality and its limits, obtains informed consent and adapts communication. Risk assessment is an ongoing professional responsibility, not a one-time tick-box exercise.

Assessment quality depends on rapport, cultural and linguistic validity, appropriate tools and transparent interpretation. A questionnaire developed in one population may not have the same meaning elsewhere. Translating words literally does not guarantee conceptual equivalence. Clinicians should state uncertainty, seek additional information when necessary and refer when needs exceed competence. Feedback should be understandable, respectful and useful for decisions.

17. Case formulation and differential diagnosis

Case formulation organises information into a working explanation of how difficulties developed and what maintains them. A practical structure is the five Ps: presenting problems, predisposing factors, precipitating factors, perpetuating factors and protective factors. Diagnosis names a recognised pattern; formulation proposes an individual explanation and guides intervention. They are related but not interchangeable. A formulation should be collaborative, tentative and revisable rather than a speculative story that blames the person or family.

Differential diagnosis compares possible explanations for a presentation. For example, poor concentration may be associated with depression, anxiety, ADHD, sleep deprivation, medication, substance use, physical illness or overwhelming circumstances. The clinician considers timing, developmental history, associated symptoms, context and evidence for or against each possibility. More than one condition may coexist. It is often safer to document uncertainty than to force a premature conclusion.

A useful formulation generates testable predictions and identifies points where change may be possible. If avoidance is maintaining fear, carefully supported approach behaviour may be a treatment target. If sleep disruption worsens mood, sleep and routine may need attention. If social isolation follows discrimination or unsafe housing, individual therapy alone may be insufficient. Formulation is reviewed as outcomes and new information emerge.

18. Evidence-based psychological interventions

Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research, clinical expertise and the person's values, culture and circumstances. CBT examines relationships among thoughts, feelings, bodily responses and behaviour through strategies such as testing predictions, behavioural experiments and coping skills. Behavioural activation helps people reconnect with meaningful activity and sources of reinforcement, particularly when low mood has led to withdrawal. Interpersonal therapy focuses on grief, role transitions and relationship difficulties.

Exposure approaches help people approach feared situations, memories or sensations in a planned and supported way when clinically appropriate. The purpose is not to overwhelm or force someone, but to support new learning and reduce avoidance. Dialectical behaviour therapy combines acceptance and change strategies with skills in mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Family and systemic therapies examine interaction patterns and may be useful when difficulties affect or are affected by relationships.

Psychodynamic therapies explore recurring relational patterns and emotional conflict; humanistic approaches emphasise empathy, authenticity and growth. No approach is universally best. Treatment selection considers formulation, severity, evidence, preference, accessibility, therapist competence and co-occurring needs. Outcomes should be monitored and plans adjusted when progress is limited or adverse effects occur. Cultural adaptation should preserve the active therapeutic principles while making examples, language and delivery meaningful.

19. Medication and multidisciplinary care

Psychotropic medicines can help some people and conditions, often as one part of a broader care plan. Broad groups include antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers, anxiolytic medicines and medicines used for attention-related conditions. Each group contains agents with different indications, benefits, risks and monitoring needs. Medication decisions belong to qualified prescribers who review medical history, interactions, physical health, previous response and personal preferences. A diagnosis alone does not determine a specific medicine.

Good medication care involves shared decision-making and clear information about expected benefits, possible adverse effects, time to response, monitoring and what to do if concerns arise. People should not start, stop or change prescribed medication solely on the basis of educational notes. Abrupt discontinuation can cause problems with some medicines. Medication may reduce symptoms enough to make psychological or social interventions more accessible, but it does not automatically resolve trauma, poverty, discrimination or relationship conflict.

Multidisciplinary care may involve psychologists, psychiatrists, nurses, social workers, occupational therapists, general practitioners, educators and peer workers. Coordination matters when mental and physical health conditions coexist. Sleep, nutrition, movement, pain, chronic illness and substance use influence psychological functioning. A shared plan identifies responsibilities, communication arrangements, review dates and the person's preferred supports. Physical health monitoring should be part of respectful, person-centred mental healthcare.

20. Suicide risk, self-harm and crisis response

Suicidal thoughts and self-harm require compassionate, direct and timely attention. Asking clearly about suicidal thoughts does not create them; it can provide an opportunity to disclose distress and receive support. Assessment explores current thoughts, intent, plans, access to means, previous behaviour, recent changes, substance use, agitation, hopelessness, reasons for living and supportive relationships. No checklist predicts an individual's actions with certainty. Assessment must lead to a collaborative safety response, not merely a numerical category.

When there is immediate danger, severe distress or inability to remain safe, urgent local emergency or clinical support is needed. A person should not be left alone when immediate risk is high and safe supervision is possible. A safety plan may identify warning signs, internal coping strategies, safe places, people to contact, professional services and steps to reduce access to lethal means. It should be practical, specific and accessible during distress.

Self-harm may serve different functions, including regulating overwhelming emotion or expressing distress. It should not be dismissed as attention-seeking. Clinicians explore function without judgement and offer alternatives, treatment and follow-up. Educational materials should avoid graphic details and simplistic explanations. Prevention includes reducing stigma, strengthening connection, improving access to timely care and addressing structural stressors. Recovery is possible, and respectful language can make help-seeking easier.

21. Culture and global mental health

Mental health is experienced and expressed within cultural, linguistic, spiritual and social contexts. Communities may use local idioms to describe distress, and people may seek help from family, faith leaders, traditional practitioners, health workers or mental health professionals. These sources may coexist. A culturally responsive clinician asks what an experience means to the person, how their community understands it, what help they have tried and what outcomes they hope for. Cultural humility means remaining curious and correcting assumptions.

In Kenya and other settings, access to care can be shaped by the distribution of trained professionals, cost, distance, stigma, competing health priorities, language, confidentiality concerns and specialist-service availability. Schools, primary healthcare facilities, universities, workplaces and community organisations can contribute to early identification and referral when they have appropriate training and safeguards. Digital services may widen access for some while excluding others through connectivity, privacy, disability or affordability barriers.

Global mental health seeks improved outcomes and equity across diverse settings. It must avoid exporting interventions without adaptation or treating one diagnostic tradition as the only valid way to understand distress. Programmes should be co-designed with local communities and evaluated in context. Policy priorities include integration into primary care, workforce development, human-rights protection, community-based services and ethical data. Public education should encourage help-seeking without implying every difficult emotion is an illness.

22. Ethics and human rights

Ethical mental health practice is grounded in dignity, autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, competence and integrity. Informed consent requires understandable information about purpose, likely benefits, risks, alternatives, confidentiality and the right to ask questions. Consent should be ongoing. Where decision-making capacity is in question, professionals assess the specific decision and support participation to the greatest extent possible, following applicable law and professional standards.

Confidentiality supports trust but has lawful and ethical limits, including certain safeguarding or serious safety situations. Limits should be explained before sensitive information is collected whenever possible. Records should be accurate, relevant, secure and shared only with appropriate authority. Professionals must recognise boundaries, avoid exploitation and seek supervision when cases exceed competence. Online communication and dual relationships create risks requiring clear policies.

Human-rights-based care rejects unnecessary coercion, degrading treatment and discrimination. Restrictive interventions, where legally permitted, require strict safeguards, necessity, proportionality, review and attention to less restrictive alternatives. People with lived experience should participate in decisions about care and service design. Accessibility includes reasonable adjustments, interpretation, disability inclusion and cultural respect. Ethical practice protects dignity while providing effective and accountable care.

23. Prevention, recovery and community services

Universal prevention promotes wellbeing across a population, for example through safe schools and anti-bullying programmes. Selective prevention targets groups with elevated exposure to risk, while indicated prevention supports individuals showing early signs of difficulty. Early intervention can reduce the duration and impact of untreated problems. Awareness campaigns that increase identification without improving access to support may leave people frustrated; prevention must be evaluated for benefit and unintended effects.

Recovery is a personal process of building a meaningful life, with or without ongoing symptoms. It may involve hope, identity, agency, relationships, practical stability and participation in education, work or community life. Clinical recovery and personal recovery overlap but are not identical. Peer support can reduce isolation and offer knowledge grounded in lived experience. Family involvement can help when chosen by the person and managed with consent and appropriate boundaries.

Community mental health services aim to provide accessible, coordinated and least-restrictive support close to where people live. Effective systems connect primary care, specialist services, social protection, housing, education, employment and crisis response. Continuity matters during transitions and after hospital discharge. Evaluation should include quality of life, autonomy, safety and social participation as well as symptom measures. Mental health is part of public health and social justice.

24. Research methods and evidence appraisal

Research uses descriptive, correlational, experimental, longitudinal, qualitative and mixed-method designs. Case studies provide detail but may not generalise. Cross-sectional studies estimate patterns at one time but cannot establish temporal order. Longitudinal research follows change but faces attrition and confounding. Randomised trials can test intervention effects under specified conditions, but participants and settings may not represent all communities. Qualitative research explores lived experience, meaning and service barriers that standardised measures may miss.

Evidence appraisal considers sampling, measurement quality, comparison groups, randomisation, blinding where feasible, attrition, effect sizes, confidence intervals, preregistration, publication bias and replication. Statistical significance does not necessarily indicate a clinically important benefit. Average effects do not guarantee every individual will respond. Reviews can synthesise evidence, but conclusions depend on the quality and comparability of included studies. Researchers should report adverse events and limitations, not only favourable outcomes.

Research ethics require voluntary informed participation, privacy, fair recruitment, minimisation of harm and appropriate review. People experiencing acute distress may need additional safeguards and accessible consent processes. Community partnership improves relevance. Culturally valid measures require careful adaptation, attention to translation and conceptual equivalence. Research should improve understanding, prevention, care and equity rather than merely classify people.

25. Integrated case reasoning

Clinical reasoning moves between observation, explanation and action. A symptom list may suggest a diagnostic pattern, but it does not explain why this person is struggling now or what support will be acceptable and useful. A formulation links current difficulties with development, learning, physical health, relationships, culture and structural conditions. It identifies maintaining cycles and protective resources, then generates a plan that can be evaluated. Good reasoning remains open to revision when new information emerges.

Consider a university student reporting persistent worry, poor sleep, reduced concentration and avoidance of presentations. Assessment asks when symptoms began, what the student fears, whether worry spans several domains, how sleep and health have changed, and whether discrimination, bullying or unsafe conditions are relevant. It explores strengths, preferred supports and academic pressures. A diagnosis should not be assumed from a short symptom list; the clinician considers duration, impairment and alternative explanations.

A shared plan might combine an appropriate psychological approach, gradual skills practice, sleep support, academic adjustments and review. If symptoms suggest a different condition or physical cause, further assessment or referral may be needed. The student participates in decisions and evaluates whether the plan helps. Diagnosis, formulation and intervention are connected but distinct tasks. Person-centred care uses evidence while respecting context, agency and goals.

Chapter summary and key terminology

Abnormal psychology studies distress, impairment and risk without reducing people to labels. Context, culture, duration, functioning and personal meaning matter. Biological, psychological and social processes interact across development. DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 support communication but have limitations. Assessment integrates interviews, measures, observation, history, risk review and strengths. Formulation explains an individual's difficulties and guides collaborative intervention. Evidence-based care combines research, professional expertise, culture and personal preferences. Prevention, recovery, human rights and community access are central.

Key terms: abnormal psychology; biopsychosocial model; diathesis; stressor; comorbidity; differential diagnosis; reliability; validity; case formulation; protective factor; harm reduction; recovery-oriented care; neurodiversity; trauma-informed practice; informed consent.

Structured university examination questions with model answers

Attempt each question before reading the model answer. Marks may be adapted by the lecturer.

1. Define abnormal psychology and discuss four dimensions used to evaluate abnormality.

Model answer: It studies patterns associated with distress, impairment or risk. Dimensions include statistical rarity, norm violation, subjective distress and functional impairment. No one dimension is sufficient: rarity can be harmless, norms vary culturally, distress may occur without disorder and impairment requires contextual interpretation. Duration, severity and safety also matter.

2. Explain why single-factor explanations of mental disorders are inadequate.

Model answer: They overlook interaction. Biological vulnerabilities shape stress sensitivity; learning and cognition shape responses; relationships and social conditions influence exposure and recovery. The biopsychosocial framework integrates these processes and identifies intervention points across health, skills, relationships and material conditions.

3. Compare biological, psychological and social models.

Model answer: The biological model studies genes, brain systems, hormones and physical health. The psychological model examines learning, cognition, emotion, attachment and coping. The social model examines relationships, culture, inequality, safety and resources. Each explains different mechanisms; integrated reasoning examines their interaction.

4. Explain the diathesis–stress model with an example.

Model answer: It proposes that vulnerability interacts with stress to increase probability of difficulty. Vulnerability may be temperamental or biological and stress may be acute or chronic. A person with heightened threat sensitivity may develop severe anxiety after repeated unsafe experiences. Protective factors alter outcomes.

5. Distinguish reliability from validity in diagnosis.

Model answer: Reliability is consistency across assessors, occasions or methods. Validity concerns whether a category captures the phenomenon it claims to represent. Clear criteria can improve reliability without resolving overlap, cultural bias or uncertain boundaries.

6. Evaluate benefits and limitations of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11.

Model answer: They provide shared language, structure research and support service planning. Limitations include symptom overlap, categorical thresholds, cultural variation and stigma. They should support communication and not replace individual formulation or clinical judgement.

7. Differentiate fear, anxiety, panic attack and panic disorder.

Model answer: Fear responds to immediate threat; anxiety concerns anticipated threat. A panic attack is an episode of intense fear or discomfort with physical and cognitive symptoms. Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected attacks plus persistent concern or behavioural change.

8. Explain the maintenance cycle in obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Model answer: An intrusive thought creates distress. A ritual or mental act temporarily reduces distress, negatively reinforcing the compulsion. Relief is short-lived, so uncertainty returns and the cycle repeats. Treatment can help reduce reliance on rituals.

9. Describe the major symptom domains of PTSD.

Model answer: They include re-experiencing, avoidance, persistent negative changes in cognition or mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity. Assessment considers exposure, duration, impairment, safety, culture and alternative explanations. Not everyone exposed to trauma develops PTSD.

10. Explain how depression affects cognition and functioning.

Model answer: It can impair concentration, memory, decision-making, motivation and expectations. Low energy and loss of pleasure make routine tasks difficult; sleep and appetite changes affect physical functioning. Withdrawal can reduce social support and positive reinforcement.

11. Distinguish mania from ordinary elevated mood.

Model answer: Mania involves a distinct change in mood and energy with symptoms such as reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, increased activity and risky decisions. It causes marked impairment or requires urgent care; happiness alone is not mania.

12. Identify positive, negative and cognitive symptoms of psychosis.

Model answer: Positive symptoms include hallucinations, delusions and disorganised speech or behaviour. Negative symptoms include reduced emotional expression, motivation or speech. Cognitive difficulties affect attention, memory and executive functioning.

13. Discuss stigma associated with psychotic disorders.

Model answer: Stereotypes may portray people as dangerous or incapable. Such assumptions are inaccurate and can cause discrimination, isolation and delayed help-seeking. Education, respectful language, supported participation and recovery-oriented services challenge stigma.

14. Compare categorical and dimensional approaches to personality disorder.

Model answer: Categorical approaches use named diagnostic groups and criteria. Dimensional approaches describe severity and traits along continua. Categories aid communication, while dimensions may represent overlap and variation. Both require developmental and cultural context.

15. Outline assessment considerations for ADHD in a university student.

Model answer: Establish developmental onset, persistent symptoms and impairment across settings. Gather history and consider sleep, anxiety, depression, learning differences, trauma, substances and environmental factors. Include strengths and the student's support preferences.

16. Explain a neurodiversity-informed approach to autism.

Model answer: It recognises neurological variation and diversity while taking disability and support needs seriously. It does not aim to make a person appear non-autistic; it supports communication, sensory access, autonomy, safety and quality of life.

17. Why should eating disorders not be identified by appearance alone?

Model answer: They occur across body sizes and appearances, and medical risk may not be visible. Stereotypes can delay assessment. Evaluation considers behaviour, distress, physical indicators and functioning with appropriate healthcare coordination.

18. Explain harm reduction in substance-use care.

Model answer: Harm reduction reduces health, social and safety consequences even when abstinence is not immediately selected or achievable. It uses non-judgemental engagement, practical risk reduction and respect for autonomy.

19. Describe the five Ps of case formulation.

Model answer: Presenting factors describe current difficulties; predisposing factors are background vulnerabilities; precipitating factors are onset-related events; perpetuating factors maintain the problem; protective factors support coping and recovery. Together they guide collaborative intervention.

20. Distinguish diagnosis from case formulation.

Model answer: Diagnosis identifies a recognised pattern using classification criteria. Formulation develops an individual explanation of development and maintenance, integrating history, context, strengths and mechanisms. Diagnosis aids communication; formulation guides personalised care.

21. Compare CBT and behavioural activation.

Model answer: CBT addresses relationships among thoughts, feelings and behaviour through collaborative strategies and experiments. Behavioural activation reconnects people with meaningful activity and reinforcement, especially where withdrawal maintains low mood.

22. Explain principles of trauma-informed care.

Model answer: Safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment and cultural sensitivity are central. Practice avoids unnecessary coercion or forced disclosure, recognises possible trauma effects and supports agency without assuming every problem is trauma-based.

23. Discuss ethical issues in psychological assessment.

Model answer: Issues include informed consent, confidentiality and its limits, competent test use, cultural validity, privacy, secure records, risk management and fairness. Assessors explain purpose, avoid unsupported conclusions and refer when needed.

24. Explain how culture influences mental-health assessment.

Model answer: Culture shapes idioms of distress, explanations, help-seeking, communication and expectations. Clinicians explore personal meanings, language, community supports and context. Cultural humility reduces misinterpretation and pathologising difference.

25. Compare universal, selective and indicated prevention.

Model answer: Universal prevention targets whole populations; selective prevention supports groups with elevated risk; indicated prevention targets individuals showing early signs. Each should be evidence-informed, accessible and evaluated.

26. Explain the role of peer support in recovery.

Model answer: Peer support uses lived experience to offer connection, practical knowledge and hope. It can reduce isolation and support self-advocacy. It should be voluntary and complement, not replace, clinical care.

27. Why is physical health important in mental healthcare?

Model answer: Illness, sleep, pain, nutrition, medication effects and substance use influence psychological symptoms. Mental-health conditions and treatments can also affect physical health. Integrated care improves assessment, monitoring and quality of life.

28. Outline a safe response to disclosure of current suicidal thoughts.

Model answer: Listen calmly and directly, assess immediacy and relevant risk, and collaboratively identify safety steps. If danger is imminent, arrange urgent local emergency or clinical support, involve trusted supports appropriately and follow safeguarding duties.

29. Identify challenges in evaluating psychological-treatment research.

Model answer: Challenges include non-representative samples, attrition, expectancy effects, short follow-up, publication bias and outcomes that may not reflect personal recovery. Appraisal considers effect sizes, adverse events, replication and cultural applicability.

30. Integrate diagnosis, formulation and intervention in a brief example.

Model answer: A student with social fear needs assessment of duration, impairment and alternatives before diagnosis. Formulation may identify fear of evaluation and avoidance as maintaining factors. A collaborative plan can use evidence-based therapy, graded practice and academic support, with review.

Further reading and responsible use

Consult current editions of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, peer-reviewed systematic reviews, national mental-health policies, professional practice standards and textbooks recommended by the lecturer. Check publication date, population, methodology and limitations. Diagnostic criteria and service pathways may change; verify current local guidance before applying information in practice. This chapter is an academic resource, not a diagnostic instrument, emergency service or substitute for qualified professional consultation. If someone is in immediate danger or unable to remain safe, seek urgent local emergency or clinical support.
