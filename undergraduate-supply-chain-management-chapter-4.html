Operations and Process Management

Designing, controlling and improving the processes that transform resources into reliable products and services.

Course guide

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, learners should be able to explain the transformation model and operations strategy; classify processes; map workflows; analyse capacity and bottlenecks; plan layouts, labour and schedules; apply lean and Six Sigma concepts; manage quality and maintenance; forecast demand; analyse queues; and design continuous improvement and operational control systems.

Study approach: Use real process examples. State the process boundary, customer requirement, assumptions, measurement method and constraints. When calculating performance, show the formula, units and interpretation. Consider quality, safety, employee wellbeing and sustainability alongside productivity.

Important: Operational targets and control limits are not interchangeable. Always use valid data, appropriate measurement definitions and safe working methods before drawing conclusions.

1. Operations management and organisational performance

Operations management is the design, direction and improvement of the processes that transform inputs into goods and services. Inputs may include materials, labour, capital, energy, information, technology and customer participation. Outputs include physical products, completed services, information and experiences. Operations management coordinates these resources so that customer requirements are met reliably and economically.

Operations is not confined to factories. Hospitals manage patient pathways, universities manage teaching and assessment processes, banks manage transactions, logistics firms manage movement and storage, and digital businesses manage platform availability and service requests. In every setting, the operations function converts resources into outcomes through linked activities, decisions and controls.

Operational performance is commonly assessed through quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost. Quality concerns conformance and fitness for purpose; speed concerns elapsed time; dependability concerns keeping promises; flexibility concerns the ability to change volume, mix or delivery; and cost concerns resource efficiency. These objectives interact, so improvement requires understanding trade-offs rather than maximising one measure in isolation.

Operations strategy translates business priorities into choices about process design, capacity, technology, location, skills, sourcing, quality and improvement. A low-cost strategy may emphasise standardisation, scale and waste reduction. A premium service may prioritise expertise, customisation and dependable response. A resilient strategy may maintain alternative capacity and carefully selected buffers.

Operational performance should be evaluated from the customer's perspective as well as through internal efficiency. A process can appear productive while creating queues, errors or rework for customers. Managers therefore combine operational data with complaints, service feedback, returns, delivery performance and outcome measures to understand whether the process creates genuine value.

2. The transformation model and process boundaries

The transformation model describes how resources enter a process, undergo activities and emerge as outputs. Transforming resources are the facilities, equipment, people and systems that act on the transformed resources. Transformed resources may be materials, information or customers. For example, a repair workshop transforms a customer's equipment using technicians, tools, spare parts and diagnostic information.

A process boundary defines where analysis begins and ends. A narrow boundary may examine order picking inside a warehouse; a wider boundary may include order receipt, inventory allocation, picking, packing, dispatch and customer delivery. Boundary choice affects which costs, delays, responsibilities and outcomes are visible. Managers should include upstream and downstream effects when they influence performance.

Processes can be mapped as sequences of activities, decisions, handoffs and feedback loops. A high-level map communicates the overall flow, while a detailed map records tasks, roles, systems, inputs, outputs and exceptions. The map should describe the process as it actually operates, not merely reproduce an idealised procedure manual.

Process owners are accountable for end-to-end performance and improvement. Activity owners manage specific tasks, while functional managers provide people and specialist resources. Clear accountability reduces gaps at handoffs. It is particularly important where a process crosses procurement, production, warehousing, transport, finance and customer service.

Process analysis should identify value-adding work, necessary support work, avoidable delay, duplication, errors and failure demand. Failure demand is additional work created because the process did not meet a need correctly the first time. Examples include repeat calls, returns, rescheduling and correction of inaccurate orders.

3. Process types, volume, variety and customer contact

Operations processes differ according to volume, variety, demand variation and visibility or customer contact. High-volume, low-variety processes often use repeatable workflows, specialised equipment and standard work. Low-volume, high-variety processes require broader skills, flexible resources and more complex scheduling.

Project processes deliver unique outputs over a defined period, such as constructing a bridge or implementing an enterprise system. Jobbing processes handle small quantities of customised work. Batch processes produce groups of similar items. Mass processes produce high volumes of standardised outputs, while continuous processes operate with uninterrupted or highly repetitive flows.

Service processes vary in customer contact and customisation. A self-service digital transaction has different operational characteristics from a face-to-face clinical consultation. High customer contact increases the importance of communication, waiting-time management, service recovery and the customer's role in the process.

Process choice should fit the required product or service, demand pattern, technology, workforce capability and cost structure. A highly automated process may be economical at stable high volume but inflexible when demand or product design changes. A flexible process may handle variety well but require more skilled labour and coordination.

Managers should avoid assuming that one process type is universally superior. The appropriate design balances cost, speed, quality, flexibility, safety and customer experience. Hybrid processes are common: a standardised back-office flow may support a customised customer-facing service.

4. Process mapping, flowcharts and service blueprints

Process mapping makes work visible so that teams can understand sequence, responsibility, decisions, information and physical movement. A flowchart uses standard symbols to represent activities, decisions, start and end points, documents and connectors. Swimlane maps assign activities to roles or departments and reveal handoffs and unclear ownership.

Value-stream mapping examines the flow of materials and information from request to delivery. It records processing time, waiting time, inventory, information signals and constraints. The distinction between processing and elapsed lead time is important: a product may receive only a small amount of active work while spending most of its time waiting between stages.

Service blueprinting extends process mapping by distinguishing customer actions, visible employee actions, backstage actions and support processes. It can show physical or digital evidence encountered by the customer, such as forms, confirmations, status messages and service environments. The blueprint helps identify where an internal failure becomes visible to the customer.

Maps should be validated through observation, interviews, system records and frontline feedback. Workshops alone may miss informal workarounds and exceptions. Managers should document process variants, decision rules and failure points without making the map so detailed that it becomes unreadable.

After mapping, teams can annotate delays, rework, bottlenecks, unnecessary movement, duplicate data entry and control weaknesses. The map becomes a baseline for redesign and a communication tool for training. Updated maps should be controlled so that staff do not rely on obsolete instructions.

5. Capacity planning and resource utilisation

Capacity is the maximum sustainable output a process or resource can deliver under defined conditions and over a specified period. Design capacity is the theoretical maximum, effective capacity reflects realistic operating constraints, and actual output is what is achieved. These measures should use consistent units and time periods.

Capacity utilisation is often calculated as actual output divided by design capacity, multiplied by 100. Efficiency may be calculated as actual output divided by effective capacity, multiplied by 100. These measures answer different questions. High utilisation is not automatically desirable if it causes queues, breakdown risk, overtime or loss of responsiveness.

Capacity planning can be long-term, medium-term or short-term. Long-term choices include facility location, major equipment and network design. Medium-term choices include staffing levels, shifts, subcontracting and planned maintenance. Short-term decisions include overtime, sequencing, temporary labour and allocation of available resources.

Capacity strategies include leading demand by adding capacity in advance, lagging demand by adding capacity after demand is proven, or matching demand through incremental adjustments. Each approach has financial and service implications. Leading can protect service but creates idle-capacity risk; lagging protects utilisation but may lose customers or create backlogs.

Capacity analysis should account for variability, product mix, downtime, learning, maintenance, staff absence and yield. A nominal machine rate may not reflect usable output. Scenario analysis and sensitivity testing help managers evaluate uncertainty and identify the least-cost way to meet an agreed service level.

6. Bottleneck analysis and the theory of constraints

A bottleneck is a resource or stage whose capacity constrains the throughput of the whole process. Increasing capacity at a non-bottleneck may simply create more work-in-progress without increasing final output. Bottleneck analysis therefore focuses improvement effort on the constraint that governs system flow.

The theory of constraints encourages managers to identify the system constraint, exploit it using existing resources, subordinate other activities to its pace, elevate its capacity if necessary and then repeat the analysis when the constraint moves. Exploiting may include reducing setup time, preventing avoidable downtime and ensuring the constraint receives good-quality inputs.

Throughput is the rate at which the system generates completed output or value over time. Managers should distinguish throughput from local activity or utilisation. A department producing many components that are not needed by the bottleneck may increase inventory rather than customer delivery.

Buffer management protects the constraint from starvation and helps identify emerging problems. A time or inventory buffer may be placed before the constraint, while a shipping buffer protects delivery commitments. Buffers should be sized and monitored deliberately; uncontrolled accumulation hides process problems and ties up working capital.

Constraint analysis requires reliable data on cycle times, downtime, queues, setup, yield and demand. It should also consider policy constraints such as approval rules, batch sizes or scheduling practices. The apparent physical bottleneck may be created by management decisions rather than equipment limitations.

7. Process design, layout and material flow

Process layout determines how people, equipment, workstations, storage and information are arranged. A functional or process layout groups similar resources and supports variety, but may require complex routing. A product or line layout arranges resources in the sequence of work and supports high-volume flow, but can be less flexible.

Cellular layouts group different resources to process families of similar products. They can reduce movement, waiting and work-in-progress while supporting team ownership. Fixed-position layouts keep the product in one place and move people and equipment to it, as in shipbuilding or large construction projects.

Layout decisions affect travel distance, congestion, safety, supervision, communication, accessibility, inventory and future expansion. Material-flow analysis can identify backtracking and crossing routes. In warehouses, slotting fast-moving items near dispatch may reduce travel, but replenishment, safety and product compatibility must also be considered.

Process design should incorporate ergonomics and human factors. Workstations should reduce unnecessary reaching, lifting, awkward posture and repetitive strain. Visual management, clear walkways, appropriate lighting, safe machine guarding and accessible emergency routes are essential design considerations.

Layout changes should be evaluated through measured scenarios and stakeholder consultation. A layout that improves average travel distance may create congestion at peak demand. Simulation, pilot testing and phased implementation can reduce the risk of costly disruption.

8. Work measurement, productivity and labour planning

Productivity relates outputs to inputs. Labour productivity may be expressed as units produced per labour hour, while multifactor productivity compares output with a combination of labour, capital, materials and energy. Productivity measures require consistent definitions of output quality and input boundaries.

Work measurement establishes the time required for a defined task under specified conditions. Techniques include direct time study, work sampling, historical data and predetermined motion-time systems. Standards should account for method, skill, fatigue, unavoidable delays and safe working practices rather than assuming uninterrupted maximum effort.

Labour planning matches skills and staffing to expected workload. It considers shift patterns, legal working-time requirements, training, absence, seasonality, overtime, fatigue and employee wellbeing. A staffing model that meets numerical demand but ignores competence or fatigue can increase defects and safety incidents.

Productivity improvement should focus on removing obstacles, improving tools, balancing workloads, training, standardising good methods and reducing rework. Targets should not encourage unsafe speed, concealment of defects or manipulation of records. Employee participation helps reveal practical barriers that management data may not show.

Productivity comparisons across sites or periods should adjust for product mix, complexity, quality, equipment age and working conditions. A simple output-per-hour figure can be misleading if one team handles more difficult work or produces higher-quality outcomes.

9. Scheduling, sequencing and workflow control

Scheduling allocates work to resources and time periods. It translates demand, capacity, priorities, due dates and constraints into an executable plan. Scheduling may occur at strategic, tactical and operational levels, and should connect production or service plans with materials, labour and delivery commitments.

Common sequencing rules include first-in-first-out, shortest processing time, earliest due date and critical ratio. Each rule has strengths and weaknesses. Shortest processing time may reduce average flow time but delay long jobs; earliest due date can protect urgent commitments but may not minimise total work-in-progress.

Forward scheduling starts work as soon as resources are available and estimates completion. Backward scheduling starts from the required completion date and calculates when activities must begin. Both methods need realistic lead times and allowance for setup, transport, approval and uncertainty.

Workflow control compares actual progress with the plan and responds to deviations. Visual boards, digital work queues, dispatch lists and exception alerts can help. Managers should avoid releasing more work than the system can process, because excessive work-in-progress lengthens lead times and obscures priorities.

Scheduling decisions should consider customer importance, contractual commitments, perishability, safety, changeover costs and downstream dependencies. Priority rules should be transparent and consistently applied, with an authorised process for genuine exceptions.

10. Lean operations and waste elimination

Lean operations focus on delivering customer value with less waste and smoother flow. Commonly identified wastes include overproduction, waiting, unnecessary transport, over-processing, excess inventory, unnecessary motion, defects and underused human capability. The aim is not simply to cut resources but to remove activities that consume effort without creating value.

Value should be defined from the customer's perspective and within the relevant process. An activity may be necessary for legal compliance or safety even if the customer does not directly see it. Lean analysis distinguishes value-adding work, necessary non-value-adding work and avoidable waste.

Tools include 5S workplace organisation, standard work, visual management, pull systems, quick changeover, mistake-proofing, flow cells and continuous improvement events. Tools should be selected to address a diagnosed problem. Applying a technique without understanding the process may create superficial activity rather than sustained improvement.

Lean systems often use pull signals to replenish items in response to actual consumption. Kanban cards or electronic signals limit work-in-progress and communicate replenishment needs. Pull systems require dependable processes, suitable batch sizes and clear replenishment rules; they are not a guarantee that inventory can be eliminated.

Lean implementation depends on respect for people, leadership behaviour and problem-solving capability. Employees should be encouraged to identify problems and test improvements. If lean is experienced only as labour reduction or intensified work, trust and long-term performance may deteriorate.

11. Six Sigma, variation and statistical process control

Six Sigma is a data-oriented approach to reducing process defects and variation. A common improvement framework is DMAIC: define the problem, measure current performance, analyse causes, improve the process and control the gains. The method requires a clear customer requirement and reliable measurement.

Variation is the difference between individual process outcomes. Common-cause variation arises from the system as it normally operates; special-cause variation results from identifiable unusual events. Confusing the two can lead managers to make unnecessary adjustments or ignore a genuine disruption.

Statistical process control uses control charts to distinguish routine process variation from signals that may indicate a change. Control limits are calculated from process data and are not the same as customer specification limits. A process can be statistically stable but incapable of meeting specifications, or unstable despite an acceptable recent average.

Process capability indices compare process spread and centring with specification limits under appropriate assumptions. Cp reflects potential capability based on spread; Cpk also considers how centred the process is. These indices should not be used blindly when data are non-normal, unstable or collected from mixed processes.

Improvement projects should quantify defect definitions, sampling methods, measurement-system reliability, baseline performance and financial or service impact. Statistical analysis supports judgement but does not replace process knowledge, ethical data collection or customer requirements.

12. Quality management and cost of quality

Quality management ensures that products and services consistently meet defined requirements and are fit for intended use. Quality is designed into processes through requirements, capable methods, trained people, suitable materials, equipment maintenance and feedback. Inspection alone cannot create quality if the underlying process is unreliable.

Quality costs are often grouped into prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure. Prevention includes training and process design; appraisal includes inspection and testing; internal failure includes scrap and rework before delivery; external failure includes returns, warranty claims, complaints and reputation damage after delivery.

Total quality management emphasises customer focus, leadership, employee involvement, process thinking, evidence-based decisions and continual improvement. It treats quality as an organisational responsibility rather than the task of a separate inspection department.

Corrective and preventive action systems record nonconformities, contain immediate risk, investigate causes, assign actions and verify effectiveness. Root-cause tools may include the five whys, cause-and-effect diagrams, Pareto analysis and process observation. Actions should address systemic causes rather than blame individuals without evidence.

Quality standards and certifications can provide a management framework, but certification does not guarantee every output is defect-free. Organisations should verify supplier and process performance through appropriate audits, testing, records and customer feedback.

13. Maintenance, reliability and asset management

Maintenance preserves or restores equipment and facilities so that they can perform required functions safely. Reactive maintenance occurs after failure; preventive maintenance is performed at planned intervals; condition-based maintenance uses observed equipment condition; predictive maintenance uses data and models to estimate likely failure.

Reliability is the probability that an item performs its required function for a stated period under stated conditions. Availability reflects the proportion of time that equipment is capable of operating when required. Maintainability concerns how readily an asset can be restored. These concepts support decisions about spare parts, redundancy and maintenance intervals.

Overall equipment effectiveness is commonly calculated as availability multiplied by performance rate multiplied by quality rate. It helps expose losses from downtime, slow running and defects. The measure must be applied consistently and should not encourage unsafe operation or conceal planned maintenance requirements.

Asset management considers lifecycle value from acquisition and commissioning through operation, maintenance, renewal and disposal. Procurement should evaluate maintainability, spare-part availability, energy use, training, warranties and support when selecting equipment. A cheap asset may have high operating and failure costs.

Maintenance planning should coordinate with production schedules, supplier lead times, safety permits and spare-parts inventory. Critical assets may require condition monitoring, backup capacity and tested recovery procedures. Maintenance records support reliability analysis and investment decisions.

14. Demand management and operations planning

Operations planning translates expected demand into resource requirements, output plans and delivery commitments. Demand may be forecast, booked, contracted or generated by replenishment signals. Managers should distinguish demand from shipments, orders and consumption because these measures can differ due to stock changes and timing.

Aggregate planning balances expected demand and capacity over a medium-term horizon. Options include adjusting workforce, shifts, inventory, subcontracting, backlog, pricing or demand timing. The chosen plan should consider cost, service, employee impact, supplier capability and the consequences of forecast error.

Sales and operations planning brings commercial, operational, supply and finance perspectives into a common plan. It reconciles product-family demand, supply capacity, inventory, revenue and resource constraints. Good planning creates a shared set of assumptions and escalates trade-offs to decision-makers.

Demand shaping can use promotions, appointments, pricing, product substitution or delivery windows to influence when and how customers consume capacity. These actions should be assessed for profitability, fairness and downstream effects. A promotion that increases orders beyond fulfilment capacity can damage service and reputation.

Planning is a continuous cycle. Actual demand, forecast accuracy, capacity performance and supply constraints should be reviewed regularly. Forecasts should be updated with new information while preserving a record of assumptions and changes so that teams can learn from systematic bias.

15. Forecasting for operational decisions

Forecasting estimates future demand or workload using historical observations, causal information and informed judgement. Qualitative methods include expert panels, customer research and sales-force estimates. Quantitative methods include naïve forecasts, moving averages, weighted moving averages, exponential smoothing and regression.

A naïve forecast uses the most recent observation as the next-period forecast. A simple moving average uses the mean of a fixed number of recent periods. A weighted moving average assigns greater influence to selected observations. Exponential smoothing updates a previous forecast using a proportion of the latest forecast error.

Forecast accuracy can be measured using mean absolute deviation, mean squared error, root mean squared error or mean absolute percentage error where appropriate. Percentage measures can become unstable when actual demand is zero or very small. Error metrics should be interpreted alongside bias and business consequences.

Forecast bias occurs when forecasts are consistently too high or too low. Tracking cumulative error and patterns by product, customer or horizon can reveal bias. A forecast that is accurate in aggregate may still be poor for individual items, so segmentation matters.

Forecasts are not promises. Operational plans should identify uncertainty, scenarios and response options. High-impact decisions may require ranges, probability estimates or sensitivity analysis rather than a single point estimate.

16. Queueing, waiting time and service operations

Queues form when demand for a service temporarily or persistently exceeds available capacity. Waiting depends on arrival patterns, service-time variability, number of servers, priority rules and customer behaviour. Even when average capacity exceeds average demand, variability can create substantial queues.

Queueing theory studies relationships among arrival rate, service rate, utilisation, queue length and waiting time. In a simple stable single-server model, utilisation is arrival rate divided by service rate and must remain below one for a steady-state queue. Real systems often require more complex models and empirical validation.

Managers can reduce waiting by increasing capacity at peak times, smoothing arrivals, simplifying service, reducing variability, providing self-service options or separating simple and complex requests. Appointment systems can improve predictability but need safeguards for no-shows, urgent cases and unequal access.

Customers experience both actual waiting and perceived waiting. Information, fair queue rules, visible progress and a comfortable environment can improve the experience. Unexplained silence or repeated changes to promised times often make waiting feel worse.

Service design should balance utilisation and responsiveness. Running every resource at near-maximum utilisation may appear efficient but leaves little capacity to absorb variation. A deliberate capacity buffer may be economically justified where waiting has high customer or safety consequences.

17. Supply chain integration and interfunctional coordination

Operations performance depends on coordination with procurement, inventory, warehousing, transport, marketing, finance, engineering and customer service. Local optimisation can damage the end-to-end system: large purchase batches may lower unit prices but raise inventory; high machine utilisation may create excess output; transport consolidation may delay urgent customer orders.

Integrated planning shares demand forecasts, production schedules, inventory status, supplier capacity, transport constraints and customer priorities. Common data definitions and agreed planning horizons reduce confusion. Information should be timely, accurate and accessible to authorised users.

Cross-functional teams resolve trade-offs that cannot be managed by one department. They may review new product introduction, supplier changes, promotions, capacity constraints, quality incidents and major customer commitments. Decision rights should be clear so that collaboration does not become an endless meeting without accountability.

Supplier integration may include collaborative forecasting, shared schedules, capacity reservations, vendor-managed replenishment and joint improvement. These arrangements require appropriate confidentiality, data security, commercial terms and realistic commitments from both sides.

Performance dashboards should show end-to-end outcomes such as order-to-delivery time, perfect-order rate, inventory turns, forecast error, throughput, defects and customer complaints. Measures should be balanced so that improving one function does not simply transfer cost or risk to another.

18. Operations technology, automation and digital systems

Operations technology includes production equipment, robotics, sensors, enterprise systems, warehouse management, transport management, manufacturing execution, workflow automation and analytics. Technology can improve precision, visibility, speed and safety, but benefits depend on process suitability and integration.

Automation is most effective when tasks are repetitive, measurable, hazardous or require consistent precision. It may be less suitable where demand is highly variable, tasks require nuanced human judgement or the investment cannot be justified by volume. A sound business case includes capital, installation, integration, maintenance, training, cybersecurity and eventual replacement.

Digital twins and simulation models can represent facilities, equipment or supply flows to test scenarios before implementation. Their value depends on model quality, data freshness and assumptions. A simulation is not a substitute for validation against real operations.

Connected devices can provide real-time condition, location, temperature, energy and production data. This visibility can support predictive maintenance and exception management. Systems must address device security, access rights, data quality, connectivity failure and safe fallback procedures.

Technology projects should define process owners, requirements, interfaces, data governance, user training, benefits measures and support arrangements. Organisations should plan for downtime and manual continuity when digital systems become unavailable.

19. Health, safety, ergonomics and operational risk

Health and safety are fundamental operational objectives. Risk assessment identifies hazards, who may be harmed, likelihood and severity, existing controls and further actions. The hierarchy of controls prioritises elimination, substitution and engineering controls before relying mainly on administrative measures or personal protective equipment.

Operational hazards may include moving machinery, vehicles, manual handling, chemicals, noise, heat, work at height, fatigue, electrical energy and psychosocial risks. Controls should be specific to the task and reviewed after incidents, process changes or new equipment.

Ergonomics designs work to fit human capabilities and limitations. It considers posture, reach, force, repetition, cognitive load, lighting, noise and workstation layout. Good ergonomics can reduce injury and error while improving comfort and productivity.

Near-miss reporting and learning systems help identify weak controls before serious harm occurs. Reports should be investigated fairly and trends reviewed. A blame culture suppresses information, while a learning culture encourages prompt reporting and practical prevention.

Operational risk management should include emergency response, business continuity, contractor safety, equipment integrity and environmental incidents. Plans need named roles, communication routes, resources and exercises. Compliance documents should reflect actual work practices.

20. Environmental performance and sustainable operations

Sustainable operations reduce environmental harm while maintaining service, quality and economic viability. Relevant issues include energy consumption, greenhouse-gas emissions, water use, material efficiency, pollution, packaging, waste, biodiversity and end-of-life impacts.

Resource efficiency begins with measurement. Managers establish baselines for energy per unit, water per service, material yield, scrap, waste and emissions. Boundaries and calculation methods should be consistent, and estimates should be distinguished from measured data.

Process redesign can reduce environmental impact through improved yield, efficient equipment, heat recovery, renewable energy, water reuse, lighter packaging, repair and product-life extension. The best intervention depends on lifecycle effects; moving an impact from one stage or location to another does not necessarily improve the total outcome.

Environmental management systems can support identification of aspects, legal obligations, objectives, operational controls, monitoring and continual improvement. Certification may demonstrate a management framework but does not itself prove that every environmental outcome is satisfactory.

Operations teams should work with procurement and suppliers to understand upstream and downstream impacts. Sustainable performance often depends on product design, material choice, transport mode, supplier processes and customer use. Claims should be evidence-based and transparent about scope.

21. Continuous improvement and organisational learning

Continuous improvement is the disciplined, ongoing effort to improve processes, products and services. The Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle begins by defining a problem and planning a change, tests the change, evaluates evidence and standardises or adjusts the approach. Small experiments can reduce the risk of large-scale failure.

Kaizen emphasises incremental improvement involving people who perform the work. Improvement suggestions should receive timely feedback and practical support. Teams need authority to raise issues and access to data, tools and decision-makers.

Root-cause analysis separates symptoms from underlying causes. The five-whys technique asks repeated questions, while cause-and-effect diagrams organise possible causes by categories such as people, methods, machines, materials, measurement and environment. These tools generate hypotheses that should be tested with evidence.

Standardisation preserves a proven method and provides a baseline for future improvement. Standard work should describe the safest and most reliable known method, while remaining open to revision when better evidence emerges. Standards that are unrealistic or outdated encourage workarounds.

Organisational learning requires recording experiments, results, failures and lessons. Teams should share improvements across sites where context permits. A practice that succeeds in one environment should be adapted and tested before being copied elsewhere.

22. Operations performance measurement and control

An operations control system compares actual performance with plans, standards and customer requirements. It detects deviations, investigates causes and initiates corrective action. Measures should be actionable, timely, understood by users and linked to decisions.

Common measures include throughput, cycle time, lead time, work-in-progress, schedule adherence, first-pass yield, defect rate, capacity utilisation, on-time delivery, cost per unit, energy intensity and customer satisfaction. Definitions must be explicit, including numerator, denominator, period, scope and data source.

Balanced scorecards connect operational indicators with financial, customer, internal-process and learning objectives. Leading indicators such as preventive maintenance completion or training may signal future performance, while lagging indicators such as defects and complaints record outcomes already experienced.

Visual management displays status, targets, abnormalities and responsibilities close to the work. It should make problems easier to see rather than become a decorative dashboard. Escalation rules should identify who responds, within what time and with what authority.

Performance reviews should distinguish controllable and external factors, test data quality and encourage honest reporting. Targets can distort behaviour if they are narrow or punitive. Managers should monitor unintended consequences and revise measures when they no longer represent the desired outcome.

23. Operations strategy, resilience and future challenges

Operations strategy determines how process capabilities support the organisation's long-term position. It involves deliberate choices about capacity, location, technology, process type, sourcing, skills, quality systems, sustainability and network configuration. Strategic choices should be consistent with customer value and financial realities.

Resilience is the ability to anticipate, absorb, respond to and recover from disruption. It may require alternate capacity, flexible processes, qualified substitutes, spare parts, cross-trained employees, supplier visibility, cybersecurity controls and tested continuity plans. Resilience is designed around critical services and acceptable recovery times.

Global disruptions, climate hazards, cyber incidents, geopolitical changes, labour shortages and infrastructure failures can affect operations. Scenario planning tests how the organisation would respond to plausible events. Plans should identify dependencies and decision triggers rather than rely on a single forecast.

Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, robotics, additive manufacturing, advanced analytics and autonomous systems may change process economics and workforce requirements. Adoption should consider reliability, explainability, safety, privacy, skills, data rights and the consequences of system failure.

Future-ready operations combine digital capability with human judgement, responsible resource use and adaptable processes. Competitive advantage comes not from technology alone but from integrating it with people, governance, supplier networks and continuous learning.

Applied operations and process management case studies

Bottleneck in a food-processing plant

Situation

A food processor has a packaging line that limits daily output although upstream mixing equipment frequently runs at high utilisation. Finished goods accumulate before packaging and customer orders are delayed.

Analysis

The operations team measures each stage's cycle time, changeover, downtime, yield and queue. It confirms packaging is the constraint and examines whether poor scheduling, material shortages and frequent changeovers reduce available time.

Intervention

The team protects packaging time, schedules product families to reduce changeovers, stages packaging materials in advance and trains maintenance staff to respond quickly. A modest equipment upgrade is assessed only after existing capacity is better exploited.

Control and evaluation

Throughput, queue size, changeover duration, unplanned downtime, waste and on-time delivery are monitored. Once packaging capacity improves, the team checks whether another process has become the constraint.

Hospital outpatient waiting-time redesign

Situation

A clinic receives complaints about long waits despite clinicians appearing busy throughout the day.

Analysis

The team maps registration, triage, consultation, diagnostics and discharge. It analyses arrival patterns, appointment adherence, service-time variation, repeat visits and handoffs, while protecting clinical safety and privacy.

Intervention

The redesign separates quick follow-up cases from complex consultations, introduces clearer appointment information, improves pre-visit records and creates escalation capacity for urgent patients. Staff test the changes in a limited clinic before wider rollout.

Control and evaluation

Measures include median and 90th-percentile waiting time, missed appointments, rework, patient experience, clinical incidents and staff workload. Results are reviewed across different patient groups to detect unequal effects.

Lean improvement in a distribution centre

Situation

A distribution centre experiences long picker travel distances, congested aisles and frequent order errors during peak periods.

Analysis

A value-stream map and travel observations identify poor slotting, duplicate handling, unclear replenishment signals and congestion around fast-moving products.

Intervention

The team relocates suitable fast movers, introduces clearer location labels, separates replenishment routes where possible and uses scan validation at critical points. Changes are piloted with safety review and employee input.

Control and evaluation

Order accuracy, pick travel per line, lines per labour hour, congestion events, replenishment delay and injury/near-miss reports are monitored together to avoid trading safety for speed.

Capacity and workforce planning for seasonal demand

Situation

An online retailer expects a major seasonal demand increase but has limited warehouse space and uncertain temporary labour availability.

Analysis

Planners create base, high and low demand scenarios and translate each into receiving, storage, picking, packing, transport and customer-service workloads. They identify skills, shift and supplier constraints.

Intervention

The organisation secures temporary labour and transport options, cross-trains permanent staff, adjusts promotional timing and prepositions selected inventory. It defines triggers for adding shifts or using overflow capacity.

Control and evaluation

The team reviews forecast error, backlog, service level, overtime, temporary-worker training, safety and cost per order daily during the peak period.

Quality improvement in a component assembly process

Situation

A manufacturer finds that a rising share of assembled components fail final inspection, causing rework and late deliveries.

Analysis

The team validates defect coding and measurement systems, stratifies failures by product, shift, supplier lot and machine, and uses process observation to test likely causes.

Intervention

Actions include supplier lot controls, fixture maintenance, operator training, revised standard work and a mistake-proofing feature. The process is monitored through appropriate control charts and verification sampling.

Control and evaluation

First-pass yield, defect rate, rework hours, supplier defects, process stability and customer returns are tracked. Corrective actions are closed only after effectiveness is demonstrated.

University examination questions and model answers

These questions test conceptual understanding, calculations and application. In extended responses, support recommendations with evidence, assumptions, formulas and consideration of unintended consequences.

1. Define operations management.

Model answer: It is the design, management and improvement of processes that transform resources such as people, materials, information and equipment into goods and services that meet customer and organisational requirements.

2. Explain the five common operations performance objectives.

Model answer: Quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost. They describe conformance, elapsed time, reliability of promises, ability to change and resource efficiency.

3. Distinguish design capacity, effective capacity and actual output.

Model answer: Design capacity is theoretical maximum output; effective capacity accounts for realistic constraints; actual output is the quantity actually produced in a stated period.

4. A plant produces 720 units against a design capacity of 900 units. Calculate utilisation.

Model answer: Utilisation = 720/900 × 100 = 80%.

5. A process produces 680 units against an effective capacity of 800 units. Calculate efficiency.

Model answer: Efficiency = 680/800 × 100 = 85%.

6. What is a bottleneck?

Model answer: A resource or process stage whose available capacity constrains the throughput of the overall system.

7. State the five focusing steps of the theory of constraints.

Model answer: Identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate other activities to it, elevate its capacity, and repeat when the constraint changes.

8. Distinguish process layout from product layout.

Model answer: Process layout groups similar resources and supports varied routing; product layout arranges resources in task sequence and supports repeatable high-volume flow.

9. Define productivity and give a formula.

Model answer: Productivity is output relative to input. Labour productivity = units of acceptable output divided by labour hours.

10. A team completes 360 orders in 45 labour hours. Calculate labour productivity.

Model answer: 360/45 = 8 completed orders per labour hour.

11. Explain the purpose of process mapping.

Model answer: It makes activities, decisions, handoffs, information and delays visible so that teams can understand, communicate and improve the end-to-end process.

12. What is value-stream mapping?

Model answer: A method for analysing material and information flow from request to delivery, including processing time, waiting, inventory and information signals.

13. Name seven forms of operational waste commonly addressed by lean.

Model answer: Overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, excess inventory, unnecessary motion and defects; underused human capability is also commonly included.

14. Explain the DMAIC improvement framework.

Model answer: Define the problem, measure baseline performance, analyse causes, improve the process and control the gains.

15. Distinguish control limits from specification limits.

Model answer: Control limits are statistically derived from process behaviour; specification limits are set by customer, design or regulatory requirements.

16. A process has 96 acceptable units from 100 produced. Calculate first-pass yield.

Model answer: First-pass yield = 96/100 × 100 = 96%, assuming all 100 units were assessed at the first pass.

17. What is overall equipment effectiveness?

Model answer: A measure commonly calculated as availability × performance rate × quality rate, expressed using consistent percentage or decimal values.

18. Explain preventive and predictive maintenance.

Model answer: Preventive maintenance is performed at planned intervals; predictive maintenance uses condition data and analysis to estimate when maintenance is needed.

19. What is aggregate planning?

Model answer: Medium-term balancing of expected demand and available capacity through workforce, shifts, inventory, subcontracting, backlog and demand-management decisions.

20. A three-period moving average uses demands 120, 150 and 180 units. Calculate the forecast for the next period.

Model answer: Forecast = (120 + 150 + 180)/3 = 150 units.

21. What is forecast bias?

Model answer: A systematic tendency for forecasts to be consistently above or below actual demand.

22. Explain why high utilisation can increase waiting time.

Model answer: At high utilisation, little spare capacity remains to absorb variability in arrivals or service times, so queues can grow rapidly even if average capacity is sufficient.

23. What is a service blueprint?

Model answer: A map distinguishing customer actions, visible employee actions, backstage activities and support processes, showing how service delivery is experienced and enabled.

24. State four ways to improve operational resilience.

Model answer: Qualify alternate sources, maintain critical buffers or spare capacity, cross-train staff, improve visibility, strengthen cybersecurity and test continuity plans.

25. A single-server service receives 18 jobs per hour and can complete 24 per hour. Calculate utilisation.

Model answer: Utilisation = 18/24 = 0.75, or 75%. The simple stable single-server model requires arrival rate below service rate.

26. Explain the cost of quality categories.

Model answer: Prevention costs avoid defects; appraisal costs assess conformity; internal failure costs arise before delivery; external failure costs arise after delivery.

27. Why should safety indicators be included in productivity improvement?

Model answer: They help ensure output gains are not achieved through unsafe speed, fatigue, excessive workload or increased injury risk.

28. Explain the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle.

Model answer: Plan a change, implement or test it, check evidence against expectations, and act by standardising, adjusting or abandoning the change.

29. What is work-in-progress and why should it be controlled?

Model answer: Work-in-progress is partially completed work between process stages. Excessive levels tie up capital, extend lead times, hide defects and make priorities harder to manage.

30. Explain how operations and procurement should coordinate.

Model answer: They should share demand, specifications, supplier capacity, quality, lead-time and inventory information so that purchasing decisions support process flow, continuity and customer commitments.

Chapter summary

Operations management converts resources into goods and services through processes that must meet customer and organisational requirements. Managers design process types, layouts, capacity, schedules and workflows while balancing quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost. Process mapping and performance measurement make the system visible and support evidence-based decisions.

Lean, Six Sigma, quality management, maintenance and continuous improvement provide complementary approaches to reducing waste, variation and failure. Forecasting, aggregate planning, queue analysis and bottleneck management help align capacity with demand. Sustainable and resilient operations integrate people, technology, suppliers, safety, environmental performance and learning into a coherent operating system.
