Course guide

Learning outcomes

After studying this chapter, learners should be able to explain management functions and theories; analyse organisational structures and leadership; apply economic concepts of scarcity, demand, supply, elasticity and cost; interpret basic financial information; evaluate budgets and investment decisions; and develop evidence-based management recommendations for supply chain organisations.

Study approach: Relate each concept to a real organisation. For numerical work, show formulas, substitutions, units and interpretation. For case analysis, distinguish evidence from assumptions and explain the consequences for employees, suppliers, customers and the wider network.

Scope note: Accounting standards, employment rules, tax, competition law and financial regulation vary by jurisdiction. This chapter explains general academic principles rather than providing legal, tax or investment advice.

1. Management as a coordinated organisational process

Management is the process of achieving organisational objectives through the coordinated use of people, money, materials, information, technology and time. In supply chain organisations, management connects procurement, production, inventory, transport, customer service, finance and supplier relationships. It is not limited to issuing instructions; it involves setting direction, designing work, developing capability, making decisions, measuring results and adapting to change.

Efficiency concerns the relationship between outputs and resources consumed, while effectiveness concerns whether the intended objectives are achieved. A warehouse may pick many order lines per hour but still be ineffective if orders are inaccurate or customers receive them late. Strong management therefore balances productivity with quality, service, safety, employee wellbeing and long-term organisational performance.

Management operates at strategic, tactical and operational levels. Strategic management establishes long-term direction, network configuration and competitive priorities. Tactical management translates strategy into budgets, capacity plans, sourcing strategies and service policies. Operational management coordinates daily orders, shifts, deliveries, stock movements and exception handling. These levels must exchange information so that local decisions remain aligned with organisational intent.

Supply chains also require management across organisational boundaries. A buyer may not directly control a supplier's factory, a carrier's fleet or a distributor's warehouse, yet its customer promise depends on all of them. Managers use contracts, service-level agreements, shared measures, communication routines, incentives and relationship governance to coordinate activity while respecting each organisation's independence.

2. The evolution of management thought

Classical management approaches emerged as organisations sought predictable output, specialisation and control in growing industrial systems. Scientific management emphasised work study, standard methods, task design and measurement. Administrative approaches examined management functions and principles, while bureaucratic theory highlighted formal rules, hierarchy, authority and documented responsibilities.

Human relations approaches drew attention to motivation, group behaviour, communication, leadership and the social conditions of work. They challenged the assumption that employees respond only to financial incentives or mechanical supervision. In a distribution centre, for example, safe productivity depends on training, equipment, workload design, trust and employee participation as well as targets.

Systems thinking views an organisation as a set of interdependent parts that exchange resources and information with an external environment. A supply chain is an especially clear example: a purchasing decision can change production schedules, warehouse space, transport demand, cash requirements and customer service. Systems thinking discourages managers from optimising one function without examining wider consequences.

Contingency theory argues that there is no single management method that is best in every situation. Appropriate structure and leadership depend on uncertainty, technology, workforce skills, scale, regulation and task complexity. A stable replenishment operation may benefit from standard routines, while a disruption-response team may need decentralised authority and rapid cross-functional coordination.

3. Planning, organising, leading and controlling

Planning establishes objectives and determines actions, resources, timing and assumptions. Supply chain plans may cover demand, supply, sourcing, inventory, capacity, distribution, budgets and continuity. Effective plans specify measurable outcomes, responsible owners, milestones and contingency triggers. Planning should be treated as a recurring decision process rather than a document prepared once and ignored.

Organising allocates tasks, authority, resources and relationships. It determines who is responsible for supplier selection, purchase approvals, receiving, quality inspection, stock adjustment, freight booking and customer communication. Clear role design reduces duplication and control gaps. Cross-functional processes require explicit ownership where no single department can deliver the outcome alone.

Leading influences people toward shared objectives through communication, direction, coaching, motivation, example and conflict resolution. Supply chain leaders must coordinate employees with different professional priorities, such as procurement cost, production continuity, financial control and customer responsiveness. Leadership is effective when people understand the purpose of decisions and can raise risks early.

Controlling compares actual performance with agreed standards, identifies deviations and initiates corrective action. It may include budget control, supplier scorecards, stock accuracy checks, quality inspection, delivery monitoring and audit. Control should generate learning, not merely blame. Managers should distinguish isolated errors from recurring process weaknesses and verify whether corrective actions work.

4. Organisational structure and supply chain coordination

Organisational structure defines reporting relationships, roles, authority and communication channels. Functional structures group people by expertise, such as procurement, finance, operations, marketing and logistics. They can develop specialist capability but may create silos when each department optimises its own targets. Supply chain performance often requires process ownership that crosses functional boundaries.

Divisional structures group activities by product, geography, customer or business unit. They can improve market focus and accountability but may duplicate procurement, systems or distribution capabilities. Matrix structures combine functional and project or product responsibilities, enabling shared expertise but sometimes creating competing priorities and unclear authority.

Process-based and network structures organise around end-to-end value creation or partner ecosystems. A supply chain control tower, for example, may bring together planners, logistics specialists, customer service and risk analysts to coordinate exceptions. Such arrangements need clear decision rights and escalation rules to prevent meetings from replacing accountable action.

Structure should fit strategy, scale, geography, technology and uncertainty. Managers should assess span of control, centralisation, formalisation and coordination costs. A central procurement team may leverage spend and standardise controls, while local teams may respond faster to local markets. Hybrid designs often combine central policy with delegated execution within defined limits.

5. Business economics and scarcity

Economics studies how individuals, organisations and societies allocate scarce resources among competing uses. Supply chain managers face scarcity in capital, labour, materials, warehouse space, transport capacity, time and reliable information. Every decision has an opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative that is forgone when a resource is committed.

Needs and wants exceed available resources, so organisations must prioritise. A transport manager allocating limited vehicle capacity to urgent medical goods may defer lower-priority shipments. The decision should be based on explicit criteria such as criticality, contractual obligations, customer impact, safety and total network consequences rather than informal influence.

Microeconomics examines decisions by consumers, firms and markets. It helps explain demand, supply, pricing, competition, production costs and supplier behaviour. Macroeconomics examines economy-wide variables such as inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, unemployment, growth and trade balances. Both levels affect procurement budgets, customer demand and supply chain investment.

Economic reasoning requires stating assumptions and recognising uncertainty. A forecast of lower freight prices may be useful for scenario planning but should not be treated as guaranteed. Managers should distinguish nominal values from inflation-adjusted values, and should compare options over consistent time horizons and currencies.

6. Demand, supply and market equilibrium

Demand describes the quantities buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices during a specified period, other things being equal. The law of demand generally predicts an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded. Demand can also be influenced by income, preferences, substitute and complementary products, expectations, population and availability.

Supply describes quantities producers are willing and able to offer at different prices. Higher prices may encourage additional supply when firms can expand output profitably, although capacity, technology, input costs, regulation and lead times constrain the response. Supply in the short run may be less flexible than supply over a longer period.

Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied under the model's assumptions. A shortage exists when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the prevailing price; a surplus exists when quantity supplied exceeds demand. In real supply chains, adjustment may occur through price, allocation, queues, contracts, rationing, substitutions or capacity investment.

Managers should not assume that price alone clears every market immediately. Critical goods may be subject to regulated prices, long-term contracts, limited supplier capacity or ethical allocation rules. For essential products, service continuity and fair access may be more important than short-term price optimisation.

7. Elasticity and its managerial applications

Price elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded resulting from a one per cent change in price, holding other factors constant. Elastic demand means quantity responds proportionately more than price; inelastic demand means the response is proportionately smaller. Elasticity varies with availability of substitutes, necessity, budget share, time and customer segment.

Income elasticity measures how demand changes as income changes. Cross-price elasticity measures how demand for one product responds to a price change in another product. Positive cross-price elasticity may indicate substitutes, while negative cross-price elasticity may indicate complements. These measures help managers assess product portfolios, supplier exposure and demand scenarios.

Elasticity is useful for evaluating promotions, price changes and capacity decisions, but estimates depend on data and market conditions. A temporary discount may shift purchases forward rather than create lasting demand. A price increase may also affect customer loyalty, competitor response and long-term market position.

Supply elasticity measures how responsive quantity supplied is to price changes. It may be low when production requires specialised equipment, scarce inputs, regulatory approval or long lead times. Procurement teams should understand supplier capacity and substitution constraints before assuming that higher prices will quickly secure additional volume.

8. Costs, production and economies of scale

Fixed costs do not vary directly with output over a relevant range and period, while variable costs change with the quantity produced or handled. Semi-variable costs contain both fixed and variable elements. Total cost is the sum of relevant cost components. Average cost is total cost divided by output, while marginal cost is the additional cost of producing one more unit.

Economies of scale occur when average cost falls as output expands, often because fixed costs are spread over more units, purchasing leverage improves or specialised equipment becomes viable. Diseconomies of scale can arise when coordination, congestion, bureaucracy or complexity grows faster than output. Scale is valuable only when demand and operating capability justify it.

Production functions describe the relationship between inputs and output. In the short run, at least one input may be fixed, so adding labour to a constrained facility may eventually produce diminishing marginal returns. In the long run, organisations can change plant, technology and network size. Capacity decisions should include ramp-up time, utilisation and demand uncertainty.

Supply chain costing should include the costs created elsewhere by a decision. A large purchase batch may reduce unit price but increase storage, handling, insurance, expiry and working-capital costs. A lower-cost distant supplier may increase lead time and disruption exposure. Relevant cost analysis compares total consequences rather than isolated price quotations.

9. Market structures and competition

Perfect competition is a theoretical market structure with many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, information and relatively free entry and exit. Firms are price takers in the model. Real procurement markets rarely meet every assumption, but the framework helps explain how competition can constrain supplier pricing and encourage efficiency.

Monopoly describes a market with a single dominant supplier and significant barriers to entry. Oligopoly involves a small number of influential firms whose decisions may be interdependent. Monopolistic competition involves many firms offering differentiated products. Market structure affects bargaining power, supplier alternatives, innovation and exposure to price changes.

Buyer power depends on purchase volume, availability of alternatives, switching costs, information, supplier dependence and the strategic importance of the customer. A large buyer may negotiate favourable terms, but excessive pressure can damage supplier investment, quality or continuity. Long-term value often depends on a commercially sustainable relationship.

Procurement professionals should understand competition law and avoid collusive practices, bid rigging, market allocation or improper exchange of competitively sensitive information. Supplier market analysis should support fair and transparent sourcing, not facilitate anti-competitive coordination.

10. Money, banking, interest rates and investment

Money performs functions as a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. Financial institutions connect savers and borrowers, facilitate payments and provide credit. Supply chain businesses use banking services for supplier payments, trade finance, payroll, foreign exchange, guarantees and working-capital management.

Interest is the cost of borrowing or return on lending. Simple interest is calculated on the original principal, while compound interest applies interest to accumulated balances. The effective cost of credit may also include arrangement fees, insurance, transaction charges and covenants. Managers should compare the total financing cost over the relevant period.

Investment decisions compare the expected benefits and costs of long-lived assets such as warehouses, vehicles, automation and information systems. Net present value discounts future cash flows to reflect the time value of money. Payback period estimates how long it takes to recover the initial investment but does not fully account for cash flows after payback or the time value of money.

Capital is scarce and projects compete for funding. Investment appraisal should include demand assumptions, implementation risk, maintenance, training, working capital, residual value and decommissioning. Sensitivity and scenario analysis reveal how project value changes when costs, benefits, adoption or operating conditions differ from expectations.

11. Inflation, exchange rates and the business environment

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level, reducing the purchasing power of money. It can increase wages, fuel, packaging, rent, freight and supplier prices. Managers should distinguish general inflation from category-specific price movements and examine whether contracts allow adjustments or require renegotiation.

Exchange rates express the value of one currency in terms of another. A depreciation of the buyer's currency can increase the domestic cost of imported inputs, while an appreciation may reduce it. Currency exposure depends on invoice currency, timing, payment terms, hedging arrangements and the ability to pass cost changes to customers.

Interest rates influence borrowing costs, consumer spending, investment and inventory financing. Higher rates can make large stock holdings more expensive and delay capital projects. Economic growth and employment affect demand, while trade policies, taxation and public investment can change supplier markets and logistics infrastructure.

Managers should use scenario analysis rather than rely on a single macroeconomic forecast. Scenarios may combine currency movement, fuel price changes, inflation, demand contraction and border disruption. The purpose is to test decisions and identify triggers, not to claim certainty about future economic conditions.

12. Financial statements and managerial interpretation

The income statement reports revenue, expenses and profit over a period. The balance sheet reports assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. The cash flow statement explains cash movements from operating, investing and financing activities. Together, these statements provide a view of performance, financial position and liquidity.

Supply chain decisions affect financial statements. Inventory is generally recognised as an asset until sold or otherwise expensed under applicable accounting rules; obsolete or damaged inventory may require write-down. Freight, labour, utilities and warehousing affect operating costs depending on accounting treatment. Capital equipment affects assets and depreciation over time.

Liquidity ratios assess the ability to meet short-term obligations. The current ratio compares current assets with current liabilities; the quick ratio excludes less liquid current assets such as inventory under its conventional form. Profitability ratios examine margins and returns, while efficiency ratios examine asset use and working capital.

Financial ratios require context. A high current ratio may indicate resilience or inefficiently tied-up resources; a low inventory balance may indicate efficient planning or dangerous understocking. Comparisons should account for industry, seasonality, accounting policies, business model and one-off events.

13. Budgets, variance analysis and financial control

A budget translates plans into expected financial resources and results. Operating budgets may cover procurement, labour, transport, warehousing, maintenance and administration. Capital budgets cover major investments. Cash budgets estimate timing of receipts and payments, helping managers identify funding gaps before they become crises.

Variance analysis compares actual results with budget or standard. A variance may be favourable or adverse depending on whether it improves or worsens the intended outcome, but the label alone does not explain performance. Lower transport spending may be favourable financially yet adverse if it results from missed deliveries or reduced service.

Flexible budgets adjust expected costs for actual activity levels, helping distinguish volume effects from price or efficiency effects. Managers should investigate material variances, identify root causes, assign corrective actions and update forecasts where assumptions have changed. The aim is to improve decisions rather than punish unavoidable deviations.

Budget participation can improve operational realism because planners and supervisors understand capacity and constraints. However, targets can be distorted by sandbagging, gaming or short-term behaviour. Governance should use transparent assumptions, challenge evidence and connect financial measures with service, quality, safety and sustainability.

14. Decision-making and problem-solving methods

Managerial decisions range from routine and structured to novel and uncertain. Programmed decisions use established rules, such as reorder triggers or approval thresholds. Non-programmed decisions address unfamiliar issues such as a major supplier failure or network redesign and require judgement, analysis and consultation.

A systematic problem-solving process defines the problem, gathers evidence, identifies stakeholders, analyses causes, develops options, evaluates trade-offs, chooses an intervention, implements it and reviews outcomes. The problem statement should describe an observable gap without embedding an untested solution.

Tools include decision trees, cost-benefit analysis, SWOT analysis, Pareto analysis, cause-and-effect diagrams, scenario planning and multi-criteria decision analysis. Each tool has limits. A weighted scorecard can structure judgement but cannot make subjective weights objective; a cost-benefit model can omit impacts that are difficult to monetise.

Good decisions make assumptions explicit and record uncertainty, accountability and review points. Managers should consider reversibility, timing, ethical consequences and the cost of delay. Where evidence is incomplete, a controlled pilot or staged commitment may be safer than an irreversible large-scale decision.

15. Leadership, motivation and employee engagement

Leadership is the process of influencing people and groups toward shared goals. Transactional leadership clarifies expectations, performance and consequences; transformational leadership communicates purpose, encourages improvement and develops commitment. Situational leadership adapts direction and support to task competence and confidence.

Motivation theories offer different explanations of employee effort. Needs-based approaches examine what people value, while expectancy theory links motivation to the belief that effort can produce performance and valued outcomes. Equity perspectives consider perceived fairness. No single theory explains every individual or context.

Supply chain work can involve shift schedules, physical demands, time pressure, repetitive tasks and safety hazards. Motivation depends on fair treatment, adequate equipment, meaningful feedback, development, reasonable workloads and employee voice. Incentives that reward speed without quality or safety safeguards may encourage harmful shortcuts.

Managers should build psychological safety so employees can report near misses, inaccurate records, supplier concerns and unrealistic plans. Engagement is not achieved by slogans; it requires credible action on issues raised, consistent communication and respect for the knowledge of frontline workers.

16. Communication, negotiation and conflict management

Communication transfers information and meaning through written, verbal, visual and digital channels. Supply chain communication must be timely, accurate, complete and appropriate to the recipient. A delivery exception message should identify the affected order, expected impact, current status, available options, decision deadline and responsible contact.

Negotiation is a process through which parties seek agreement where interests overlap and differ. Preparation includes defining objectives, alternatives, constraints, evidence, authority and relationship considerations. A strong negotiator understands the other party's needs and seeks sustainable value rather than focusing only on extracting a concession.

Distributive negotiation treats value as a fixed amount to be divided, while integrative negotiation explores options that may create additional value. For example, a buyer and supplier might agree on more reliable forecasts and consolidated deliveries in exchange for a mutually acceptable price and capacity commitment.

Conflict may arise from scarce resources, unclear roles, competing measures, cultural differences or incompatible assumptions. Managers should separate facts from interpretations, listen to affected parties, clarify interests and agree actions. Serious misconduct, safety issues or legal concerns require formal escalation rather than informal compromise.

17. Ethics, corporate governance and social responsibility

Business ethics concerns principles used to judge conduct and decisions, including honesty, fairness, respect, accountability and avoidance of harm. Supply chain ethics applies to procurement integrity, labour conditions, product safety, environmental impacts, data handling and treatment of suppliers and customers.

Corporate governance defines how an organisation is directed, controlled and held accountable. It includes board oversight, delegated authority, internal controls, risk management, disclosure and stakeholder accountability. Good governance establishes who can approve commitments, manage conflicts, investigate concerns and monitor performance.

Ethical sourcing requires risk-based due diligence into working conditions, human rights, environmental practices and supplier conduct. A supplier code is only a starting point. Organisations need credible assessment, worker voice, remediation, follow-up and responsible purchasing practices that do not make compliance impossible through unrealistic lead times or prices.

Corporate social responsibility considers how business activity affects employees, communities, customers, the environment and wider society. Responsible practice should be integrated into strategy and operational decisions. Managers should avoid unsupported claims and report methods, boundaries, limitations and progress honestly.

18. Project management and organisational change

Projects are temporary endeavours undertaken to create a defined product, service or change. Supply chain projects may implement a warehouse management system, redesign a distribution network, qualify a new supplier or automate a process. Project management coordinates scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, risk, communication and stakeholders.

Project scope defines what is included and excluded, expected deliverables and acceptance criteria. Work breakdown structures divide deliverables into manageable work packages. Schedules identify dependencies and milestones. Budgets estimate resources and contingency. Change control assesses requests before altering agreed baselines.

Organisational change affects roles, routines, systems, skills and relationships. Resistance may reflect uncertainty, loss of control, workload concerns or legitimate weaknesses in the proposed design. Change management should explain the case for change, involve affected users, provide training and support, and monitor adoption.

Project success should be judged by realised benefits, not merely by delivering on time and within budget. A system can be technically launched yet fail to improve data accuracy or service because users do not adopt it. Benefits owners should measure outcomes after implementation and address gaps.

19. Risk management and managerial control

Risk management identifies uncertainties that could affect objectives, evaluates their significance, selects responses and monitors changing exposure. Supply chain managers should consider operational, financial, strategic, compliance, safety, environmental, cyber and reputational risks. A risk register records descriptions, causes, consequences, existing controls, owners and actions.

Risk assessment may use qualitative scales or quantitative models. Likelihood and impact matrices help prioritise attention but can create false precision when ratings are subjective. Quantitative methods such as expected loss, sensitivity analysis or simulation may support decisions when data and assumptions are suitable.

Controls can be preventive, detective or corrective. Segregation of duties, supplier approval and access restrictions prevent or reduce errors; reconciliations, audits and alerts detect deviations; recovery plans and corrective actions address consequences. Controls should be proportionate to risk and should not create more cost or delay than the exposure justifies.

Managers must test whether controls operate in practice. A policy may exist but be ignored, misunderstood or technically bypassed. Control assurance combines evidence, observation, sampling, exception review and follow-up. Lessons from incidents should update procedures, training, contracts and system design.

20. Entrepreneurship, innovation and business models

Entrepreneurship identifies opportunities and mobilises resources to create value under uncertainty. Supply chain entrepreneurs may develop sourcing platforms, cold-chain services, fulfilment solutions, repair networks, local manufacturing or specialised distribution. Opportunity assessment considers customer need, market size, competition, capability, regulation and financial viability.

A business model explains how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value. It includes customer segments, value proposition, channels, relationships, key activities, resources, partners, cost structure and revenue streams. A logistics platform, for example, may coordinate capacity across independent carriers and earn fees for matching and visibility services.

Innovation can be incremental or radical and may concern products, processes, services or business models. Open innovation uses knowledge and collaboration beyond the organisation's boundaries. Pilots and minimum viable solutions can test assumptions, but they must protect customers, workers, data and safety.

Entrepreneurial decisions involve uncertainty and failure risk. Managers should distinguish a failed experiment that produces useful learning from repeated investment in an unsupported idea. Stage-gate decisions, clear success measures, capped exposure and honest reporting help organisations learn while controlling risk.

21. Business strategy and competitive advantage

Strategy establishes how an organisation intends to create distinctive value and sustain performance. It involves choices about markets, customers, products, capabilities, partners and resource allocation. Strategy is not simply a list of goals; it requires trade-offs and a coherent pattern of decisions.

Competitive advantage may arise from lower total cost, differentiation, reliable service, specialised knowledge, network reach, innovation or strong relationships. Supply chain capabilities can support advantage when they are valuable to customers and difficult for competitors to replicate. A low-cost sourcing model is not an advantage if it repeatedly causes quality failures.

Strategic analysis considers external opportunities and threats alongside internal strengths and weaknesses. Industry analysis examines customer power, supplier power, rivalry, substitutes and entry barriers. Resource-based analysis considers whether capabilities are valuable, rare, difficult to imitate and supported by the organisation.

Implementation requires objectives, measures, budgets, accountable owners and communication. Strategy should be reviewed when evidence changes, but constant direction changes can undermine execution. Managers should distinguish a genuine strategic adjustment from a reaction to every short-term fluctuation.

22. Performance measurement and continuous improvement

Performance measurement converts objectives into indicators that can guide decisions. Supply chain managers may monitor order fulfilment, inventory turns, forecast error, supplier defects, procurement savings, cost-to-serve, cash conversion, safety, emissions and employee development. Measures need clear definitions, reliable data and accountable owners.

Key performance indicators should be balanced. A cost-only target may damage service; a speed-only target may increase accidents; a high utilisation target may remove necessary resilience. A balanced scorecard links financial outcomes with customer value, internal processes, and learning and capability development.

Continuous improvement uses evidence to identify gaps, test changes and standardise successful practices. The Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle begins with a problem and proposed change, tests it at an appropriate scale, evaluates results and then adopts, adapts or abandons the intervention. Improvement should involve the people who perform the work.

Root-cause analysis asks why a problem occurs rather than treating symptoms. Pareto analysis prioritises frequent or high-impact causes; process mapping reveals delays and handoffs; statistical process control distinguishes common variation from special causes. Tools should support disciplined investigation rather than become paperwork.

23. Applied economics for procurement and supply decisions

Procurement decisions respond to input markets, supplier capacity, competition, inflation, exchange rates and customer demand. Economic analysis helps estimate how changes in price or availability affect total cost and operating plans. Managers should identify which costs are fixed, variable, avoidable, sunk or incremental for the decision under review.

Make-or-buy analysis compares internal production or service delivery with external sourcing. Relevant factors include avoidable cost, capacity, quality, intellectual property, strategic control, supplier capability, flexibility, risk and transition cost. A simple unit-cost comparison can be misleading if it ignores fixed costs that remain after outsourcing.

Break-even analysis estimates the activity level at which total revenue equals total cost under stated assumptions. Contribution per unit equals selling price less variable cost per unit. Break-even quantity equals fixed cost divided by contribution per unit. The method assumes stable relationships within the relevant range and should be treated as a planning model.

Scenario analysis evaluates alternative market conditions, such as fuel price increases, currency depreciation, demand growth or supplier shortages. Managers can compare expected cost, service and cash effects and identify thresholds that trigger action. This supports robust decisions even when the most likely scenario is uncertain.

24. Building a management and economics case analysis

A strong case analysis begins with the organisational context, decision question and evidence. It identifies objectives, stakeholders, constraints and the relevant time horizon. The analyst separates facts, assumptions and opinions so that readers can understand the basis of the recommendation.

Analysis combines management concepts with economic reasoning. It may examine organisational structure, leadership, incentives, demand and supply, market power, cost behaviour, cash flow and risk. Quantitative calculations should show formulas, units, assumptions and interpretation, while qualitative evidence explains implementation and stakeholder consequences.

Alternatives should be compared against explicit criteria such as cost, service, quality, feasibility, compliance, resilience, sustainability and strategic fit. The preferred option should explain why it is superior under the stated assumptions and what evidence could change the decision.

A recommendation needs an implementation plan with owners, milestones, resources, communication, controls and performance measures. It should include risks, contingencies and a review date. Good analysis is not merely descriptive: it leads to a defensible decision and a way to test whether the decision worked.

Applied management and economics case studies

Reducing warehouse operating cost without weakening service

Situation

A distribution company reports rising labour and handling costs while customers complain about late and inaccurate orders.

Analysis

The manager maps receiving, put-away, replenishment, picking and dispatch. The team analyses order profiles, travel distance, rework, overtime, training and error causes. It distinguishes volume growth from process inefficiency.

Options and action

A pilot redesigns fast-moving product locations, standardises replenishment triggers, improves shift handovers and trains staff in scanning and exception reporting. The business case includes equipment, training and transition costs.

Evaluation

Results are compared with baseline measures for cost per order, picking accuracy, dispatch time, safety and employee feedback. Savings are accepted only if service and safety remain within agreed thresholds.

Evaluating a make-or-buy decision

Situation

A manufacturer considers outsourcing a packaging operation to reduce unit cost and release floor space.

Analysis

The finance and operations teams identify avoidable internal costs, retained overhead, supplier charges, freight, quality inspection, transition cost, capacity constraints and risks to product confidentiality.

Options and action

They compare internal improvement, partial outsourcing and full outsourcing. Sensitivity analysis tests changes in volume, supplier price, exchange rate and defect rates. Supplier capacity and continuity are assessed separately from price.

Evaluation

The decision is staged through a controlled pilot and contract performance measures. The organisation retains a contingency plan and reviews actual total cost and service after implementation.

Managing a supplier price increase

Situation

A key supplier requests a substantial price increase, citing energy, labour and imported input costs.

Analysis

Procurement validates the cost drivers, contract terms, market evidence, currency exposure and alternative supply options. The team evaluates the effect on product margin, customer price and working capital.

Options and action

Negotiation explores transparent indexation, volume commitments, delivery consolidation, specification alternatives and joint waste reduction. The buyer avoids unsupported accusations and tests the supplier's assumptions.

Evaluation

An agreement records the pricing basis, review dates, service commitments and audit rights. The buyer monitors market changes and develops longer-term alternatives where concentration risk remains.

Leading a digital inventory transformation

Situation

An organisation introduces barcode-based stock control after repeated discrepancies between system records and physical stock.

Analysis

Managers identify process causes, user needs, master-data weaknesses, device requirements, integration risks and training gaps. They assess how work practices will change across receiving, storage, picking and returns.

Options and action

A phased rollout begins with a pilot site, clear data standards, role-based access, training, support and fallback procedures. Employees participate in testing and reporting defects.

Evaluation

The organisation measures record accuracy, transaction completion, stockout frequency, adjustment rates and adoption. Governance assigns ownership for data quality and ongoing system maintenance.

University examination questions and model answers

Questions assess conceptual understanding, application, calculation and managerial judgement. In quantitative answers, state assumptions and interpret the result in its organisational context.

1. Define management and explain its relevance to supply chain organisations.

Model answer: Management coordinates people and resources through planning, organising, leading and controlling to achieve objectives. Supply chains require it to align procurement, operations, logistics, finance, suppliers and customer service.

2. Distinguish efficiency from effectiveness.

Model answer: Efficiency concerns the resources used relative to outputs; effectiveness concerns achievement of intended objectives. High activity or low cost does not prove that customer or organisational objectives have been met.

3. Explain the four core management functions.

Model answer: Planning sets objectives and actions; organising allocates roles and resources; leading influences and supports people; controlling compares performance with standards and corrects deviations.

4. Compare classical and human relations management approaches.

Model answer: Classical approaches emphasise structure, task design, rules and productivity. Human relations approaches highlight motivation, groups, communication, employee needs and social conditions at work.

5. What is contingency theory?

Model answer: It argues that effective management depends on context, including uncertainty, task, technology, workforce, scale and environment; no single structure or leadership style suits every situation.

6. Differentiate strategic, tactical and operational management.

Model answer: Strategic management sets long-term direction; tactical management translates it into functional plans and budgets; operational management coordinates daily work and exceptions.

7. Define opportunity cost and give a supply chain example.

Model answer: Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone. Using limited warehouse space for one product means that space cannot be used for another product or activity.

8. State the law of demand and two factors that can shift demand.

Model answer: The law of demand generally predicts that quantity demanded falls as price rises, other things equal. Income, preferences, substitutes, complements, expectations and population can shift demand.

9. Distinguish fixed, variable and total costs.

Model answer: Fixed costs remain constant over a relevant activity range and period; variable costs change with activity; total cost is the sum of relevant cost components.

10. A product sells for KSh 1,500, has variable cost KSh 900 and fixed costs KSh 300,000. Calculate break-even units.

Model answer: Contribution per unit = 1,500 − 900 = KSh 600. Break-even quantity = 300,000/600 = 500 units.

11. Explain economies and diseconomies of scale.

Model answer: Economies of scale reduce average cost as output expands, for example by spreading fixed cost. Diseconomies arise when coordination, congestion or complexity causes average cost to rise.

12. What is price elasticity of demand?

Model answer: It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, other things equal. It indicates how sensitive demand is to price.

13. A price rises by 10% and quantity demanded falls by 15%. Calculate the price elasticity using percentage changes.

Model answer: Elasticity = −15%/10% = −1.5. The absolute value exceeds one, indicating elastic demand in this estimate.

14. Distinguish monopoly and oligopoly.

Model answer: A monopoly has one dominant supplier with substantial entry barriers; an oligopoly has a small number of influential firms whose decisions are interdependent.

15. What is a make-or-buy decision?

Model answer: It compares internal provision with external sourcing using relevant cost, capacity, quality, capability, control, risk, flexibility and transition implications.

16. Explain the purpose of a budget.

Model answer: A budget translates plans into expected financial resources and results, supports coordination and control, and helps identify funding requirements and variances.

17. Distinguish a current ratio from a quick ratio.

Model answer: The current ratio compares current assets with current liabilities. The quick ratio excludes less liquid current assets such as inventory under its conventional calculation.

18. A firm has current assets of KSh 900,000 and current liabilities of KSh 600,000. Calculate the current ratio.

Model answer: Current ratio = 900,000/600,000 = 1.5:1.

19. Explain the role of leadership in supply chain management.

Model answer: Leadership aligns people across functions and partner organisations, communicates priorities, develops capability, resolves conflict and encourages early reporting of operational risks.

20. What is psychological safety?

Model answer: It is a shared belief that people can raise questions, report mistakes and express concerns without unreasonable fear of humiliation or retaliation.

21. Distinguish distributive and integrative negotiation.

Model answer: Distributive negotiation divides a perceived fixed value; integrative negotiation explores interests and options that can create additional value for both parties.

22. Identify four elements of good corporate governance.

Model answer: Examples include board oversight, clear delegated authority, internal controls, risk management, transparency, accountability and ethical conduct.

23. What is variance analysis?

Model answer: It compares actual performance with budget or standard, investigates differences and supports corrective action. A favourable financial variance may still harm service or quality.

24. Explain the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle.

Model answer: Plan a change and expected result; Do implement or pilot it; Check evidence against expectations; Act by standardising, adapting or abandoning the change.

25. A project costs KSh 2 million and is expected to generate annual net cash savings of KSh 500,000. Calculate simple payback.

Model answer: Payback = 2,000,000/500,000 = 4 years, assuming equal annual net savings and ignoring discounting.

26. State two limitations of simple payback.

Model answer: It ignores cash flows after the payback point and does not account for the time value of money or risk differences unless separately adjusted.

27. Explain why organisational structure affects supply chain performance.

Model answer: Structure determines authority, communication, specialisation and coordination. Functional silos may slow end-to-end decisions, while poorly defined matrix roles can create conflicting priorities.

28. What is a risk register?

Model answer: A record of identified risks, causes, consequences, existing controls, likelihood or impact assessments, owners and planned responses.

29. A supplier offers a lower unit price but requires larger batches and longer lead times. What should the buyer assess?

Model answer: Assess total cost of ownership, inventory and working-capital costs, storage, obsolescence, service risk, supplier reliability, flexibility and disruption exposure.

30. Outline a defensible management recommendation.

Model answer: Define the problem and evidence, identify stakeholders and constraints, analyse causes, compare alternatives against clear criteria, recommend an option, specify implementation and controls, and define outcome measures.

Chapter summary

Management integrates planning, organising, leading and controlling to achieve organisational objectives. Management theories provide different lenses for understanding tasks, structures, people and organisational environments. Supply chain organisations need cross-functional and inter-organisational coordination, clear authority, effective leadership and continuous control.

Business economics explains scarcity, opportunity cost, demand, supply, elasticity, production, costs, market structure, money and macroeconomic conditions. Financial statements, budgets, variance analysis and investment appraisal support resource allocation. Ethical governance, negotiation, employee engagement, risk management and continuous improvement shape implementation. Managers should use evidence, state assumptions, compare alternatives and measure outcomes across cost, service, quality, resilience and sustainability.
