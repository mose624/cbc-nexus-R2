Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, learners should be able to classify inventory; explain inventory cost and service trade-offs; calculate economic order quantities, reorder points and safety stock; distinguish independent and dependent demand; interpret ABC analysis; explain MRP and ERP inventory records; evaluate warehouse and fulfilment methods; assess inventory accuracy, valuation and performance; and design risk-based, sustainable inventory policies.

Study approach: Show every calculation, define each variable and maintain consistent units and time periods. State assumptions before applying EOQ, reorder-point or safety-stock formulas. Link numerical results to practical decisions about supplier constraints, shelf life, storage, customer service and risk.

Important: Inventory formulas are decision aids, not universal rules. Validate demand and lead-time assumptions, confirm stock-record accuracy and assess the cost of both shortages and excess stock before implementing a policy.

1. Inventory management in supply chains

Inventory is a stock of materials, components, work in progress, finished goods, spare parts or supplies held to support future operations and customer demand. Inventory management determines what should be held, where it should be located, how much should be ordered or produced, when replenishment should occur and how stock should be controlled. It connects procurement, production, warehousing, transport, finance and customer service.

Inventory exists for several reasons. Cycle stock supports normal replenishment between orders; safety stock protects against uncertainty in demand and lead time; anticipation stock is accumulated before predictable peaks or planned shutdowns; pipeline stock is moving through a supply network; decoupling stock allows adjacent processes to operate with some independence; and speculative stock may be acquired in response to expected price or availability changes.

Inventory can improve product availability, production continuity and response time, but it also consumes working capital and space. It may deteriorate, become obsolete, expire, be damaged or lose value. Managers therefore seek an appropriate balance between service and total system cost rather than simply minimising stock or maximising availability without regard to cost.

Inventory decisions should be linked to the organisation's service promise and supply chain strategy. A hospital may prioritise availability of critical medicines and emergency supplies; a fashion retailer may manage short product life cycles and markdown risk; a manufacturer may protect a constrained production line from component shortages; and an online retailer may position fast-moving products close to customers.

Good inventory management depends on accurate records, disciplined processes, clear ownership, reliable suppliers, demand information and physical control. A mathematically sound reorder rule cannot compensate for inaccurate stock balances, unrecorded issues, poor receiving, uncontrolled substitutions or an unreliable lead-time assumption.

2. Inventory classifications and stock purpose

Stock should be classified according to its purpose, value, demand pattern, criticality, physical characteristics and supply risk. Raw materials are inputs awaiting processing; components are purchased or manufactured items used in assembly; work in progress is partially completed output; finished goods are ready for sale or dispatch; maintenance, repair and operations supplies support facilities; and consumables are used during normal activities.

Cycle stock is the quantity expected to be consumed during a normal replenishment cycle. Safety stock is additional inventory held to absorb uncertainty. Pipeline inventory represents items in transit or otherwise committed but not yet available for use. These categories can overlap in operational records, so definitions should be documented to prevent double counting.

Anticipation stock may be built before seasonal demand, supplier closures or known capacity constraints. Such stock can smooth production and protect customer service, but forecasts may be wrong and storage costs may rise. Managers should compare the cost of early production and holding with overtime, capacity expansion, lost sales or emergency procurement later.

Decoupling inventory separates stages so that a disruption at one workstation does not immediately stop all downstream work. It can be justified where processes have different operating rates or reliability. However, buffers can also conceal chronic imbalance, defects or equipment failure. Buffer levels should therefore have explicit purposes, limits and review triggers.

Criticality-based classification considers the consequence of stockout, not only annual consumption value. A low-cost seal, fuse or specialist spare may stop an entire plant if unavailable. A robust classification combines value, demand variability, lead time, substitutability, shelf life, safety consequences and recovery time.

3. Inventory costs and total cost of ownership

Inventory decisions involve several cost categories. Ordering costs include purchase-order processing, supplier administration, receiving and inspection. Setup costs arise when production equipment is prepared for a batch. Holding costs include capital, storage, insurance, handling, deterioration, obsolescence, shrinkage and taxes where applicable. Shortage costs include lost contribution, expediting, production stoppage, penalties and damage to customer trust.

Purchase price alone is not the total cost of acquiring and using an item. Total cost of ownership may include freight, customs, duties, inspection, quality failures, payment terms, packaging, handling, storage, maintenance, disposal and the cost of risk. A low quoted price can be outweighed by unreliable delivery, high defect rates or expensive after-sales support.

Annual holding cost is often estimated as average inventory multiplied by a holding-cost rate. The rate should reflect relevant capital and operating costs, not an arbitrary industry percentage. Different items may have different carrying costs because of temperature control, security, insurance, shelf life or special handling.

Shortage costs are difficult to measure because they include both immediate and longer-term effects. A stockout may cause a backorder, substitution, lost sale, production delay or safety risk. Managers should distinguish recoverable demand from permanently lost demand and consider whether the shortage affects a critical customer or downstream process.

Relevant-cost analysis compares the incremental costs and benefits of alternative inventory policies. Sunk costs should not drive a forward decision, while opportunity costs and capacity constraints may matter. Sensitivity analysis is useful because demand, lead time, interest rates, supplier prices and service consequences are uncertain.

4. Demand patterns, independent and dependent demand

Independent demand is driven by external customer requirements and is usually forecast at the item or product-family level. Finished products and service parts often have independent demand. Dependent demand is derived from the quantity and timing of another item, such as components required by a production schedule or ingredients required by a recipe.

Dependent demand is commonly calculated through bills of materials and material requirements planning. If one finished product requires three units of component A, a planned order for 200 finished products creates gross component demand of 600 units before considering existing stock, scheduled receipts, scrap and safety requirements.

Demand patterns may be stable, seasonal, trending, intermittent, lumpy or highly variable. A fast-moving staple may have regular daily demand, while an expensive replacement part may experience long periods of no demand followed by a large requirement. Different patterns need different forecasting, classification and replenishment methods.

Demand aggregation can improve forecast stability, but aggregated forecasts may hide local variation. A national demand forecast may be useful for total procurement while warehouse-level replenishment needs location-specific patterns. Managers should forecast at the level where decisions are made and where meaningful data are available.

Promotions, price changes, product launches, competitor activity, weather, economic conditions and customer contracts can alter demand. Historical data should therefore be interpreted alongside causal information. A forecast should state its horizon, unit of measure, assumptions and uncertainty rather than being treated as a guaranteed order quantity.

5. ABC analysis and inventory prioritisation

ABC analysis classifies inventory according to annual consumption value, commonly calculated as annual usage multiplied by unit cost. A items account for a large share of total value and receive close management attention; B items have intermediate value; C items are individually low-value and numerous. The exact proportions are organisation-specific and should be determined from actual data.

ABC classification supports differentiated control. A items may receive frequent review, accurate forecasts, tight approval controls, supplier collaboration and cycle counting. B items may use periodic review and standard replenishment. C items may be controlled through simple ordering rules, two-bin systems or economical bulk purchasing where storage and obsolescence risks are acceptable.

ABC analysis does not measure operational criticality. A cheap component may be essential to a safety system, while an expensive item may have a reliable substitute. A useful extension combines ABC with criticality, variability, lead time, shelf life and supply risk to form a multi-dimensional classification.

Classification should be recalculated when prices, usage, product mix or sourcing arrangements change. One-off unusual consumption can distort annualised value, so managers should inspect outliers and understand the underlying reason. New products with little history may require provisional classification based on engineering, customer or expert assessment.

ABC analysis is a prioritisation tool, not a reason to neglect C items. Weak control of low-value supplies can create frequent stockouts, high administrative effort or safety issues. The objective is to match the control method to the economic and operational consequences of error.

6. Economic order quantity and ordering decisions

Economic order quantity is a classical model that estimates the order size that minimises the sum of annual ordering and holding costs under specified assumptions. The basic model assumes known and constant demand, fixed ordering cost, constant unit price, instantaneous replenishment, no shortages and a stable holding-cost rate. Real operations often violate one or more assumptions.

The basic EOQ formula is Q* = square root of (2DS/H), where D is annual demand in units, S is the cost of placing one order and H is annual holding cost per unit. If holding cost is expressed as a proportion i of unit purchase price C, then H = iC. Units and time periods must be consistent.

Suppose annual demand is 12,000 units, order cost is KSh 500 per order and annual holding cost is KSh 20 per unit. EOQ = square root of (2 × 12,000 × 500 / 20) = square root of 600,000, approximately 775 units. The result is a starting point, not an automatic purchasing instruction.

At EOQ, the model balances annual ordering and cycle-stock holding costs. Expected orders per year are D/Q, and average cycle stock is approximately Q/2 when demand is steady and replenishment is instantaneous. Annual relevant cost can be estimated as (D/Q)S + (Q/2)H, excluding purchase cost where unit price is constant.

Practical order quantities may be adjusted for supplier minimums, pack sizes, truckload economics, quantity discounts, storage limits, expiry dates, cash constraints and demand uncertainty. Managers should compare feasible alternatives using total cost and service implications rather than rounding blindly to the nearest convenient number.

7. Reorder points and lead-time demand

A reorder point is the inventory position at which a replenishment order should be triggered. In a simple deterministic system, reorder point equals demand during lead time. If average daily demand is 40 units and replenishment lead time is 6 days, the reorder point is 240 units, assuming stable demand and reliable lead time.

Inventory position is not necessarily the same as physical on-hand stock. It may include on-hand inventory plus confirmed open orders minus backorders or allocations, depending on the system's definition. Reorder logic should use a consistent inventory-position calculation so that outstanding orders are not ignored or counted twice.

Lead time is the elapsed time from the relevant trigger to usable receipt. It may include internal approval, supplier processing, production, dispatch, transport, customs, receiving, inspection and put-away. A quoted supplier manufacturing time is therefore not always the end-to-end replenishment lead time.

When demand and lead time vary, the reorder point may include safety stock. A common formulation is average demand during average lead time plus safety stock. For independent normally distributed demand and lead time, more advanced formulas combine demand variance and lead-time variance, but assumptions and data quality must be checked.

Reorder points should be reviewed when demand, supplier performance, transport routes, production policy or service requirements change. An outdated lead-time master can cause systematic stockouts or excess inventory. Exception reporting should identify items whose actual replenishment times repeatedly differ from planned values.

8. Safety stock and service-level decisions

Safety stock is additional inventory held to protect against uncertainty in demand, replenishment lead time or both. It reduces the probability or impact of stockout during replenishment, but increases holding cost and may create expiry or obsolescence risk. The appropriate level depends on the cost of shortage and the desired service level.

A cycle service level is the probability of not experiencing a stockout during a replenishment cycle. A fill rate is the proportion of demand supplied immediately from available stock. These measures are different: an item may experience frequent small stockouts but still have a high fill rate, or rare stockouts that are very large.

Under a simplified model with constant lead time and normally distributed demand, safety stock can be estimated as z multiplied by the standard deviation of demand during lead time. The z value corresponds to the selected cycle service level under the normal distribution assumption. The model is not appropriate without checking distribution, independence and data stability.

For example, if the standard deviation of demand during lead time is 30 units and the selected z value is 1.65, safety stock is 49.5, approximately 50 units. If mean lead-time demand is 240 units, the reorder point becomes approximately 290 units. The service target should be justified by shortage consequences and carrying cost.

Service-level choices should be segmented. Critical clinical supplies may warrant high protection, while low-margin items with reliable substitutes may justify a lower target. Managers should measure achieved service, stockout frequency, backorders and inventory investment to ensure that the chosen policy works in practice.

9. Perpetual and periodic inventory systems

A perpetual inventory system updates stock records whenever a transaction occurs, including receipt, issue, transfer, return, adjustment and dispatch. It can provide near-real-time visibility and support automated replenishment. Its accuracy depends on disciplined scanning, timely transaction posting, correct master data and secure access.

A periodic review system checks inventory at defined intervals and places orders based on the difference between a target level and the inventory position. It may be easier to administer for low-value items or suppliers with scheduled ordering windows, but it can require more safety stock because demand accumulates between reviews.

Perpetual systems do not eliminate the need for physical verification. Errors may arise from missed scans, incorrect units, unrecorded damage, picking mistakes, theft, interface failures and duplicate transactions. Cycle counting compares selected stock records with physical quantities and investigates discrepancies.

Periodic stocktaking counts inventory at a particular date, often for financial reporting and control. It may require operational disruption or careful movement controls. Cycle counting spreads verification across the year and can focus more frequently on high-value, critical or error-prone items.

Inventory policies should define who may create or amend item records, approve adjustments, receive goods, issue stock and perform independent counts. Segregation of duties, audit trails, exception reports and controlled adjustment reasons reduce fraud and error.

10. Material requirements planning and bills of materials

Material requirements planning calculates time-phased component requirements from a master production schedule, bills of materials, inventory records and planned lead times. It converts the demand for finished products into quantities and dates for components and materials. It is particularly useful where demand is dependent on parent-item production.

A bill of materials lists the components, quantities and relationships required to make a product. It may be single-level or multi-level and should include valid alternatives, scrap factors, effectivity dates and engineering revisions where relevant. Incorrect bills of materials generate incorrect purchasing and production plans.

MRP begins with gross requirements, subtracts available inventory and scheduled receipts to determine net requirements, applies lot-sizing rules and offsets planned orders by lead time. The resulting planned order releases indicate when procurement or production should begin, subject to capacity and supplier constraints.

MRP is sensitive to data accuracy. Errors in inventory balances, lead times, order quantities, bills of materials and master schedules can produce nervous or infeasible plans. Nervousness occurs when small changes in demand or timing create repeated changes to planned orders, increasing supplier and production disruption.

MRP primarily plans materials; it does not automatically prove that labour, machines, suppliers or transport have sufficient capacity. Capacity requirements planning and supplier confirmation should be used to test whether the proposed plan can be executed. Planners should also manage frozen horizons and exception messages to limit unnecessary rescheduling.

11. Enterprise resource planning and integrated inventory data

Enterprise resource planning systems integrate business functions through shared data and transaction processes. Inventory records may connect purchasing, receiving, production, warehousing, sales, finance and maintenance. Integration can reduce duplicate entry and improve traceability, but only when data definitions and responsibilities are consistent.

Item master data typically includes item code, description, unit of measure, dimensions, weight, storage conditions, status, planning parameters, valuation method and approved supplier information. Poor naming conventions, duplicate records and inconsistent units can undermine procurement, forecasting and reporting.

Location master data identifies warehouses, zones, bins, production stores and status areas such as quarantine or inspection. Lot and serial tracking support traceability where required. Batch attributes may include manufacture date, expiry date, supplier lot, quality status and country of origin.

System implementation should define transaction ownership, validation rules, approval workflows, user roles, interfaces and reconciliation controls. Opening balances and migrated data should be cleansed and verified. Training must cover real transaction scenarios rather than only navigation through screens.

System outages require controlled continuity procedures. Organisations should know how to receive, issue and trace critical stock during downtime, how to prevent duplicate posting after restoration and how to reconcile manual records. Cybersecurity, backups, access controls and tested recovery plans are part of inventory governance.

12. Warehouse design, storage and slotting

Warehouse design determines how goods are received, inspected, stored, picked, packed, staged and dispatched. The layout should reflect product characteristics, order profiles, handling equipment, safety requirements, throughput, building constraints and expected growth. Poor design creates travel, congestion, damage and inaccurate stock records.

Storage strategies include fixed locations, random or dynamic locations, class-based storage and dedicated zones. Fixed locations can make items easy to find but may waste space when stock is low. Random storage can improve space utilisation when supported by reliable location control and scanning.

Slotting assigns products to storage locations according to velocity, size, weight, compatibility, replenishment frequency, security and picking relationships. Fast-moving items may be positioned near dispatch or ergonomic picking heights, but heavy or hazardous products require suitable locations and safe handling arrangements.

Warehouse processes should distinguish receiving, quality inspection, put-away, replenishment, picking, packing, staging and shipping. Clear status controls prevent uninspected, damaged or quarantined stock from being allocated as available. Returns should be assessed before being returned to saleable inventory.

Warehouse performance measures include inventory accuracy, dock-to-stock time, order-picking accuracy, lines picked per hour, space utilisation, damage rate, order cycle time and on-time dispatch. Productivity measures should be balanced with safety, quality and customer outcomes.

13. Picking methods and order fulfilment

Order picking retrieves the required items from storage to satisfy a customer or production request. It is often a labour-intensive warehouse activity and a major source of travel and errors. Picking method should fit order size, product range, order profile, facility layout and technology.

Discrete picking completes one order at a time. Batch picking collects items for several orders in one route and sorts them later. Zone picking assigns workers to specific warehouse zones, while wave picking releases groups of orders according to time, carrier, route or operational priority. Hybrid methods are common.

Pick-path optimisation seeks to reduce unnecessary travel while respecting location access, equipment constraints, product compatibility and replenishment activity. Shortest-distance routing is not always best if it creates congestion or delays critical orders. Slotting and order batching can have a larger effect than route algorithms alone.

Barcode scanning, voice-directed picking, pick-to-light and automated storage systems can improve speed and accuracy. Each method has investment, integration, maintenance and training requirements. Technology should be selected after measuring the current process and defining the error or productivity problem.

Order fulfilment should include verification, packaging, documentation, carrier handoff and customer notification. A correct pick can still result in a failed delivery if packaging is unsuitable, labels are inaccurate or dispatch cut-offs are missed. End-to-end performance should be measured from order acceptance to successful delivery.

14. Inventory accuracy, shrinkage and cycle counting

Inventory accuracy measures the agreement between recorded stock and physical stock. Inaccurate records create false availability, unnecessary purchases, production shortages, lost sales and unreliable financial statements. Accuracy should be measured using clear rules for quantity, location, item identity and status.

Shrinkage refers to stock loss arising from theft, damage, administrative error, supplier shortage, mis-picking or other unexplained differences. Investigation should distinguish process errors from deliberate misconduct and identify whether losses are concentrated by item, location, shift, transaction type or supplier.

Cycle counting selects items for repeated physical verification during the year. Counts may be scheduled by ABC class, criticality, movement frequency, historical error or risk. Blind counts, where the counter does not see the recorded quantity, can reduce confirmation bias, while independent recounts support material adjustments.

Root-cause investigation should examine receiving, put-away, labelling, unit conversion, picking, returns, transfers, system interfaces, access and adjustment approvals. Correcting the quantity without correcting the cause allows the discrepancy to recur. Corrective action should be assigned and effectiveness checked.

Inventory accuracy is a shared responsibility. Procurement, warehouse teams, production, sales and finance all create transactions that affect records. Clear cut-off rules, standard operating procedures and training help maintain reliable balances across organisational boundaries.

15. Perishable, hazardous and regulated inventory

Perishable inventory loses value or becomes unusable over time. Food, medicines, biological materials and temperature-sensitive products may require expiry-date control, validated storage, temperature monitoring and documented handling. First-expired-first-out is often more suitable than simple first-in-first-out where expiry dates differ.

First-in-first-out issues the oldest received stock first; first-expired-first-out issues the stock with the earliest expiry first. These methods are not identical. Batch and lot traceability help organisations identify affected stock during recalls, quality incidents or regulatory investigations.

Hazardous materials may require segregation, compatible storage, ventilation, spill containment, safety data sheets, controlled access and trained handlers. Storage and transport requirements depend on the material and applicable jurisdiction. Inventory systems should record hazards and prevent incompatible items from being allocated to the same storage area.

Regulated products may require licences, temperature records, controlled-substance registers, chain-of-custody documentation or specific disposal procedures. Organisations must identify the applicable national and sector requirements rather than assuming one country's rules apply globally.

Disposal of expired, damaged or hazardous inventory should be authorised, documented and carried out through appropriate channels. Items should be blocked from normal allocation while their status is investigated. Environmental and safety obligations continue after the item is removed from the inventory ledger.

16. Inventory valuation and financial reporting

Inventory is an asset held for sale, in production for sale or in the form of materials and supplies to be consumed in production or service delivery. Financial reporting rules define recognition, measurement, cost formulas, write-downs and disclosure. Organisations should apply the accounting framework relevant to their reporting jurisdiction.

Inventory cost may include purchase costs, conversion costs and other costs incurred to bring stock to its present location and condition. Purchase costs may include non-recoverable taxes, freight and handling, while trade discounts and rebates are generally considered in determining cost under applicable standards.

Common cost formulas include first-in-first-out and weighted average for interchangeable inventory under many accounting frameworks. Specific identification may be appropriate for items that are not ordinarily interchangeable or are segregated for specific projects. The permitted choices and detailed requirements depend on the applicable accounting standards.

Inventory is commonly assessed for net realisable value or another required recoverable amount under the relevant framework. Obsolete, damaged, slow-moving or expired items may require write-downs. A write-down should be supported by evidence such as expected selling price, completion costs, disposal costs and condition.

Operational and financial inventory records should reconcile. Differences may arise from cut-off, unposted receipts, goods in transit, ownership terms, unit conversion, consignment arrangements or valuation errors. Reconciliation should be timely and supported by auditable documentation.

17. Inventory performance measures and dashboards

Inventory turnover is commonly calculated as cost of goods sold divided by average inventory for a stated period. It indicates how often average inventory is consumed or sold, but it should be compared across similar businesses, product categories and accounting periods. A high turnover is not automatically good if it reflects repeated stockouts.

Days inventory outstanding estimates the number of days represented by average inventory and is often calculated as average inventory divided by cost of goods sold, multiplied by days in the period. The formula should use consistent valuation and time periods. Different sectors have different normal inventory profiles.

Service measures include line fill rate, order fill rate, case fill rate, on-time-in-full delivery, backorder rate and stockout frequency. Each measure captures a different aspect of availability. Dashboards should define the denominator and treatment of substitutions, partial shipments and customer-requested delays.

Planning measures include forecast accuracy, forecast bias, supplier lead-time adherence, schedule stability, order frequency and replenishment cycle time. Control measures include record accuracy, cycle-count completion, aged stock, expiry exposure, damage and adjustment rates.

Dashboards should segment results by item class, location, supplier, channel and customer where useful. Aggregate averages can hide a small number of critical failures. Exception-based reporting should identify the items and causes that require management action rather than overwhelm users with raw transaction counts.

18. Supplier collaboration and vendor-managed inventory

Supplier collaboration improves the flow of information and materials between buyers and suppliers. Shared forecasts, inventory visibility, capacity plans, delivery schedules and quality data can reduce uncertainty and improve replenishment. Collaboration requires agreed data definitions, secure access and clear commercial responsibilities.

Vendor-managed inventory is an arrangement in which a supplier monitors agreed customer inventory or consumption data and takes responsibility for proposing or executing replenishment within defined parameters. The customer retains important governance duties, including setting service expectations, validating data and monitoring performance.

Consignment inventory is stock held at the customer's site or under its control while ownership remains with the supplier until an agreed consumption or sale event. Contract terms should define title transfer, insurance, loss, obsolescence, counting, replenishment, invoicing and termination.

Collaborative planning, forecasting and replenishment can coordinate promotions, product introductions, seasonal peaks and capacity. It should not be treated as an automatic transfer of all inventory risk to a supplier. Forecast accuracy, information quality, incentives and dispute procedures influence results.

Supplier collaboration should be supported by performance reviews and risk controls. Organisations should avoid excessive dependence on one supplier or sharing commercially sensitive data without safeguards. Alternative sources and continuity arrangements remain important even in long-term partnerships.

19. Just-in-time systems and pull replenishment

Just-in-time seeks to make or replenish what is needed, when it is needed and in the quantity needed. It aims to reduce excess inventory, expose process problems and improve flow. JIT depends on reliable processes, short and predictable lead times, quality at source, supplier coordination and disciplined scheduling.

Pull systems authorise replenishment in response to actual consumption or a downstream signal. Kanban cards, containers or electronic messages communicate the item, quantity and replenishment trigger. The number of signals limits work-in-progress and helps prevent uncontrolled production.

JIT can reduce storage, handling, obsolescence and working-capital costs, but it can increase vulnerability when suppliers, transport or production processes are unreliable. The right buffer depends on disruption probability, recovery time, item criticality and the cost of holding stock.

Implementation should begin with stable processes, accurate data, supplier capability and a clear understanding of variability. Removing safety stock before addressing defects and unreliable lead times can cause shortages rather than improvement. Pilot programmes allow teams to learn and adjust replenishment rules.

Modern resilient operations often combine lean flow with selective strategic buffers. Critical components, long-lead-time imports and items with limited substitutes may justify protection. The objective is not zero inventory at any cost but the lowest sensible total system cost consistent with required service and resilience.

20. Inventory risk, resilience and continuity planning

Inventory risk includes demand uncertainty, supplier failure, transport disruption, geopolitical restrictions, cyber incidents, quality failures, natural hazards, climate events, theft, fire, contamination and regulatory change. Risk assessment should consider likelihood, impact, detectability, exposure duration and recovery options.

Critical-item analysis identifies materials whose absence would stop a service, create safety risk, breach a contract or cause disproportionate financial loss. It should consider substitutes, repair options, supplier concentration, time to qualify an alternative and the inventory needed during recovery.

Resilience options include alternate suppliers, regional sourcing, safety stock, strategic reserves, flexible specifications, substitution approvals, spare capacity, repair and refurbishment, improved visibility and tested contingency routes. Each option has cost and lead-time implications.

Business continuity plans should identify triggers, decision rights, communication channels, priority customers, allocation rules, recovery targets and escalation contacts. Plans should be exercised through realistic scenarios, including supplier outage, warehouse loss, transport blockage and systems downtime.

Inventory resilience must avoid indiscriminate stockpiling. Excess stock can expire, tie up cash and crowd out more critical materials. A segmented policy based on criticality, lead time, variability and substitutability is more defensible than a uniform increase across all items.

21. Sustainable and circular inventory management

Sustainable inventory management considers resource use, emissions, waste, packaging, product life, reverse logistics and responsible disposal. Inventory choices influence energy used in storage, transport frequency, product damage, expiry, returns and the amount of material ultimately discarded.

Better demand planning and replenishment can reduce overproduction, obsolete stock and emergency shipments. Packaging redesign, reusable containers, repairable products and returnable transport packaging may reduce waste, but require reverse-flow processes and lifecycle assessment.

Circular inventory systems keep products and materials in use through repair, refurbishment, remanufacturing, reuse and recycling. Returned goods need inspection, grading, traceability and a decision on their next destination. Reverse logistics should be designed rather than treated as an unplanned afterthought.

Environmental performance measures may include waste per unit, product recovery rate, packaging intensity, energy per stored unit, expiry write-offs and emissions from storage and transport. Measurement boundaries should be transparent, and environmental claims should be supported by credible evidence.

Procurement, product design, warehouse operations and customer service need shared responsibility for circular flows. A reusable packaging scheme, for example, requires customer participation, return incentives, cleaning, inspection, tracking and sufficient reverse transport capacity.

22. Inventory optimisation, analytics and emerging technologies

Inventory optimisation uses data and models to determine stocking levels, reorder policies, allocation and network placement across many items and locations. It can balance service, holding cost, ordering cost, supplier constraints and risk. Optimisation quality depends on accurate data and realistic assumptions.

Analytics may classify demand patterns, detect anomalies, estimate lead-time distributions, identify excess and slow-moving stock, and recommend replenishment. Machine-learning forecasts can use promotions, weather, web activity and other signals, but should be compared with simpler baselines and monitored for bias and drift.

Radio-frequency identification, barcodes, connected sensors and location technologies improve visibility. Sensors can monitor temperature, humidity, shock or location for sensitive goods. Device calibration, data integrity, connectivity, cybersecurity and exception response are necessary for trustworthy monitoring.

Automation in warehouses may include conveyors, sortation, autonomous mobile robots, automated storage and retrieval systems and robotic picking. Investment decisions should consider throughput, product mix, building constraints, maintenance, safety, integration, workforce skills and the cost of business interruption.

Artificial intelligence should support accountable decisions rather than obscure them. Planners need to understand recommendation logic, confidence, data limitations and override rules. Human review is especially important for critical supplies, unusual demand events, regulatory items and decisions with substantial financial or safety consequences.

23. International inventory, trade and logistics considerations

International inventory planning must account for longer and more variable replenishment lead times, customs clearance, port congestion, border procedures, documentation, currency movements and trade rules. A supplier's production lead time is only one component of the total time before stock is available for use.

Incoterms allocate specified responsibilities, costs and risks between seller and buyer in an international sale. They do not by themselves determine ownership transfer, payment terms, product quality or every customs obligation. Buyers should understand the selected rule, named place and version, and coordinate it with the contract and insurance.

Import planning should verify product classification, origin, licences, duties, taxes, restricted-party screening and applicable sanctions. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and product. Incorrect documentation can cause delays, penalties, seizure or unexpected landed cost.

Currency exposure affects the local-currency cost of imported inventory. Organisations may use budgeting rates, contractual currency clauses or approved hedging arrangements. Finance and procurement should coordinate to understand exposure between quotation, order, shipment and payment dates.

International sourcing decisions should compare landed cost, service, quality, lead-time variability, geopolitical exposure, sustainability and recovery options. A distant low-cost supplier may create higher total cost when transit time, inventory investment and disruption risk are considered.

24. Inventory governance, ethics and internal controls

Inventory governance establishes who owns policy, who approves purchasing and adjustments, how stock is safeguarded, and how performance and risk are reviewed. Policies should define authorisation limits, segregation of duties, supplier onboarding, receiving, issuing, returns, write-offs and disposal.

Fraud risks include fictitious receipts, false supplier invoices, diversion of stock, collusion, unauthorised adjustments, duplicate payments and manipulation of counts. Preventive controls include approved supplier records, purchase-order matching, access restrictions, independent counts and documented approval of write-offs.

Ethical inventory decisions include fair supplier treatment, avoidance of conflicts of interest, responsible handling of restricted goods, accurate reporting and protection of confidential commercial information. Staff should disclose conflicts and use formal channels to report suspected misconduct.

Audit trails should preserve who performed a transaction, when it occurred, what changed, why an adjustment was made and who approved it. Shared accounts and informal spreadsheet changes weaken accountability. Access should follow least-privilege principles and be reviewed periodically.

Governance must also address emergency purchasing. Urgent procurement may need streamlined approval, but the reason, scope, supplier choice, price, receipt and post-event review should be documented. Emergency status should not become a routine route around controls.

25. Designing an integrated inventory policy

An integrated inventory policy combines segmentation, forecast method, replenishment method, service target, order quantity, safety stock, review frequency, supplier arrangement and exception rules. It should reflect item value, demand pattern, criticality, lead time, shelf life, substitutability and supply risk.

Policy design begins by defining the service promise and measuring baseline performance. Managers then validate demand history, supplier lead times, stock records, costs and constraints. They can compare alternative policies through simulation, pilot tests or scenario analysis before changing a large item portfolio.

Different item classes may require different controls. Stable, low-value consumables may use simple two-bin replenishment; high-value predictable items may use close review and scheduled orders; intermittent critical spares may need risk-based stocking and repair options; and perishable products may need expiry-aware replenishment.

Implementation requires ownership, master-data changes, supplier communication, staff training, system configuration and monitoring. A policy should specify what happens when a supplier misses a delivery, demand spikes, a quality hold occurs or the stock record is unreliable.

Policies should be reviewed after material changes in demand, price, lead time, product design, supplier risk, service expectations or regulation. Continuous improvement uses evidence to revise assumptions and eliminate rules that no longer serve the organisation.

Applied inventory and materials management case studies

A manufacturer faces repeated component shortages

Situation

A manufacturer repeatedly stops an assembly line because one imported electronic component arrives late. Purchasing has focused on unit price and the standard supplier lead time, while production reports frequent emergency requests.

Diagnosis

The cross-functional team maps the true end-to-end lead time, analyses supplier delivery variation, checks inventory accuracy and calculates the cost of downtime, expediting and lost output. It assesses whether the component is genuinely interchangeable and how long an alternative supplier would take to qualify.

Response

The organisation establishes a risk-based safety buffer, negotiates delivery visibility and escalation commitments, qualifies a second source and improves the accuracy of planned order releases. It also investigates whether design changes could reduce dependence on the constrained component.

Monitoring and learning

The team monitors supplier on-time-in-full performance, lead-time variability, stockout hours, buffer consumption, obsolete stock and total landed cost. The buffer is reviewed after supplier reliability improves rather than left permanently unchanged.

Retailer reduces excess and obsolete stock

Situation

A retailer has high inventory value but frequent stockouts of popular items and large markdowns on slow sellers. Its central warehouse uses a single weeks-of-cover target across very different product categories.

Diagnosis

Analysts segment products by ABC value, demand variability, seasonality, lifecycle and substitutability. They compare forecast bias, store-level availability, aged stock and supplier lead times. They identify that aggregate targets hide local demand and product lifecycle differences.

Response

The retailer adopts differentiated replenishment policies, shorter review cycles for volatile products, lifecycle-based markdown triggers and store-level allocation rules. It improves product launch forecasts and coordinates promotions with supplier capacity.

Monitoring and learning

The dashboard tracks sell-through, stockout rate, aged inventory, markdown loss, forecast error, inventory turns and on-time delivery. Managers review both availability and excess so that one objective is not improved at the expense of the other.

Hospital protects critical medical supplies

Situation

A hospital experiences shortages of selected sterile supplies and medicines despite holding substantial total inventory. Some items expire while other critical products are unavailable.

Diagnosis

Clinical, pharmacy, procurement and stores teams classify items by clinical criticality, shelf life, supplier lead time, substitutability and demand uncertainty. They inspect expiry records, batch traceability, storage conditions and emergency replenishment routes.

Response

The hospital implements first-expired-first-out rotation, critical-item safety policies, approved substitution protocols, temperature monitoring and supplier escalation arrangements. It creates a controlled emergency reserve for selected items and trains staff in stock transactions.

Monitoring and learning

Measures include stockout incidents, expiry write-offs, batch traceability, storage excursions, emergency orders and service availability. Clinical governance reviews whether inventory policy protects patient care without creating unsafe overstock.

Warehouse inventory records do not match physical stock

Situation

A distribution business discovers that system stock often differs from physical counts. Employees report that receipts, returns and transfers are sometimes entered at the end of a shift or adjusted through spreadsheets.

Diagnosis

An audit traces item movements from purchase order to receiving, put-away, picking, dispatch and returns. It identifies missed scans, inconsistent units of measure, duplicate item codes and weak approval of adjustments.

Response

The company standardises transaction timing, cleans item and location masters, introduces scan validation, assigns cycle-count classes and requires independent approval for material adjustments. Staff receive practical training and supervisors review exception reports daily.

Monitoring and learning

Inventory accuracy, adjustment value, unposted transaction age, count completion and root-cause closure are reviewed. Repeated discrepancies trigger process investigation rather than repeated manual correction.

International supply disruption and landed-cost review

Situation

A distributor buys a product from a distant supplier at a low quoted price but faces port congestion, currency volatility, customs delays and expensive emergency air freight.

Diagnosis

Finance and procurement calculate total landed cost, including freight, duties, insurance, handling, financing of pipeline inventory and the cost of variability. They compare alternative origins, transport modes and local suppliers under multiple disruption scenarios.

Response

The distributor negotiates shipment visibility, evaluates a regional backup source, sets a targeted buffer for long-lead-time items and reviews contract delivery terms and trade documentation. It avoids shifting all volume without testing supplier capability and quality.

Monitoring and learning

The organisation tracks landed cost per unit, transit-time variation, customs holds, premium freight, supplier concentration and stockout exposure. Sourcing choices are reviewed against resilience and service outcomes, not quoted price alone.

University examination questions and model answers

These questions assess conceptual understanding, quantitative reasoning and application. For calculations, show the formula, substitute values, state units and interpret the result. For case questions, justify the proposed policy using service, total cost, data quality and supply risk.

1. Define inventory management and explain its strategic importance.

Model answer: Inventory management determines what stock is held, how much, where and when it is replenished, and how it is controlled. It affects customer service, production continuity, working capital, operating cost, resilience and financial reporting.

2. Distinguish cycle stock, safety stock, anticipation stock and pipeline stock.

Model answer: Cycle stock supports normal replenishment; safety stock protects against uncertainty; anticipation stock is built before expected peaks or disruptions; pipeline stock is moving through the supply network and is not yet available for use.

3. Explain four major inventory cost categories.

Model answer: Ordering or setup costs, holding costs, purchase or acquisition costs, and shortage costs. Total cost analysis should also consider quality failures, obsolescence, expediting and lost customer contribution.

4. What is the difference between independent and dependent demand?

Model answer: Independent demand comes from external requirements and is generally forecast. Dependent demand is derived from the planned production or service requirement for another item, often through a bill of materials.

5. Explain ABC analysis and one limitation.

Model answer: ABC analysis ranks items by annual consumption value to prioritise control. It can overlook low-value but operationally critical items, so it should be combined with criticality, variability and supply-risk analysis.

6. Calculate EOQ where annual demand is 10,000 units, ordering cost is KSh 400 and annual holding cost is KSh 16 per unit.

Model answer: EOQ = square root of (2DS/H) = square root of (2 × 10,000 × 400 / 16) = square root of 500,000 ≈ 707 units.

7. Using the EOQ in Question 6, estimate the number of orders per year.

Model answer: Orders per year = D/Q = 10,000/707 ≈ 14.14 orders per year, subject to practical order-size constraints.

8. Calculate annual ordering cost at EOQ for Question 6.

Model answer: Annual ordering cost = (D/Q)S = (10,000/707) × 400 ≈ KSh 5,657, using the rounded EOQ.

9. A business uses 35 units daily and lead time is 8 days. Calculate the basic reorder point.

Model answer: Reorder point = daily demand × lead time = 35 × 8 = 280 units, assuming constant demand and lead time and no safety stock.

10. If mean lead-time demand is 280 units and safety stock is 65 units, calculate the reorder point.

Model answer: Reorder point = 280 + 65 = 345 units.

11. Distinguish cycle service level and fill rate.

Model answer: Cycle service level is the probability of completing a replenishment cycle without a stockout. Fill rate is the proportion of total demand supplied immediately from available inventory.

12. Explain the role of a bill of materials in MRP.

Model answer: A bill of materials identifies the components and quantities required to make a parent item. MRP uses it to explode finished-product requirements into time-phased dependent demand.

13. State four inputs required for material requirements planning.

Model answer: Master production schedule, bill of materials, inventory status records and planned lead times. Accurate scheduled receipts and lot-sizing rules are also important.

14. Why can inaccurate inventory records undermine MRP?

Model answer: MRP nets gross requirements against available stock and scheduled receipts. Incorrect balances create false shortages or surpluses, leading to wrong order quantities and dates.

15. Distinguish perpetual and periodic inventory systems.

Model answer: Perpetual records are updated for each transaction. Periodic systems review stock at defined intervals and replenish to a target. Both require physical verification and reliable transaction controls.

16. Explain first-in-first-out and first-expired-first-out.

Model answer: FIFO issues the earliest received stock first. FEFO issues the stock with the earliest expiry date first and is often preferable where shelf life differs.

17. Calculate inventory turnover if annual cost of goods sold is KSh 24 million and average inventory is KSh 4 million.

Model answer: Inventory turnover = 24 million/4 million = 6 times per year.

18. Calculate days inventory outstanding using 365 days for the data in Question 17.

Model answer: Days inventory outstanding = 365/6 ≈ 60.8 days, assuming consistent cost and average inventory definitions.

19. What is vendor-managed inventory?

Model answer: An arrangement in which a supplier monitors agreed customer stock or consumption information and manages or recommends replenishment within defined parameters and contractual responsibilities.

20. Explain two benefits and two risks of just-in-time inventory.

Model answer: Benefits include lower holding cost and reduced waste. Risks include greater exposure to supplier disruption and less buffer against variability. JIT needs reliable processes and suppliers.

21. What is consignment inventory?

Model answer: Stock located at or controlled by the customer while ownership remains with the supplier until a contractually defined event such as consumption or sale.

22. State four controls that improve inventory accuracy.

Model answer: Timely transaction scanning, controlled item and location masters, cycle counting, segregation of duties, independent adjustment approval, audit trails and reconciliation.

23. Explain why high inventory turnover may be undesirable.

Model answer: It may reflect insufficient buffers and repeated stockouts, lost sales or production interruptions. Turnover must be considered alongside service, availability and risk.

24. What is total landed cost?

Model answer: The complete cost of obtaining goods at the required destination, potentially including purchase price, freight, insurance, duties, taxes, customs, handling, financing and relevant risk costs.

25. How should an organisation manage an item that is low in value but critical to safety?

Model answer: Classify it by criticality as well as value, confirm approved substitutes and lead times, set a risk-appropriate service policy, monitor supplier reliability and protect traceability and storage controls.

26. Explain how cycle counting differs from annual stocktaking.

Model answer: Cycle counting verifies selected items throughout the year, often prioritised by risk or value. Annual stocktaking is a broader physical count at a particular reporting date and may disrupt operations.

27. What is inventory shrinkage?

Model answer: The loss or unexplained reduction of recorded stock due to theft, damage, supplier shortage, process error, mis-picking or other causes.

28. Explain how inventory policy supports sustainability.

Model answer: It can reduce overproduction, expiry, obsolete stock, packaging waste and emergency transport, while enabling repair, reuse, refurbishment, reverse logistics and responsible disposal.

29. Why should international inventory planning include currency and customs risks?

Model answer: Exchange rates change local acquisition cost, while customs procedures, classification, duties and documentation affect landed cost and replenishment lead time. Both can affect availability and working capital.

30. Design a basic differentiated inventory policy for three items: a stable low-value consumable, a high-value predictable component and an intermittent critical spare.

Model answer: Use a simple two-bin or periodic policy for the consumable; close forecast, supplier scheduling and value controls for the high-value component; and criticality-based safety stock, alternative-source qualification and repair/substitution planning for the intermittent spare.

Chapter summary

Inventory management is a strategic coordination activity that balances availability, continuity, working capital and total cost. Stock should be classified by purpose, value, criticality, demand pattern and supply risk. ABC analysis, EOQ, reorder points and safety-stock calculations provide useful foundations, but their assumptions must be tested against actual operating conditions.

MRP translates finished-product plans into time-phased material requirements, while ERP and warehouse systems support transactions, traceability and control. Accurate records, disciplined receiving and issuing, cycle counting, suitable storage and effective fulfilment processes are essential. Sustainable and resilient policies use segmentation, supplier collaboration, analytics and selective buffers to protect service without creating unnecessary waste or stock accumulation.
