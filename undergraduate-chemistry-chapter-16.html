1. Introduction to polymer science

A polymer is a macromolecule composed of repeating structural units connected by covalent bonds. The repeat unit is derived from one or more monomers, although its exact composition may differ from the monomer after polymerisation. Polymer science connects organic chemistry, physical chemistry, materials engineering and biology. The properties of a polymer depend not only on chemical composition but also on molar-mass distribution, chain architecture, stereochemistry, crystallinity, morphology, additives and processing history. A molecular description must therefore be linked to structure across several length scales.

2. Monomers, repeat units and degree of polymerisation

A monomer is a molecule capable of forming covalent links in a polymerisation reaction. The repeat unit is the smallest constitutional unit whose repetition describes the chain structure. Degree of polymerisation is the number of repeat units in a chain, often estimated from molar mass divided by repeat-unit molar mass. End groups, branching and copolymer composition can make this estimate approximate. Correct repeat-unit drawing is essential for calculating theoretical molar mass and interpreting polymer names.

3. Classification of polymers

Polymers can be classified by origin as natural, modified natural or synthetic; by thermal response as thermoplastics, thermosets or elastomers; by chain architecture as linear, branched, crosslinked or network; and by polymerisation mechanism as chain-growth or step-growth. These categories describe different dimensions and should not be treated as mutually exclusive. For example, a synthetic polymer may be a linear thermoplastic made by chain-growth polymerisation.

4. Addition and condensation terminology

Addition polymerisation traditionally describes joining unsaturated monomers without elimination of a small molecule, as in polyethylene formation. Condensation polymerisation describes reactions in which functional groups combine with elimination of a small molecule such as water or methanol, as in some polyesters. Modern mechanistic classification distinguishes chain-growth and step-growth processes more precisely. Not every step-growth reaction releases a small molecule, and not every chain-growth process involves a carbon–carbon double bond.

5. Chain-growth polymerisation: overview

In chain-growth polymerisation, monomer adds to an active chain end. The process is commonly divided into initiation, propagation and termination or chain transfer. The active centre may be a radical, carbocation, carbanion or coordination complex. A relatively small number of growing chains can consume large quantities of monomer, so high molar mass may develop early in the reaction. Initiator efficiency, propagation rate and termination pathways control the resulting molar-mass distribution.

6. Free-radical polymerisation

Free-radical polymerisation uses radicals generated by thermal, photochemical or redox initiators. Initiation creates a radical that adds to a vinyl monomer; propagation repeats this addition; termination occurs by combination or disproportionation. Chain transfer can move the radical to another molecule and reduce chain length. Oxygen often inhibits radical polymerisation by trapping carbon-centred radicals. Reaction temperature, initiator concentration and monomer purity influence rate, conversion and polymer architecture.

7. Radical initiators and kinetics

Common radical initiators include organic peroxides and azo compounds that decompose to radicals. For a simplified steady-state system, the rate of initiation is related to initiator decomposition rate and efficiency, while termination depends on the square of radical concentration. Under standard simplifying assumptions, polymerisation rate varies approximately with monomer concentration and the square root of initiator concentration. Real systems may deviate because of viscosity, diffusion limitations and changing termination rates.

8. Ionic polymerisation

Cationic polymerisation proceeds through carbocationic chain ends and is favoured by monomers that stabilise positive charge, such as certain vinyl ethers and substituted alkenes. Anionic polymerisation involves carbanionic chain ends and is suited to monomers that stabilise negative charge, including styrenic and conjugated monomers under suitable conditions. Water and other protic impurities can terminate living ionic chains. Counterion, solvent polarity, temperature and monomer structure strongly affect selectivity.

9. Living and controlled polymerisation

Living polymerisation ideally proceeds without irreversible termination or chain transfer, allowing predictable chain extension and block copolymer synthesis. Controlled radical methods such as ATRP, RAFT and nitroxide-mediated polymerisation regulate radical concentration or exchange between active and dormant chains. These methods can narrow dispersity and control architecture, but are not perfectly living in every system. Residual catalyst, chain-end fidelity, conversion and side reactions must be evaluated.

10. Coordination polymerisation and Ziegler–Natta catalysts

Coordination polymerisation uses a transition-metal centre to bind and insert monomer into a metal–carbon bond. Ziegler–Natta catalysts enabled industrial production of polyethylene and stereoregular polypropylene. Metallocene and other single-site catalysts provide additional control over comonomer incorporation, molecular weight and tacticity. Catalyst composition and support influence active-site structure, productivity and polymer microstructure.

11. Ring-opening polymerisation

Ring-opening polymerisation converts cyclic monomers into polymer chains by opening a ring, often relieving ring strain or exploiting favourable thermodynamics. Lactones can form polyesters, lactams can form polyamides, and cyclic ethers can form polyethers. Mechanisms may be anionic, cationic, coordination-insertion or enzymatic. Water, alcohols and other nucleophiles can act as initiators or chain-transfer agents and influence molar mass and end-group chemistry.

12. Step-growth polymerisation

Step-growth polymerisation involves reactions between functional groups on monomers, oligomers and growing chains. Any two suitably reactive species may combine, so high molar mass generally requires very high functional-group conversion and near-stoichiometric balance. Polyesters, polyamides, polyurethanes and many networks are made by step-growth routes. Reaction engineering must manage viscosity, removal of by-products where relevant, heat transfer and functional-group stoichiometry.

13. Carothers equation and conversion

For an ideal linear step-growth polymerisation of balanced bifunctional monomers, the number-average degree of polymerisation is related to conversion p by Xn=1/(1−p). At 99% conversion, the ideal Xn is 100. This relation assumes equal reactivity of functional groups, no cyclisation, exact stoichiometric balance and no side reactions. A small stoichiometric imbalance sharply limits achievable chain length even at high conversion.

14. Polyesters and polycondensation

Polyesters contain ester linkages in the main chain. They may be prepared by condensation of diols with diacids or by transesterification of suitable derivatives. Poly(ethylene terephthalate), PET, is a major packaging and fibre polymer. Molecular weight depends on conversion, stoichiometry, catalyst and removal of volatile by-products. Hydrolysis and glycolysis can depolymerise some polyesters, creating opportunities and challenges for chemical recycling.

15. Polyamides and nylon chemistry

Hydrogen bonding between amide groups contributes to strength, melting behaviour and moisture sensitivity. Chain regularity and crystallinity influence fibre performance and processing temperatures.

16. Polyurethanes and polyureas

Polyurethanes form through reaction of isocyanate groups with hydroxyl groups, producing urethane linkages. Polyureas form through reaction of isocyanates with amines. Formulations can produce flexible foams, rigid insulation, elastomers, coatings and adhesives. Isocyanate functionality, polyol structure, water content, catalysts and blowing agents control network formation and cellular morphology. 17. Epoxy resins and thermosetting networks

Epoxy resins contain reactive epoxide groups that crosslink with curing agents such as amines or anhydrides. Curing creates a three-dimensional network that cannot simply melt and flow like a thermoplastic. Network density influences glass-transition temperature, modulus, solvent resistance and brittleness. Cure schedules, stoichiometry, fillers and thermal history affect residual stress and final performance. Recycling thermosets is challenging because covalent networks resist remelting.

18. Crosslinking and network formation

Crosslinking joins polymer chains into a network through covalent bonds, ionic interactions or other junctions. Chemical crosslinks can improve dimensional stability, solvent resistance and elastic recovery, but excessive crosslink density may reduce extensibility and toughness. Gelation occurs when a system forms a sample-spanning network. Rubber vulcanisation is a classic example in which sulfur-based crosslinks improve elasticity and durability.

19. Copolymer architecture

Copolymers contain two or more chemically distinct repeat units. Architectures include random, statistical, alternating, block and graft copolymers. Sequence distribution affects phase behaviour, crystallinity, glass transition, solubility and mechanical response. Block copolymers can self-assemble into nanoscale domains because chemically distinct blocks may be incompatible while remaining covalently linked. Feed composition does not always equal instantaneous copolymer composition because monomer reactivity ratios differ.

20. Polymer stereochemistry and tacticity

Tacticity describes the stereochemical arrangement of pendant groups along a polymer chain. Isotactic polymers have a consistent stereochemical orientation, syndiotactic polymers alternate, and atactic polymers have irregular placement. Tacticity can strongly influence crystallisation, melting temperature, modulus and solvent resistance. Stereospecific coordination catalysts allow production of highly isotactic polypropylene, illustrating how catalyst design controls properties at the molecular level.

21. Cis–trans structure and diene polymers

Conjugated diene polymerisation can produce different microstructures, including cis-1,4, trans-1,4 and 1,2-addition units. Natural rubber is primarily cis-1,4-polyisoprene, whose chain geometry supports flexible, entangled coils. Gutta-percha is rich in trans-1,4-polyisoprene and is more crystalline and rigid. Microstructure is controlled by catalyst, solvent, temperature and reaction conditions.

22. Molar mass and molecular-weight distributions

Synthetic polymers usually contain chains of different lengths, so a single molar mass is insufficient. Number-average molar mass Mn weights each molecule equally, while weight-average molar mass Mw weights chains according to their mass. Dispersity Đ=Mw/Mn describes breadth of the distribution and is at least one. Measurement methods include size-exclusion chromatography, osmometry, light scattering and end-group analysis, each with assumptions and limitations.

23. Size-exclusion chromatography

Size-exclusion chromatography separates polymer molecules by hydrodynamic size rather than direct chemical affinity. Large coils are excluded from more pores and elute earlier; smaller molecules enter more pores and elute later. Calibration with standards gives apparent molar masses, but polymer conformation and chemistry affect hydrodynamic volume. Universal calibration or light-scattering detection can improve interpretation. Aggregation and column interactions can distort results.

24. Polymer chain conformation and statistics

In dilute solution, polymer chains adopt conformations governed by bond rotations, excluded volume, solvent quality and intramolecular interactions. Ideal-chain models include freely jointed and freely rotating chains. Real chains show persistence length and characteristic ratios. The radius of gyration and end-to-end distance describe coil dimensions. Scaling relationships connect chain size to molar mass and solvent conditions, supporting interpretation of scattering and viscosity measurements.

25. Glass transition temperature

The glass transition temperature Tg marks a change in segmental mobility in amorphous polymer regions. Below Tg, the material is typically glassy and relatively rigid; above Tg, segmental motion increases and the material becomes softer or rubbery, depending on architecture and crystallinity. Tg is not a first-order melting transition and depends on measurement timescale, heating rate, plasticisers, moisture and thermal history.

26. Melting temperature and crystallinity

Semicrystalline polymers contain ordered crystallites embedded in amorphous regions. Melting temperature Tm reflects loss of crystalline order, whereas Tg concerns amorphous segmental mobility. Crystallinity depends on chain regularity, tacticity, cooling rate, nucleating agents and processing. Differential scanning calorimetry measures thermal transitions, while X-ray diffraction provides information about crystalline structure. Many polymers show broad melting endotherms because crystallites vary in size and perfection.

27. Polymer morphology and lamellar structure

Semicrystalline polymers often form folded-chain lamellae that assemble into larger spherulitic structures. The crystalline and amorphous phases jointly determine mechanical and optical properties. Cooling rate and flow during processing can create orientation and skin–core morphology. Defects, tie molecules and entanglements connect crystallites and influence toughness. Morphology is therefore a bridge between molecular structure and bulk performance.

28. Viscoelasticity and time-dependent response

Polymers display both elastic energy storage and viscous energy dissipation. Under constant stress, creep describes increasing strain with time; under constant strain, stress relaxation describes decreasing stress. Dynamic mechanical analysis measures storage modulus, loss modulus and damping as functions of temperature and frequency. Time–temperature superposition can extend measured behaviour across timescales when the material is thermorheologically simple.

29. Rubber elasticity and elastomers

Elastomers are lightly crosslinked or physically networked polymers that can undergo large reversible deformation. Their restoring force is largely entropic: stretching reduces the number of accessible chain conformations, and relaxation increases conformational entropy. The ideal rubber model links stress to absolute temperature and network-chain density. Real elastomers show viscoelasticity, strain-induced crystallisation, filler effects and damage at large deformation.

30. Mechanical properties and testing

Tensile testing measures stress–strain behaviour, including modulus, yield stress, tensile strength and elongation at break. Impact tests assess resistance to rapid fracture, while hardness tests probe local indentation response. Results depend on specimen geometry, conditioning, strain rate, temperature and standards used. Polymer properties are sensitive to processing history, orientation, moisture and ageing, so test conditions must accompany reported values.

31. Fracture, toughness and fatigue

Strength is not the same as toughness: toughness concerns energy absorbed before fracture. Crack propagation depends on defects, molecular entanglement, crystallinity, crosslink density and environmental conditions. Repeated cyclic loading can cause fatigue even below the monotonic tensile strength. Toughening strategies include rubber particles, controlled phase separation, fibre reinforcement and molecular architectures that promote energy dissipation.

32. Polymer solutions and solvent quality

Polymer solubility depends on chemical similarity, cohesive-energy interactions, temperature, molar mass and crystallinity. The Flory–Huggins model describes mixing using entropic and enthalpic contributions, with the interaction parameter χ representing polymer–solvent compatibility. High-molar-mass polymers have a small combinatorial entropy of mixing, so modest energetic penalties can prevent dissolution. Swelling of crosslinked networks differs from dissolution because chains remain connected.

33. Osmotic pressure and dilute-solution properties

Osmotic pressure arises from solvent chemical-potential differences across a semipermeable membrane. For sufficiently dilute polymer solutions, osmotic pressure can be expanded in concentration terms, and the leading term can be used to estimate number-average molar mass. Intrinsic viscosity reflects hydrodynamic volume per unit mass and depends on polymer–solvent interactions and temperature. These measurements complement chromatographic molar-mass analysis.

34. Polymer processing: melt extrusion

Extrusion forces polymer melt through a shaped die using a rotating screw. It is used to make films, pipes, fibres, profiles and compounding pellets. Screw design controls conveying, melting, mixing and pressure development. Temperature, shear rate, residence time and degradation stability must be balanced. Melt fracture, die swell, surging and nonuniform mixing are common processing issues linked to rheology and equipment design.

35. Injection moulding

Injection moulding melts polymer pellets, injects the melt into a cooled mould, holds pressure during packing, and ejects the solidified part. Cycle time, mould temperature, injection speed and packing pressure influence shrinkage, warpage, weld lines and residual stress. Semi-crystalline polymers undergo crystallisation during cooling, which can increase shrinkage. Tool design and gate location influence flow orientation and mechanical anisotropy.
