Chapter 1 — Foundations: Logic, Sets, Relations and Proof

Learning outcomes

Formalise mathematical statements; distinguish implication from equivalence; manipulate predicates and quantifiers; prove set identities; classify relations; construct equivalence classes and quotient sets; analyse injective, surjective and bijective functions; and write direct, contrapositive, contradiction and induction proofs.

Study method: Reproduce definitions and proofs without looking. Attempt each problem before opening its individual worked solution. Numerical examples alone do not establish a universal claim.

1.1 Mathematical statements and propositional logic

A proposition is a declarative statement with exactly one truth value, true or false. A sentence with an unassigned variable, such as \(x^2=4\), is an open sentence rather than a proposition until a value or quantifier is supplied.

Logical connectives. For propositions \(P,Q\): negation \(\neg P\); conjunction \(P\land Q\); disjunction \(P\lor Q\); implication \(P\Rightarrow Q\); equivalence \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\).

The implication \(P\Rightarrow Q\) is false only when \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false. Its converse is \(Q\Rightarrow P\); its inverse is \(\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q\); its contrapositive is \(\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P\). An implication is equivalent to its contrapositive, but not generally to its converse.

\[P\Rightarrow Q\equiv\neg P\lor Q,\qquad P\Leftrightarrow Q\equiv(P\Rightarrow Q)\land(Q\Rightarrow P).\]

A tautology is true for every truth assignment; a contradiction is always false. De Morgan's laws are

\[\neg(P\land Q)\equiv(\neg P\lor\neg Q),\qquad\neg(P\lor Q)\equiv(\neg P\land\neg Q).\]

For example, “if an integer \(n\) is divisible by 6, then it is divisible by 3” is true. Its converse is false, since 3 is divisible by 3 but not by 6.

1.2 Predicates, domains and quantifiers

A predicate \(P(x)\) has a truth value depending on \(x\); its domain must be stated. The universal quantifier \(\forall\) means “for every”; the existential quantifier \(\exists\) means “there exists”.

Negation rules. \[\neg(\forall x\in A\,P(x))\equiv\exists x\in A\,\neg P(x),\qquad\neg(\exists x\in A\,P(x))\equiv\forall x\in A\,\neg P(x).\]

Quantifier order matters. Over \(\mathbb R\), \(\forall x\,\exists y\,(y>x)\) is true, but \(\exists y\,\forall x\,(y>x)\) is false. In the first statement, \(y\) may depend on \(x\); in the second, one fixed real number must exceed every real number.

  • To prove a universal statement, take an arbitrary element of the domain.
  • To disprove a universal statement, provide one counterexample.
  • To prove an existential statement, exhibit and verify a witness.
  • To disprove an existential statement, show every candidate fails.

1.3 Sets, operations and algebra of sets

A set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects. \(x\in A\) denotes membership; \(A\subseteq B\) means every element of \(A\) belongs to \(B\). The empty set is \(\varnothing\). The power set \(\mathcal P(A)\) consists of all subsets of \(A\); if \(|A|=n\), then \(|\mathcal P(A)|=2^n\).

Union: \(A\cup B=\{x:x\in A\text{ or }x\in B\}\). Intersection: \(A\cap B=\{x:x\in A\text{ and }x\in B\}\). Difference: \(A\setminus B=\{x\in A:x\notin B\}\). For universal set \(U\), \(A^c=U\setminus A\).
Distributivity and De Morgan: \[A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C),\qquad(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c.\]

Proof by element method: To prove \(X=Y\), establish both inclusions. If \(x\in A\cap(B\cup C)\), then \(x\in A\) and either \(x\in B\) or \(x\in C\); hence \(x\in(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)\). Conversely, membership in the right-hand side implies \(x\in A\) and \(x\in B\cup C\), giving the left-hand side. Thus equality holds. For finite sets, \(|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|\).

1.4 Cartesian products, relations and equivalence classes

The Cartesian product \(A\times B=\{(a,b):a\in A,b\in B\}\). A binary relation from \(A\) to \(B\) is a subset of \(A\times B\).

Properties of a relation \(R\) on \(A\): reflexive if \(aRa\) for every \(a\); symmetric if \(aRb\Rightarrow bRa\); antisymmetric if \(aRb\) and \(bRa\Rightarrow a=b\); transitive if \(aRb\) and \(bRc\Rightarrow aRc\).

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Its equivalence class is \([a]=\{x\in A:xRa\}\). Equivalence classes partition \(A\): each element lies in one class, and two classes are equal or disjoint. A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive.

Example: On \(\mathbb Z\), define \(a\sim b\) iff \(5\mid(a-b)\). This is an equivalence relation with five classes, represented by \([0],[1],[2],[3],[4]\) modulo 5.

1.5 Functions, images and inverse mappings

A function \(f:A\to B\) assigns each element of \(A\) exactly one element of \(B\). The domain is \(A\), codomain \(B\), and image \(f(A)=\{f(a):a\in A\}\subseteq B\).

Injective: \(f(a_1)=f(a_2)\Rightarrow a_1=a_2\). Surjective: for every \(b\in B\), some \(a\in A\) satisfies \(f(a)=b\). Bijective: both injective and surjective.

A function has an inverse \(f^{-1}:B\to A\) exactly when it is bijective. Composition is \((g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))\), when the domains and codomains match; composition is not generally commutative.

1.6 Proof methods and mathematical induction

Direct proof

Assume the hypotheses and derive the conclusion from definitions and established results. If \(a=2r\) and \(b=2s\), then \(a+b=2(r+s)\), so the sum of two even integers is even.

Contrapositive and contradiction

To prove \(P\Rightarrow Q\), one may prove the equivalent \(\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P\). For contradiction, assume the desired conclusion is false and derive an impossibility. For example, assuming \(\sqrt2=p/q\) in lowest terms gives \(p^2=2q^2\); hence \(p\) is even, and substitution forces \(q\) even, a contradiction.

Mathematical induction

For \(P(n)\) on integers \(n\ge n_0\), prove (i) \(P(n_0)\), and (ii) for arbitrary \(k\ge n_0\), \(P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)\). Then \(P(n)\) holds for all \(n\ge n_0\).

Example: \(1+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2\). The base case \(n=1\) holds. Assuming the identity for \(k\), add \(k+1\): \(k(k+1)/2+(k+1)=(k+1)(k+2)/2\), establishing the next case. Strong induction assumes all cases from \(n_0\) through \(k\) to prove \(k+1\); it is useful when a step depends on multiple earlier cases.

Question 1 — Quantifier negation and logical equivalence

Let \(x,y\in\mathbb R\). (a) Negate \(\forall x\in\mathbb R\,\exists y\in\mathbb R\;(y^2=x^2+1)\). (b) Determine whether the original is true. (c) Explain why reversing the quantifiers changes the assertion.

8 marks
Show worked answer

(a) \(\exists x\in\mathbb R\,\forall y\in\mathbb R:\ y^2\ne x^2+1\).

(b) It is true: for arbitrary real \(x\), choose \(y=\sqrt{x^2+1}\). Since \(x^2+1\ge1\), this is real and satisfies the equation.

(c) The reversed assertion requires one fixed \(y\) to work for every \(x\). Taking \(x=0\) forces \(y^2=1\), while \(x=1\) forces \(y^2=2\); impossible.

Question 2 — Set identity by double inclusion

For arbitrary \(A,B,C\subseteq U\), prove \(A\setminus(B\cap C)=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\setminus C)\). Establish both inclusions and identify the logical law used.

8 marks
Show worked answer

If \(x\in A\setminus(B\cap C)\), then \(x\in A\) and \(x\notin B\cap C\). De Morgan gives \(x\notin B\) or \(x\notin C\), so \(x\in(A\setminus B)\cup(A\setminus C)\).

Conversely, if \(x\) belongs to the right-hand side, then \(x\in A\) and at least one of \(x\notin B\), \(x\notin C\) holds. Thus \(x\notin B\cap C\), proving membership in the left-hand side. Both inclusions give equality.

Question 3 — Cardinality and power sets

Let \(A\) be finite with \(|A|=n\ge1\), and fix \(a\in A\). Prove that exactly \(2^{n-1}\) subsets of \(A\) contain \(a\). Deduce the number that do not contain \(a\).

7 marks
Show worked answer

Every subset containing \(a\) has a unique representation \(\{a\}\cup S\), where \(S\subseteq A\setminus\{a\}\). This is a bijection with \(\mathcal P(A\setminus\{a\})\), which has \(2^{n-1}\) members. Subsets not containing \(a\) are exactly the subsets of \(A\setminus\{a\}\), so there are also \(2^{n-1}\). These two families partition \(\mathcal P(A)\).

Question 4 — Equivalence relation and quotient set

On \(\mathbb Z\), define \(a\sim b\) iff \(a-b\in6\mathbb Z\). Prove that this is an equivalence relation, list its distinct classes and show \([14]=[2]\).

10 marks
Show worked answer

Reflexive: \(a-a=0=6(0)\). Symmetric: \(a-b=6k\) implies \(b-a=6(-k)\). Transitive: \(a-b=6k\) and \(b-c=6m\) imply \(a-c=6(k+m)\). Thus all three properties hold.

The six classes are \([0],[1],[2],[3],[4],[5]\), with \([r]=\{6k+r:k\in\mathbb Z\}\). Since \(14-2=12=6(2)\), \(14\sim2\), hence \([14]=[2]\).

Question 5 — Partial order on a power set

Let \(S=\{1,2,3\}\) and order \(\mathcal P(S)\) by inclusion. Prove inclusion is a partial order; identify its least and greatest elements; give an incomparable pair and explain why this is not a total order.

9 marks
Show worked answer

Reflexivity follows from \(X\subseteq X\). Antisymmetry follows because \(X\subseteq Y\) and \(Y\subseteq X\) imply the sets have identical elements. Transitivity follows because \(X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z\) implies \(X\subseteq Z\). The least element is \(\varnothing\), and the greatest is \(S\). The sets \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) are incomparable, so not every pair is comparable; the order is not total.

Question 6 — Injectivity, surjectivity and inverse

For \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), \(f(x)=x^3-3x\), determine whether \(f\) is injective and surjective. Does \(f^{-1}:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) exist? Justify without relying solely on a graph.

10 marks
Show worked answer

It is not injective: \(f(0)=0=f(\sqrt3)\), although \(0\ne\sqrt3\). It is surjective: for arbitrary \(y\in\mathbb R\), set \(g(x)=x^3-3x-y\). This polynomial is continuous, tends to \(-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\) and \(+\infty\) as \(x\to+\infty\). The intermediate value theorem gives a real root, so \(f(x)=y\). No inverse on all of \(\mathbb R\) exists because \(f\) is not injective.

Question 7 — Contrapositive proof and parity

Prove that if \(n^2\) is odd for an integer \(n\), then \(n\) is odd. Use the contrapositive and identify where integrality is used.

6 marks
Show worked answer

Prove the contrapositive: if \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even. Since \(n\in\mathbb Z\), write \(n=2k\) for \(k\in\mathbb Z\). Then \(n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)\), which is even. The contrapositive is equivalent to the required implication. Integrality permits the even representation \(n=2k\).

Question 8 — Irrationality by contradiction

Prove \(\sqrt3\) is irrational. State the lowest-terms assumption and justify why \(3\mid p^2\) implies \(3\mid p\).

8 marks
Show worked answer

Assume \(\sqrt3=p/q\), with integers \(p,q\), \(q\ne0\), and \(\gcd(p,q)=1\). Squaring gives \(p^2=3q^2\), so \(3\mid p^2\). Since 3 is prime, Euclid's lemma gives \(3\mid p\); write \(p=3r\). Substitution gives \(9r^2=3q^2\), hence \(q^2=3r^2\) and \(3\mid q\). This contradicts \(\gcd(p,q)=1\). Therefore \(\sqrt3\) is irrational.

Question 9 — Induction and a summation identity

Prove for every integer \(n\ge1\) that \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k(k+1)=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}3\). Explicitly use the induction hypothesis.

8 marks
Show worked answer

Base case \(n=1\): both sides equal 2. Assume \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k(k+1)=n(n+1)(n+2)/3\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k(k+1)&=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}3+(n+1)(n+2)\\&=(n+1)(n+2)\left(\frac n3+1\right)\\&=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}3.\end{aligned}\]

This is the formula for \(n+1\). Induction completes the proof.

Question 10 — Characterisation of injective mappings

Let \(f:A\to B\). Prove that \(f\) is injective iff for all \(X,Y\subseteq A\), \(f(X\cap Y)=f(X)\cap f(Y)\).

12 marks
Show worked answer

Forward direction: If \(z\in f(X\cap Y)\), then \(z=f(a)\) for \(a\in X\cap Y\), so \(z\in f(X)\cap f(Y)\). Conversely, if \(z\in f(X)\cap f(Y)\), then \(z=f(x)=f(y)\) for some \(x\in X,y\in Y\). Injectivity gives \(x=y\), hence \(z\in f(X\cap Y)\).

Reverse direction: Suppose the identity holds for all subsets. If \(f(a)=f(b)=z\), choose \(X=\{a\}\), \(Y=\{b\}\). Then \(z\in f(X)\cap f(Y)=f(X\cap Y)\), so \(X\cap Y\ne\varnothing\). Therefore \(a=b\), proving injectivity.

Chapter 2 — Calculus I: Limits and Single-Variable Differentiation

Undergraduate · Chapter 2 This unit develops the limit as a mathematical object, continuity, differentiability, the derivative as a linear approximation, and the principal theorems governing differentiable functions.

2.1 The limit of a function

Let f be defined on a deleted neighbourhood of a∈ℝ. We say limx→af(x)=L if for every ε>0 there exists δ>0 such that 0<|x−a|<δ implies |f(x)−L|<ε. The value f(a), if defined, is irrelevant to the limit. Limits are unique: if a function had two distinct limits L and M, disjoint sufficiently small neighbourhoods around L and M would both have to contain all sufficiently near function values, a contradiction.

One-sided limits. The right-hand limit x→a⁺ restricts x>a; the left-hand limit x→a⁻ restricts x<a. A two-sided finite limit exists precisely when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.

Limit laws (sum, product, quotient with nonzero limiting denominator) follow from the ε–δ definition. For rational functions, cancellation may simplify a punctured-neighbourhood expression but does not change the original function's domain.

2.2 Continuity

f is continuous at a if f(a) exists, limx→af(x) exists, and the limit equals f(a). Equivalently, for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that |x−a|<δ implies |f(x)−f(a)|<ε. Sums and products of continuous functions are continuous; a quotient is continuous where its denominator is nonzero; compositions preserve continuity.

Intermediate Value Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], then every value between f(a) and f(b) is attained at some c∈[a,b]. In particular, if f(a)f(b)<0, f has a zero in (a,b).

2.3 Derivative and differentiability

The derivative at a is the limit of the difference quotient, when it exists:

\[f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}.\]

Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability. For example, |x| is continuous at 0 but its left and right derivatives are −1 and +1.

Core differentiation rules. \((cf)'=cf'\), \((f+g)'=f'+g'\), \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\), and \((f/g)'=(f'g-fg')/g^2\) where g≠0. The chain rule gives \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)\).

For n∈ℤ, the power rule gives (xⁿ)'=nxⁿ⁻¹ on the domain where the expression is differentiable. Exponential and logarithmic derivatives include (eˣ)'=eˣ and (ln x)'=1/x for x>0. Trigonometric derivatives are (sin x)'=cos x and (cos x)'=−sin x.

2.4 Mean value theorems and consequences

Rolle's theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and f(a)=f(b), then some c∈(a,b) satisfies f'(c)=0.
Mean Value Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), there exists c∈(a,b) such that \[f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.\]

If f′(x)≥0 throughout an interval, the MVT implies f is nondecreasing there. If f′=0 throughout an interval, f is constant. The theorem also gives the standard uniqueness argument: if two differentiable functions have the same derivative on an interval, their difference is constant.

2.5 Taylor approximation and error

If f has sufficiently many derivatives near a, its degree-n Taylor polynomial is \(T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!\). Under the hypotheses of Taylor's theorem, the remainder has Lagrange form \(R_n(x)=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{n+1}/(n+1)!\) for some ξ between a and x. A polynomial approximation is useful only when its remainder is controlled.

Question 1 — Epsilon–delta proof of a limit

Using the formal ε–δ definition, prove that limx→2(3x−1)=5.

8 marks
Show worked answer

Let ε>0. Choose δ=ε/3. If 0<|x−2|<δ, then |(3x−1)−5|=3|x−2|<3δ=ε. Thus the definition is satisfied and the limit is 5.

Question 2 — Removable discontinuity

Define f(x)=(x²−9)/(x−3) for x≠3 and f(3)=k. Determine the value of k that makes f continuous at 3, and prove continuity there using the limit definition.

8 marks
Show worked answer

For x≠3, f(x)=x+3, so limx→3f(x)=6. Continuity requires k=6. With k=6, |f(x)−f(3)|=|(x+3)−6|=|x−3|. Given ε>0 choose δ=ε; then |x−3|<δ implies |f(x)−f(3)|<ε. Hence f is continuous at 3 exactly when k=6.

Question 3 — Derivative from first principles

For f(x)=x²−4x, calculate f′(a) directly from the difference quotient. Hence determine all stationary points and classify them.

10 marks
Show worked answer

\[f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(a+h)^2-4(a+h)-(a^2-4a)}h=\lim_{h\to0}(2a+h-4)=2a-4.\] Thus f′(x)=2x−4, which vanishes at x=2. Since f(x)=(x−2)²−4, the stationary point is a global minimum at (2,−4).

Question 4 — Differentiability versus continuity

Let f(x)=|x−1|. Prove that f is continuous at x=1 but not differentiable there. Use one-sided difference quotients.

8 marks
Show worked answer

Continuity follows from |f(x)−f(1)|=|x−1|→0. For h>0, [f(1+h)−f(1)]/h=h/h=1. For h<0, the quotient is (−h)/h=−1. Since the one-sided limits differ, f′(1) does not exist, despite continuity.

Question 5 — Chain rule with nested functions

Differentiate \(F(x)=\ln(1+e^{\sin(x^2)})\), stating the intermediate functions and their domains.

8 marks
Show worked answer

Set u=x², v=sin u, w=eᵛ and F=ln(1+w). By the chain rule, F′(x)=[1/(1+eᵛ)]·eᵛ·cos(u)·2x. Therefore \[F'(x)=\frac{2xe^{\sin(x^2)}\cos(x^2)}{1+e^{\sin(x^2)}}.\] The logarithm's argument is strictly positive for all real x.

Question 6 — Product and quotient rules

For x>0, differentiate \(g(x)=x^2\ln x/(1+x)\) and simplify your answer.

8 marks
Show worked answer

Let u=x²ln x and v=1+x. Then u′=2xln x+x and v′=1. The quotient rule gives \[g'(x)=\frac{(2x\ln x+x)(1+x)-x^2\ln x}{(1+x)^2}=\frac{x[(x+2)\ln x+x+1]}{(1+x)^2}.\]

Question 7 — Mean Value Theorem application

Verify the hypotheses of the Mean Value Theorem for f(x)=x² on [1,3], find all permissible c, and interpret the result geometrically.

7 marks
Show worked answer

f is a polynomial, hence continuous on [1,3] and differentiable on (1,3). The theorem gives f′(c)=(9−1)/(3−1)=4. Since f′(c)=2c, c=2, which lies in (1,3). The tangent slope at x=2 equals the secant slope joining (1,1) and (3,9).

Question 8 — Monotonicity and uniqueness of a root

Show that the equation x³+x−1=0 has exactly one real root. You may use the Intermediate Value Theorem and a derivative argument.

10 marks
Show worked answer

Let f(x)=x³+x−1. It is continuous, with f(0)=−1 and f(1)=1, so the IVT gives at least one root in (0,1). Also f′(x)=3x²+1>0 for every real x, so f is strictly increasing by the MVT. A strictly increasing function has at most one zero. Therefore exactly one real root exists.

Question 9 — Taylor polynomial and remainder bound

Find the degree-3 Maclaurin polynomial for eˣ and use Taylor's theorem to bound the error when approximating e⁰·² by this polynomial. Give a numerical upper bound.

10 marks
Show worked answer

The polynomial is P₃(x)=1+x+x²/2+x³/6. Taylor's remainder satisfies |R₃(0.2)|≤e^0.2(0.2)^4/24 because the fourth derivative is eˣ and on [0,0.2] it is at most e^0.2. Thus |R₃|≤1.222·0.0016/24<0.000082.

Question 10 — A parameter-dependent differentiability problem

Let \(f(x)=\begin{cases}ax+b,&x\leq1\\x^2+1,&x>1.\end{cases}\) Determine a and b so that f is differentiable at 1. Prove the resulting function is differentiable there.

10 marks
Show worked answer

Continuity at 1 requires a+b=2. Equality of one-sided derivatives requires a=2, since the left derivative is a and the right derivative is 2x evaluated at 1. Hence b=0. With (a,b)=(2,0), both pieces equal 2 at 1 and both one-sided difference quotients tend to 2, so f′(1)=2.

Chapter 3 — Calculus II: Integration and Series

Undergraduate level · Second calculus unit. This chapter develops integration as an inverse process to differentiation and as a limit of sums, then studies improper integrals, numerical approximation, sequences, infinite series and convergence tests. Students should justify convergence claims and state the hypotheses of every theorem used.

3.1 Antiderivatives and the definite integral

An antiderivative of f on an interval I is a differentiable function F satisfying F′(x)=f(x) on I. If F and G are antiderivatives of the same function on an interval, then F−G is constant. Thus an indefinite integral denotes a family: ∫f(x)dx=F(x)+C.

The definite integral is defined as a limit of Riemann sums. For a partition P of [a,b], choose sample points ξᵢ in each subinterval and form Σf(ξᵢ)Δxᵢ. If these sums approach a common limit as the mesh of the partition tends to zero, f is Riemann integrable and that limit is ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part I. If f is continuous on [a,b] and F(x)=∫ₐˣ f(t)dt, then F is differentiable on (a,b) and F′(x)=f(x).
Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II. If f is continuous on [a,b] and F′=f, then ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx=F(b)−F(a).

The theorem links the limit-of-sums definition to antiderivatives. It does not mean every function has an elementary antiderivative; for example, ∫e−x²dx has no elementary closed form.

3.2 Integration techniques

Substitution. If u=g(x), then ∫f(g(x))g′(x)dx=∫f(u)du. For definite integrals, transform both the integrand and the limits, or substitute and then evaluate using the original limits consistently.

Integration by parts. From the product rule, ∫u,dv=uv−∫v,du. For definite integrals, ∫ₐᵇu v′dx=[uv]ₐᵇ−∫ₐᵇv u′dx.

Partial fractions. A proper rational function can be decomposed into simpler fractions after factoring its denominator over the relevant field. Repeated linear factors require terms A₁/(x−a)+A₂/(x−a)²+…; irreducible real quadratic factors require linear numerators.

Trigonometric substitution. Expressions involving √(a²−x²), √(a²+x²), or √(x²−a²) can be simplified using x=a sinθ, x=a tanθ, or x=a secθ, respectively, with domains and signs handled carefully.

3.3 Improper integrals

An integral is improper when the interval is unbounded or the integrand is unbounded at an endpoint or interior point. It is defined by a limit. For example, ∫₁∞f(x)dx means limR→∞∫₁ᴿf(x)dx, if the limit exists finitely. If the limit fails to exist or is infinite, the integral diverges.

p-integral tests. ∫₁∞x−pdx converges iff p>1. ∫₀¹x−pdx converges iff p<1. These are distinct endpoint behaviours and should not be confused.

For nonnegative functions, comparison is useful: if 0≤f≤g and ∫g converges, then ∫f converges; if 0≤g≤f and ∫g diverges, then ∫f diverges. Limit comparison applies when f/g tends to a positive finite constant.

3.4 Numerical integration

The composite trapezoidal rule approximates an integral by replacing the curve on each subinterval with a straight segment. With equal width h=(b−a)/n:

\[T_n=\frac h2\left[f(x_0)+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(x_i)+f(x_n)\right].\]

Composite Simpson's rule, requiring even n, is

\[S_n=\frac h3\left[f(x_0)+4\sum_{i\text{ odd}}f(x_i)+2\sum_{i\text{ even},,0<i<n}f(x_i)+f(x_n)\right].\]

If f has a continuous second derivative, the composite trapezoidal error is bounded by (b−a)h² max|f″|/12. If f has a continuous fourth derivative, the composite Simpson error is bounded by (b−a)h⁴ max|f⁽⁴⁾|/180. These are error bounds, not estimates that always equal the actual error.

3.5 Sequences and limits

A sequence (aₙ) converges to L if for every ε>0 there exists N such that n≥N implies |aₙ−L|<ε. A sequence is bounded if |aₙ|≤M for some M and all n. Every convergent sequence is bounded, and every monotone bounded sequence converges. The converse of the latter statement is false in general: convergence does not require monotonicity.

Monotone convergence theorem for sequences. Every increasing sequence bounded above converges to its supremum; every decreasing sequence bounded below converges to its infimum.

3.6 Infinite series and convergence

An infinite series Σaₙ is defined through its partial sums sₙ=Σk=1ⁿaₖ. The series converges precisely when (sₙ) converges. A necessary condition is aₙ→0, but this condition alone is not sufficient: the harmonic series Σ1/n diverges.

  • Geometric series: Σₙ₌₀∞arⁿ converges to a/(1−r) iff |r|<1.
  • p-series: Σ1/nᵖ converges iff p>1.
  • Comparison: compare nonnegative terms with a known convergent or divergent series.
  • Limit comparison: if aₙ,bₙ>0 and lim aₙ/bₙ=c∈(0,∞), both series have the same convergence behaviour.
  • Ratio test: if L=limsup |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|, convergence is absolute for L<1 and divergence for L>1; L=1 is inconclusive.
  • Root test: use limsup ⁿ√|aₙ| with the same thresholds.
  • Alternating series test: Σ(−1)ⁿbₙ converges if bₙ decreases monotonically to zero. The error after N terms is at most bₙ₊₁.

3.7 Absolute and conditional convergence

A series Σaₙ converges absolutely if Σ|aₙ| converges. Absolute convergence implies convergence. A series is conditionally convergent if it converges but does not converge absolutely. For conditionally convergent real series, rearranging terms can change the sum; this is why absolute convergence is a stronger and structurally important property.

3.8 Power series and Taylor series

A power series centred at a is Σcₙ(x−a)ⁿ. Its radius of convergence R is determined, when the ratio limit exists, by R=lim |cₙ/cₙ₊₁|. It converges absolutely for |x−a|<R and diverges for |x−a|>R; each endpoint must be tested separately. Within the interval of convergence, a power series may be differentiated and integrated term by term on compact subintervals.

The Taylor series of f at a is Σf⁽ⁿ⁾(a)(x−a)ⁿ/n!. A function need not equal its Taylor series merely because all derivatives exist. Equality requires a suitable remainder to tend to zero. For example, eˣ has the globally convergent series Σxⁿ/n!, while smoothness alone is not enough to guarantee such representation.

Chapter 4 — Multivariable Calculus

Undergraduate level · Several-variable differential and integral calculus. This chapter extends limits, continuity, differentiation and integration to functions of several real variables. It develops partial derivatives, differentiability, the chain rule, gradient methods, constrained extrema, multiple integrals and coordinate transformations.

4.1 Functions of several variables and domains

A scalar-valued function of two variables is a mapping f:D⊆ℝ²→ℝ, while a vector-valued mapping may take values in ℝᵐ. The domain must be determined from restrictions such as nonzero denominators, nonnegative radicands and positive logarithm arguments. A level set f(x,y)=c is a curve in the domain; in three dimensions it is a level surface.

The graph z=f(x,y) is a surface. A trace is obtained by intersecting it with a coordinate plane or another plane. Contour plots display level curves and are useful for understanding gradients and extrema.

4.2 Limits and continuity in ℝ²

We say lim(x,y)→(a,b)f(x,y)=L if for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that 0<√((x−a)²+(y−b)²)<δ implies |f(x,y)−L|<ε. Unlike one-variable limits, checking a few paths is not enough to prove a multivariable limit exists. However, two paths giving different limiting values prove that the limit does not exist.

Useful existence methods include algebraic simplification, polar coordinates x=r cosθ, y=r sinθ, and bounds. If |f(x,y)−L|≤g(r) where g(r)→0 independently of θ, the squeeze theorem proves the limit. Continuity is defined by equality of the function value and the limit.

4.3 Partial derivatives and higher derivatives

The first partial derivatives are defined by holding the other variables fixed:

\[f_x(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}h,\qquad f_y(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}h.\]

Second partial derivatives include fₓₓ, fₓᵧ, fᵧₓ and fᵧᵧ. If the relevant mixed partial derivatives are continuous in a neighbourhood, Clairaut's theorem gives fₓᵧ=fᵧₓ. The Hessian matrix of a twice differentiable scalar function is the matrix of second partial derivatives.

4.4 Differentiability and the total differential

Partial derivatives existing at a point do not, by themselves, guarantee differentiability there. Differentiability at (a,b) means there is a linear map giving a first-order approximation with an error that is small relative to the distance from (a,b):

\[f(a+h,b+k)=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)h+f_y(a,b)k+o(\sqrt{h^2+k^2}).\]

A sufficient condition is that the first partial derivatives exist in a neighbourhood and are continuous at the point. The total differential is df=fₓdx+fᵧdy. For small increments, Δf≈df, with the approximation justified by differentiability.

4.5 The gradient and directional derivatives

The gradient is ∇f=(fₓ,fᵧ) in ℝ² and ∇f=(fₓ,fᵧ,f_z) in ℝ³. For a unit vector u, the directional derivative is Dᵤf=∇f·u. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase and its magnitude is the maximum directional derivative. At a regular point of a level curve or surface, ∇f is normal to the level set.

4.6 The multivariable chain rule

If z=f(x,y), where x=x(t) and y=y(t), then dz/dt=fₓ dx/dt+fᵧ dy/dt. For a composition of mappings, the derivative is the product of the corresponding Jacobian matrices. This matrix form is essential in coordinate changes, optimisation and numerical algorithms.

For implicit relations F(x,y,z)=0, if F_z≠0 locally, the implicit function theorem permits z to be treated locally as a function of x and y, with zₓ=−Fₓ/F_z and zᵧ=−Fᵧ/F_z.

4.7 Critical points and the second derivative test

An interior critical point occurs where ∇f=0 or a required derivative does not exist. For a twice continuously differentiable function of two variables, define D=fₓₓfᵧᵧ−(fₓᵧ)² at a critical point. If D>0 and fₓₓ>0, there is a local minimum; if D>0 and fₓₓ<0, a local maximum; if D<0, a saddle point. If D=0, the test is inconclusive.

For global extrema on a compact domain, continuity guarantees that maximum and minimum values are attained. One must examine interior critical points and the boundary, including corners or endpoints where applicable.

4.8 Constrained optimisation and Lagrange multipliers

To optimise f(x,y) subject to g(x,y)=c, regular constrained extrema satisfy ∇f=λ∇g, provided ∇g≠0 at the candidate. Geometrically, the level curves of f and g are tangent at a regular constrained extremum. The candidates must then be evaluated and compared; the multiplier equations alone do not classify a point as a maximum or minimum.

4.9 Double and triple integrals

A double integral ∬Rf(x,y)dA accumulates a scalar density over a planar region. For a rectangular region, an iterated integral can be evaluated by Fubini's theorem when the integrand is continuous (or under suitable integrability hypotheses). For nonrectangular regions, describe the bounds carefully using vertical or horizontal slices. Triple integrals extend this construction to volume regions.

4.10 Change of variables and Jacobians

For a differentiable one-to-one coordinate transformation (u,v)↦(x(u,v),y(u,v)), area transforms according to dA=|J|dudv, where J=∂(x,y)/∂(u,v). Thus ∬Rf(x,y)dA= ∬Sf(x(u,v),y(u,v))|J|dudv. In polar coordinates x=r cosθ, y=r sinθ and |J|=r, so dA=r dr dθ. In cylindrical coordinates dV=r dz dr dθ; in spherical coordinates dV=ρ²sinφ dρ dφ dθ under the usual convention for φ.

4.11 Applications and interpretation

Multivariable calculus is used to model temperature fields, pressure, population surfaces, production functions and energy landscapes. Partial derivatives measure marginal effects; the gradient describes local sensitivity; Hessians describe local curvature; multiple integrals calculate mass, charge or accumulated quantities from densities.

Chapter 5 — Vector Calculus

Undergraduate level · Vector fields, integral theorems and geometric analysis. This chapter develops the differential and integral calculus of scalar and vector fields. It connects local operators—gradient, divergence and curl—to global quantities through line, surface and volume integrals. Students should be able to state theorem hypotheses, orient curves and surfaces consistently, and select an appropriate integral theorem for a given problem.

5.1 Scalar fields, vector fields and parameterised curves

A scalar field on a region D⊆ℝⁿ is a function φ:D→ℝ, such as temperature or density. A vector field is a mapping F:D→ℝⁿ. In three dimensions write F(x,y,z)=P(x,y,z)i+Q(x,y,z)j+R(x,y,z)k. A vector field assigns both a direction and magnitude to each point in its domain. Examples include velocity, force and electromagnetic fields.

A smooth parameterised curve is r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)), a≤t≤b. Its velocity is r′(t), its speed is |r′(t)|, and its acceleration is r″(t). The curve is regular where r′(t)≠0. Its arc length is L=∫ₐᵇ|r′(t)|dt. Reversing the parameter changes the direction of traversal but not the geometric curve or its length.

Unit tangent. For a regular curve, T(t)=r′(t)/|r′(t)|. When the derivative exists and is nonzero, the curvature is κ=|dT/ds|=|r′×r″|/|r′|³ in ℝ³. Curvature measures the rate at which the unit tangent turns per unit arc length.

5.2 Differentiation and integration of vector-valued functions

For r(t)=(x₁(t),…,xₙ(t)), differentiation and integration are componentwise: r′(t)=(x₁′,…,xₙ′) and ∫r(t)dt=(∫x₁dt,…,∫xₙdt)+C. The fundamental theorem of calculus therefore applies componentwise. In mechanics, if r is position, v=r′ is velocity and a=v′ is acceleration. If acceleration is known, integration determines velocity and position only after initial conditions are supplied.

For a particle moving along a curve, speed is |v|, not generally the signed derivative of a single coordinate. The tangential component of acceleration is dv/dt and the normal component has magnitude κv². Thus a=(dv/dt)T+κv²N where the Frenet normal N is defined when curvature is nonzero.

5.3 The gradient of a scalar field

For a differentiable scalar field φ(x,y,z), its gradient is ∇φ=(φₓ,φᵧ,φ_z). For a unit direction u, the directional derivative is Dᵤφ=∇φ·u. The gradient is normal to a regular level surface φ=c and points in the direction of greatest local increase. The differential dφ=∇φ·dr describes the first-order change in φ.

For example, if φ(x,y,z)=x²y+z³, then ∇φ=(2xy,x²,3z²). At a point where ∇φ≠0, the tangent plane to the level surface φ=constant is ∇φ(a)·(r−a)=0. A normal vector is therefore ∇φ(a).

5.4 Divergence and its interpretation

For F=(P,Q,R), the divergence is the scalar field div F=∇·F=Pₓ+Qᵧ+R_z. It measures the local net outward flux per unit volume in the infinitesimal limit. Positive divergence indicates a local source, negative divergence a local sink, and zero divergence a solenoidal field. The physical interpretation depends on the field and its units.

Flux density interpretation. For a small volume V around a point where F is smooth, div F is the limit of the outward flux through ∂V divided by the volume, as V shrinks to the point. This is a local statement; zero divergence does not imply that F itself is zero.

5.5 Curl and local rotation

The curl of F=(P,Q,R) is the vector field

\[\nabla\times F=\left(R_y-Q_z,\;P_z-R_x,\;Q_x-P_y\right).\]

Curl measures infinitesimal circulation and the axis of local rotation. A field with curl zero is called irrotational. On a simply connected open region in ℝ³, a continuously differentiable field with curl F=0 is conservative; the domain condition matters because holes can obstruct a global potential. In two dimensions, for F=(P,Q), the scalar out-of-plane curl is Qₓ−Pᵧ.

5.6 Line integrals of scalar fields and vector fields

Let C be parameterised by r(t), a≤t≤b. The scalar line integral is ∫Cg ds=∫ₐᵇg(r(t))|r′(t)|dt. It accumulates a scalar density along the curve; for example, it can calculate the mass of a thin wire with linear density g.

The work integral of a vector field is ∫CF·dr=∫ₐᵇF(r(t))·r′(t)dt. It measures work done by a force along a path. Reversing the orientation changes the sign of a vector line integral, while a scalar line integral with ds is unchanged. A valid parameterisation must trace the intended curve with the stated orientation.

5.7 Conservative fields, potential functions and path independence

A vector field F is conservative if there exists a scalar potential φ such that F=∇φ. For a piecewise smooth curve C from A to B, the fundamental theorem for line integrals gives ∫CF·dr=φ(B)−φ(A). Consequently, the integral is independent of the path and is zero around every closed curve in the domain.

To test a proposed potential in ℝ³, integrate one component and compare the remaining partial derivatives. For F=(P,Q,R), a candidate φ must satisfy φₓ=P, φᵧ=Q and φ_z=R. Equality of cross-partials is a useful necessary test. On a simply connected domain, a C¹ field with curl zero is conservative; on a domain with holes, curl zero alone need not guarantee a global potential.

5.8 Green's theorem in the plane

Let C be a positively oriented, simple, closed, piecewise smooth curve bounding a region D, and let P,Q have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing D. Green's theorem states

Green's theorem (circulation form).
\[\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_D\left(Q_x-P_y\right)\,dA.\]

The positive orientation is counterclockwise, leaving the region on the left as the curve is traversed. The flux form is

\[\oint_C(P\,dy-Q\,dx)=\iint_D(P_x+Q_y)\,dA.\]

Green's theorem converts a boundary line integral into a double integral over the enclosed region, or conversely. It is especially useful when the boundary is complicated but the planar curl or divergence is simple. For regions with holes, all boundary components must be oriented consistently with the positive orientation of the region.

5.9 Surfaces, parametrisation and surface area

A smooth surface patch may be parameterised by r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)). The tangent vectors are rᵤ and rᵥ; a normal vector is rᵤ×rᵥ. The area element is dS=|rᵤ×rᵥ|dudv. The parameterisation is regular where rᵤ×rᵥ≠0. Surface area is ∬S1 dS.

For a graph z=f(x,y), a standard upward orientation has dS=√(1+fₓ²+fᵧ²)dA and an upward normal area vector n dS=(-fₓ,-fᵧ,1)dA. The unit normal is obtained by dividing by its magnitude. Orientation is essential for flux integrals but not for scalar surface area.

5.10 Surface integrals and flux

The scalar surface integral is ∬Sg dS=∬Dg(r(u,v))|rᵤ×rᵥ|dudv. The flux of F through an oriented surface is

\[\iint_S F\cdot n\,dS=\iint_D F(r(u,v))\cdot(r_u\times r_v)\,dudv,\]

where the order of the cross product must agree with the chosen orientation. Reversing the normal reverses the flux sign. Flux measures the amount of a vector field crossing a surface, rather than the amount lying along it.

5.11 Stokes' theorem

Stokes' theorem. If S is an oriented, piecewise smooth surface with positively oriented boundary C=∂S, and F is continuously differentiable on an open set containing S, then
\[\oint_C F\cdot dr=\iint_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS.\]

The orientation of C and the normal n are linked by the right-hand rule: when the right thumb points along n, the curled fingers indicate the positive boundary direction. Stokes' theorem equates circulation around the boundary with the flux of curl through the surface. Different surfaces with the same oriented boundary give the same value when the theorem's hypotheses apply.

5.12 The divergence theorem (Gauss' theorem)

Divergence theorem. Let V be a bounded solid region with a piecewise smooth, closed boundary surface ∂V, oriented by the outward unit normal n. If F is continuously differentiable on an open set containing V, then
\[\iint_{\partial V}F\cdot n\,dS=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot F\,dV.\]

The theorem converts total outward flux across a closed surface into the volume integral of divergence. The boundary must be closed and outward-oriented. It is often simpler to integrate divergence over the volume than to parameterise every face of the boundary.

5.13 Identities and theorem selection

For sufficiently smooth scalar and vector fields, important identities include ∇×(∇φ)=0, ∇·(∇×F)=0, and ∇·(φF)=∇φ·F+φ∇·F. These identities are consequences of equality of mixed partial derivatives under appropriate regularity assumptions. They provide checks on calculations and appear throughout fluid mechanics, electromagnetism and continuum physics.

  • Use the gradient for directional change and normals to level sets.
  • Use divergence for local sources and sinks, or total flux through a closed surface.
  • Use curl for local circulation, or Stokes' theorem on an oriented surface with boundary.
  • Use Green's theorem for a positively oriented planar boundary.
  • Use a potential function to evaluate work when the field is conservative and the endpoints are known.

Chapter 6 — Linear Algebra and Matrix Theory

Undergraduate level · Vector spaces, linear transformations, matrices, determinants and spectral theory. This chapter develops linear algebra from the abstract structure of vector spaces to computational matrix methods and eigenvalue theory. Emphasis is placed on the relationship between algebraic definitions, geometric interpretation and reliable computation. Unless stated otherwise, vector spaces and matrices are over the real field ℝ; the corresponding complex results are analogous, with conjugate transpose used in inner-product settings.

6.1 Linear systems and the geometry of solutions

A linear system in n unknowns can be written Ax=b, where A∈ℝᵐˣⁿ, x∈ℝⁿ and b∈ℝᵐ. Each row represents a linear constraint. The solution set is either empty or an affine translate of the null space N(A)={x:Ax=0}. Thus if x₀ is one solution, every solution has the form x=x₀+v, where v∈N(A).

A system is consistent precisely when b belongs to the column space of A. It has a unique solution precisely when it is consistent and the null space is trivial. It has infinitely many solutions when it is consistent and at least one free variable remains. Row operations preserve the solution set when applied to the augmented matrix [A|b].

6.2 Gaussian elimination and row reduction

Elementary row operations are: interchange two rows; multiply a row by a nonzero scalar; and add a scalar multiple of one row to another. They produce row-equivalent matrices. Gaussian elimination reduces a matrix to row-echelon form; Gauss–Jordan elimination continues to reduced row-echelon form. Pivot columns identify leading variables, while non-pivot columns correspond to free variables.

For numerical work, partial pivoting selects a large-magnitude pivot in the current column to reduce amplification of round-off error. A matrix is in row-echelon form if nonzero rows precede zero rows, each leading entry lies to the right of the leading entry in the row above, and entries below each leading entry are zero. Reduced row-echelon form additionally has leading entries equal to 1 and zero entries elsewhere in each pivot column.

6.3 Matrix algebra and elementary matrices

For compatible matrices, addition is entrywise and multiplication is defined by (AB)ᵢⱼ=Σₖaᵢₖbₖⱼ. Matrix multiplication is associative and distributive but generally not commutative. The identity matrix I satisfies AI=IA=A. The transpose satisfies (AB)ᵀ=BᵀAᵀ. An elementary row operation is equivalent to left multiplication by an elementary matrix.

A square matrix A is invertible if there exists A⁻¹ with AA⁻¹=A⁻¹A=I. Equivalent conditions include det(A)≠0, rank(A)=n, trivial null space, and linear independence of its columns. When A is invertible, Ax=b has the unique solution x=A⁻¹b. In computation, solving by factorisation is normally preferable to explicitly forming A⁻¹.

6.4 Determinants and their properties

The determinant det(A) is a scalar associated with a square matrix. It is multilinear and alternating in the rows (or columns), and det(I)=1. It measures the signed volume-scaling factor of the associated linear transformation. A zero determinant means that the transformation collapses dimension and the matrix is singular.

Key properties are det(AB)=det(A)det(B), det(Aᵀ)=det(A), and det(A⁻¹)=1/det(A) when A is invertible. Interchanging two rows changes the sign; multiplying a row by c multiplies the determinant by c; adding a multiple of one row to another leaves it unchanged. For triangular matrices, the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries. Cofactor expansion is useful for theoretical calculations, while elimination is generally more efficient for larger matrices.

6.5 Vector spaces and subspaces

A vector space V over a field F is a set with vector addition and scalar multiplication satisfying closure, associativity, commutativity of addition, additive identity and inverses, and the distributive and scalar identity laws. Familiar examples include ℝⁿ, polynomial spaces Pₙ, spaces of continuous functions and matrix spaces.

A nonempty subset W⊆V is a subspace if it is closed under linear combinations: for all u,v∈W and α,β∈F, αu+βv∈W. Equivalently, it contains zero and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. The solution set of a homogeneous linear system is a subspace; the solution set of a nonhomogeneous system is generally an affine set rather than a subspace.

6.6 Span, linear independence, bases and dimension

The span of vectors v₁,…,vₖ is the set of all linear combinations Σᵢcᵢvᵢ. The vectors are linearly independent if Σᵢcᵢvᵢ=0 implies every cᵢ=0. A basis is a linearly independent spanning set. Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis, and all bases have the same number of elements; this number is the dimension.

In ℝⁿ, a set of n vectors is a basis if and only if its column matrix is invertible. A spanning set can be reduced to a basis by removing redundant vectors; an independent set can be extended to a basis. Coordinates relative to a basis are unique, and changing basis is represented by an invertible transition matrix.

6.7 Fundamental subspaces: row space, column space and null space

For A∈ℝᵐˣⁿ, the column space Col(A) is a subspace of ℝᵐ, the row space Row(A) is a subspace of ℝⁿ, and the null space N(A) is a subspace of ℝⁿ. The rank is dim Col(A), equivalently dim Row(A). The nullity is dim N(A).

Rank–nullity theorem. For a linear map T:V→W with V finite-dimensional, dim V=dim ker(T)+dim im(T). For an m×n matrix A, rank(A)+nullity(A)=n.

The pivot columns of the original matrix form a basis for its column space. The nonzero rows of a row-echelon form form a basis for the row space. A basis for the null space is obtained by expressing pivot variables in terms of free variables in Ax=0.

6.8 Linear transformations and matrix representations

A map T:V→W is linear if T(αu+βv)=αT(u)+βT(v). Its kernel ker(T)={v:T(v)=0} is a subspace of V, and its image im(T)={T(v):v∈V} is a subspace of W. T is injective exactly when its kernel is {0}; it is surjective exactly when its image is W.

Once bases are chosen in V and W, T is represented by a matrix. The columns of the representation are the coordinates of the images of the domain basis vectors. Composition corresponds to matrix multiplication, with the order reflecting the order of composition. A change of basis changes the matrix representation but not the underlying linear map.

6.9 Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and characteristic polynomials

For a square matrix A, a nonzero vector v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ if Av=λv. Thus (A−λI)v=0 has a nonzero solution precisely when det(A−λI)=0. The polynomial p_A(λ)=det(λI−A) is the characteristic polynomial. Its roots are the eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity.

The eigenspace E_λ=ker(A−λI) has dimension called the geometric multiplicity. Geometric multiplicity is at least 1 and at most algebraic multiplicity. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. The trace is the sum of eigenvalues and the determinant is their product, counting algebraic multiplicity over ℂ.

6.10 Diagonalisation and matrix powers

An n×n matrix A is diagonalizable if A=PDP⁻¹ for an invertible P and diagonal D. The columns of P are a basis of eigenvectors and the diagonal entries of D are the corresponding eigenvalues. A matrix with n distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. More generally, it is diagonalizable if the direct sum of its eigenspaces has dimension n.

When A=PDP⁻¹, powers are efficiently computed as Aᵏ=PDᵏP⁻¹. Diagonalisation is useful for linear recurrences, repeated applications of a linear transformation, and systems of constant-coefficient differential equations. Not every matrix is diagonalizable; repeated eigenvalues require checking the dimensions of their eigenspaces.

6.11 Inner products, orthogonality and projections

An inner product on a real vector space is a symmetric, bilinear, positive-definite map ⟨u,v⟩. In ℝⁿ the standard inner product is u·v=Σᵢuᵢvᵢ. The induced norm is ||v||=√⟨v,v⟩. Vectors are orthogonal if their inner product is zero. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states |⟨u,v⟩|≤||u||||v||, with equality precisely when the vectors are linearly dependent.

For a nonzero vector u, the orthogonal projection of v onto span{u} is projᵤ(v)=(⟨v,u⟩/⟨u,u⟩)u. The residual v−projᵤ(v) is orthogonal to u. For an orthonormal set q₁,…,qₖ, projection onto its span is Σᵢ⟨v,qᵢ⟩qᵢ.

6.12 Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation and QR factorisation

Given linearly independent vectors v₁,…,vₖ in an inner-product space, Gram–Schmidt constructs an orthonormal basis q₁,…,qₖ for the same span. Set u₁=v₁ and q₁=u₁/||u₁||. Recursively, uⱼ=vⱼ−Σᵢ<ⱼ⟨vⱼ,qᵢ⟩qᵢ and qⱼ=uⱼ/||uⱼ||. In exact arithmetic, each uⱼ is nonzero when the original vectors are independent.

For a matrix with independent columns, the process yields A=QR, where Q has orthonormal columns and R is upper triangular. QR factorisation supports least-squares solutions and stable numerical algorithms. In floating-point computation, modified Gram–Schmidt or Householder transformations are often preferred for improved numerical stability.

6.13 Symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem

Real spectral theorem. Every real symmetric matrix A=Aᵀ is orthogonally diagonalizable: A=QΛQᵀ, where Q is orthogonal and Λ is real diagonal. Its eigenvalues are real, and eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

This result underpins principal component analysis, quadratic optimisation, vibration analysis and covariance modelling. For a symmetric matrix, the quadratic form xᵀAx becomes Σᵢλᵢyᵢ² after the orthogonal coordinate change x=Qy. The signs of the eigenvalues determine whether the form is positive definite, negative definite, semidefinite or indefinite.

6.14 Quadratic forms and definiteness

A quadratic form is q(x)=xᵀAx, where A may be taken symmetric because xᵀAx depends only on (A+Aᵀ)/2. It is positive definite if q(x)>0 for all x≠0, positive semidefinite if q(x)≥0, negative definite if q(x)<0 for all x≠0, and indefinite if it takes both positive and negative values.

For real symmetric A, positive definiteness is equivalent to all eigenvalues being positive. Sylvester's criterion states that A is positive definite if and only if all leading principal minors are positive. Definiteness determines local curvature in optimisation and stability in many physical systems.

6.15 Least-squares approximation

When Ax=b is inconsistent, a least-squares solution minimises ||Ax−b||₂². Differentiating the objective yields the normal equations AᵀAx=Aᵀb. If A has full column rank, AᵀA is invertible and the solution is unique: x̂=(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb. Geometrically, A x̂ is the orthogonal projection of b onto Col(A), so the residual b−Ax̂ is orthogonal to every column of A.

For numerical work, QR factorisation or singular value decomposition is generally more stable than explicitly forming and solving the normal equations, because forming AᵀA can square the condition number.

6.16 Numerical conditioning and singular value decomposition

The condition number κ₂(A)=σmax/σmin for an invertible matrix, where σmax and σmin are its largest and smallest singular values. A large condition number means small perturbations in the data can cause large relative changes in the solution. The singular value decomposition is A=UΣVᵀ, where U and V are orthogonal and Σ has nonnegative singular values on its diagonal (or rectangular diagonal).

SVD reveals rank, supports stable least-squares computation, gives the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse, and is central to low-rank approximation. Truncating small singular values can regularise noisy or ill-conditioned problems, but the truncation threshold should be justified by the data and numerical precision.

Chapter 7 — Ordinary Differential Equations

Undergraduate level · First-order equations, linear theory, higher-order equations, transforms, systems and stability. An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates an unknown function of one independent variable to one or more of its derivatives. ODEs model population dynamics, mechanical oscillations, electrical circuits, chemical kinetics, heat exchange and many other processes. This chapter develops analytical methods, existence and uniqueness principles, numerical approximations and qualitative analysis.

7.1 Definitions, order, degree and initial-value problems

An ODE has the general form F(t,y,y′,…,y⁽ⁿ⁾)=0. Its order is the highest derivative present. Its degree, when defined, is the power of the highest-order derivative after the equation has been made polynomial in derivatives. A solution is a sufficiently differentiable function satisfying the equation on an interval. A general solution typically contains arbitrary constants; initial or boundary conditions select particular solutions.

An nth-order equation can often be rewritten as a first-order system by setting x₁=y, x₂=y′,…,xₙ=y⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾. An initial-value problem (IVP) specifies y(t₀), y′(t₀), …, y⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾(t₀). A boundary-value problem specifies conditions at more than one point, for example y(a)=α and y(b)=β. An IVP and a boundary-value problem need not have the same existence or uniqueness properties.

7.2 Separable first-order equations

An equation y′=g(t)h(y) is separable wherever the division is valid. For h(y)≠0, write dy/h(y)=g(t)dt and integrate. Equilibrium solutions satisfying h(y)=0 must be checked separately because division by h(y) can remove them. An implicit relation may be the natural form of the solution; solving explicitly for y is not always possible or necessary.

Example. For y′=ty, separation gives dy/y=t dt, hence ln|y|=t²/2+C and y=Ce^(t²/2). The solution y=0 is included by C=0. If y(0)=2, then C=2.

7.3 First-order linear equations and integrating factors

A first-order linear equation has the standard form y′+p(t)y=q(t). An integrating factor is μ(t)=exp(∫p(t)dt). Multiplication by μ gives (μy)′=μq, so μ(t)y(t)=∫μ(t)q(t)dt+C. The method applies on intervals where p and q are continuous. The arbitrary constant is determined by an initial condition.

For y′+2y=e^(−t), choose μ=e^(2t). Then (e^(2t)y)′=e^t, giving y=e^(−t)+Ce^(−2t). This decomposition displays a particular response and a homogeneous transient.

7.4 Exact differential equations and integrating factors

An equation M(t,y)dt+N(t,y)dy=0 is exact on a suitable simply connected region if there is a potential Ψ(t,y) with Ψₜ=M and Ψᵧ=N. A necessary local condition, under continuous first partial derivatives, is Mᵧ=Nₜ. To solve, integrate M with respect to t, include an unknown function of y, and determine that function by matching Ψᵧ=N. The solution is Ψ(t,y)=C.

If Mᵧ≠Nₜ, the equation is not exact as written. An integrating factor depending only on t may exist when (Mᵧ−Nₜ)/N is a function of t alone; one depending only on y may exist when (Nₜ−Mᵧ)/M is a function of y alone. These tests are sufficient in the stated circumstances, not universal guarantees.

7.5 Homogeneous first-order equations and Bernoulli equations

A first-order homogeneous equation y′=F(y/t) can be reduced using y=vt, so y′=v+t v′. The resulting equation in v and t is often separable. The substitution must be used on intervals where t≠0, and solutions lost through division should be checked separately.

Bernoulli's equation y′+p(t)y=q(t)yⁿ, with n≠0,1, becomes linear after setting u=y^(1−n). Since u′=(1−n)y^(−n)y′, the transformed equation is u′+(1−n)p(t)u=(1−n)q(t). Solutions with y=0 may need separate consideration depending on n and the domain.

7.6 Existence and uniqueness for first-order IVPs

Picard–Lindelöf theorem (local form). For y′=f(t,y), y(t₀)=y₀, if f and ∂f/∂y are continuous on a rectangle containing (t₀,y₀), then there exists a unique local solution through that initial point.

Continuity of f alone is sufficient for at least one local solution under the Peano existence theorem, but does not guarantee uniqueness. A common uniqueness condition is local Lipschitz continuity in y. The theorem is local: it does not by itself establish that a solution exists for all time. Blow-up, singularities or leaving the domain may limit the interval of existence.

7.7 Autonomous equations and qualitative phase lines

An autonomous scalar equation has the form y′=f(y). Equilibria satisfy f(y*)=0. On intervals where f is positive, solutions increase; where it is negative, solutions decrease. An equilibrium is asymptotically stable if nearby solutions approach it as t increases, and unstable if nearby solutions move away. For a simple equilibrium, f′(y*)<0 implies local asymptotic stability, while f′(y*)>0 implies instability.

Qualitative analysis can reveal long-term behaviour without an explicit formula. Solutions of a well-posed autonomous scalar IVP cannot cross an equilibrium solution when uniqueness holds. This order-preserving property is useful in population and chemical concentration models.

7.8 Numerical methods: Euler and improved Euler

For y′=f(t,y), with step h and initial approximation yₙ at tₙ, forward Euler gives yₙ₊₁=yₙ+h f(tₙ,yₙ). It has local truncation error O(h²) and global error O(h) under standard smoothness assumptions. Smaller steps usually improve accuracy, but increase computational cost and do not automatically cure instability.

The explicit trapezoidal or Heun method first predicts y*=yₙ+h f(tₙ,yₙ), then corrects yₙ₊₁=yₙ+(h/2)[f(tₙ,yₙ)+f(tₙ₊₁,y*)]. It is a second-order method under suitable smoothness assumptions. Error estimates and step-size control are important in practical computation.

7.9 Fourth-order Runge–Kutta method

The classical RK4 method uses k₁=f(tₙ,yₙ), k₂=f(tₙ+h/2,yₙ+hk₁/2), k₃=f(tₙ+h/2,yₙ+hk₂/2), k₄=f(tₙ+h,yₙ+hk₃), and yₙ₊₁=yₙ+(h/6)(k₁+2k₂+2k₃+k₄). It has global error O(h⁴) for sufficiently smooth problems and suitable step sizes. RK4 is widely used, but stiff equations often require implicit or specialised solvers.

7.10 Higher-order linear equations and the superposition principle

A general nth-order linear equation is aₙ(t)y⁽ⁿ⁾+…+a₁(t)y′+a₀(t)y=g(t), with aₙ(t)≠0. The associated homogeneous equation has an n-dimensional solution space on an interval where the coefficients are continuous and the leading coefficient is nonzero. If y₁,…,yₙ form a fundamental set, the complementary solution is y_c=Σᵢcᵢyᵢ.

Superposition principle. For a linear homogeneous differential operator L, if Ly₁=0 and Ly₂=0, then L(c₁y₁+c₂y₂)=0. For a nonhomogeneous equation Ly=g, every solution has the form y=y_c+y_p, where y_p is one particular solution.

7.11 Linear equations with constant coefficients

For a homogeneous equation with constant coefficients, try y=e^(rt). The characteristic polynomial is obtained by replacing each derivative by the corresponding power of r. Distinct real roots rᵢ yield terms e^(rᵢt). A real repeated root r of multiplicity m yields e^(rt), te^(rt),…,t^(m−1)e^(rt). A complex pair α±iβ yields real solutions e^(αt)cos(βt) and e^(αt)sin(βt), with additional powers of t for repeated roots.

For example, y″−3y′+2y=0 has characteristic equation (r−1)(r−2)=0, so y=C₁eᵗ+C₂e²ᵗ. The initial conditions determine C₁ and C₂.

7.12 Nonhomogeneous equations: undetermined coefficients

For constant-coefficient linear equations, undetermined coefficients construct a trial particular solution based on the forcing term. Polynomial, exponential, sine and cosine forcing terms, and their finite sums, are suitable. If a trial term overlaps with a complementary solution, multiply the trial by a sufficient power of t to obtain linear independence. The method is not generally applicable to arbitrary forcing functions or variable coefficients.

For y″−3y′+2y=e^(3t), try yₚ=Ae^(3t). Substitution gives (9−9+2)Ae^(3t)=e^(3t), hence A=1/2. The complete solution is y=C₁eᵗ+C₂e²ᵗ+(1/2)e^(3t).

7.13 Variation of parameters

For y″+p(t)y′+q(t)y=g(t), let y₁,y₂ be a fundamental pair and W=y₁y₂′−y₁′y₂ their Wronskian. A particular solution is yₚ=−y₁∫(y₂g/W)dt+y₂∫(y₁g/W)dt. The formula is valid on intervals where W≠0 and the coefficients and forcing term have the required continuity. It is more general than undetermined coefficients, though integrals may be difficult or non-elementary.

7.14 Cauchy–Euler equations

A Cauchy–Euler equation has coefficients arranged as t²y″+a t y′+b y=0. On an interval not containing t=0, try y=tʳ. This gives the indicial equation r(r−1)+ar+b=0. Distinct roots yield tʳ¹ and tʳ²; a repeated root r yields tʳ and tʳln|t|. Complex roots α±iβ yield tᵅcos(βln|t|) and tᵅsin(βln|t|). The interval and sign of t must be handled consistently.

7.15 Laplace transforms and initial conditions

The Laplace transform is ℒ{f(t)}=F(s)=∫₀∞e^(−st)f(t)dt where the integral converges. Important formulas include ℒ{1}=1/s, ℒ{e^(at)}=1/(s−a), ℒ{sin bt}=b/(s²+b²), ℒ{cos bt}=s/(s²+b²), and ℒ{f′}=sF(s)−f(0). Also ℒ{f″}=s²F(s)−sf(0)−f′(0).

Transforming a linear IVP converts derivatives into algebraic expressions in s together with the initial data. Solve for F(s), decompose into standard partial fractions, and invert. The method is particularly useful for discontinuous or piecewise forcing, impulses and circuit switching. Existence of the transform requires suitable growth and piecewise continuity conditions.

7.16 Systems of first-order linear ODEs

A linear system is x′=A(t)x+g(t). For constant A and g=0, solutions are governed by the matrix exponential e^(At)=Σₖ₌₀∞(At)ᵏ/k!. If A is diagonalizable, A=PDP⁻¹ and e^(At)=Pe^(Dt)P⁻¹, where e^(Dt) is diagonal with entries e^(λᵢt). Generalised eigenvectors handle defective matrices. For nonzero forcing, variation of constants gives x(t)=e^(A(t−t₀))x(t₀)+∫ₜ₀ᵗe^(A(t−s))g(s)ds.

For a two-dimensional autonomous system x′=Ax, eigenvalues determine the local phase portrait: negative real parts indicate asymptotic decay, any positive real part indicates instability, and purely imaginary eigenvalues require additional analysis in nonlinear systems. Repeated or defective eigenvalues can produce polynomial factors multiplying exponentials.

7.17 Modelling, dimensional consistency and interpretation

Constructing an ODE model requires defining state variables, independent variables, parameters, assumptions and initial data. Dimensional analysis checks that every term in an equation has compatible units. A model should be interpreted within its assumptions: constant coefficients, unlimited resources, linear restoring forces or instantaneous mixing may be approximations rather than universal truths.

Examples include exponential growth y′=ky, logistic growth y′=ry(1−y/K), Newton cooling T′=−k(T−Tₐ), and damped oscillation my″+cy′+ky=0. Parameter signs and magnitudes determine whether a model grows, decays, oscillates or approaches equilibrium. Model validation requires comparing predictions with observations and checking sensitivity to parameters and initial conditions.

7.18 Worked university examples

Example 1 — First-order linear IVP. Solve y′+2y=4, y(0)=1. The integrating factor is e^(2t), so (e^(2t)y)′=4e^(2t). Integration gives y=2+Ce^(−2t). The initial condition gives C=−1; hence y=2−e^(−2t).
Example 2 — Repeated characteristic root. Solve y″−4y′+4y=0. The characteristic polynomial is (r−2)², so y=(C₁+C₂t)e^(2t). The factor t is required because the root is repeated.
Example 3 — Laplace transform. Solve y′+y=1, y(0)=0. Taking transforms gives sY(s)+Y(s)=1/s, so Y=1/[s(s+1)]=1/s−1/(s+1). Therefore y(t)=1−e^(−t), t≥0.
Example 4 — Logistic equilibrium. For y′=ry(1−y/K), with r,K>0, equilibria are 0 and K. Since f′(y)=r(1−2y/K), f′(0)=r>0 and f′(K)=−r<0. Thus zero is unstable and carrying capacity K is locally asymptotically stable for positive populations.

Chapter 8 — Partial Differential Equations (Introductory)

Classification, first-order PDEs, separation of variables, Fourier methods and classical boundary-value problems. A partial differential equation (PDE) relates an unknown function of two or more independent variables to its partial derivatives. PDEs describe diffusion, waves, electrostatics, fluid flow and quantum systems. The central tasks are classification, selecting suitable coordinates, constructing solutions and interpreting initial and boundary data.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

8.1 Definitions, order and linearity

For u=u(x₁,…,xₙ), a PDE is F(x,u,Du,D²u,…)=0. Its order is the highest derivative order. A linear PDE is linear in u and all its derivatives; coefficients may depend on independent variables. A homogeneous linear PDE has zero forcing term. A quasilinear PDE is linear in its highest-order derivatives but may be nonlinear in lower-order terms.

8.2 Classification of second-order equations

For A uₓₓ+2B uₓᵧ+C uᵧᵧ+ lower-order terms = f, classify using Δ=B²−AC: hyperbolic if Δ>0, parabolic if Δ=0, elliptic if Δ<0. Examples: wave equation uₜₜ=c²uₓₓ is hyperbolic; heat equation uₜ=κuₓₓ is parabolic; Laplace equation uₓₓ+uᵧᵧ=0 is elliptic. Classification is local when coefficients vary.

8.3 First-order PDEs and characteristics

For a uₓ+b uᵧ=c, characteristic curves satisfy dx/ds=a, dy/ds=b, du/ds=c. Along these curves the PDE becomes an ODE. For uₓ+uᵧ=0, characteristics have y−x constant and u is constant along each line, so u=F(y−x). Initial data must be prescribed on a non-characteristic curve for a locally determined solution.

8.4 Separation of variables

For a product ansatz u(x,t)=X(x)T(t), substitution into a linear PDE often separates independent-variable expressions and sets each equal to a constant. Boundary conditions determine admissible eigenvalues and eigenfunctions; initial data determine expansion coefficients. Separation is a construction method, not a claim that every solution is a single product.

Chapter 9 — Real Analysis

Sequences, limits, continuity, differentiation, integration, metric spaces and rigorous convergence. Real analysis supplies the rigorous foundations of calculus. It studies completeness of the real numbers, convergence, continuity, differentiation, integration and function spaces. Proofs depend on precise quantifiers and estimates rather than graphical intuition.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

9.1 Ordered fields and completeness

The real numbers form a complete ordered field. Completeness may be expressed as the least-upper-bound property: every nonempty set bounded above has a supremum. The Archimedean property states that for every real x there is n∈N with n>x. Completeness distinguishes ℝ from ℚ.

9.2 Sequences and convergence

A sequence (aₙ) converges to L if for every ε>0 there exists N such that n≥N implies |aₙ−L|<ε. Limits are unique. Every convergent sequence is bounded. The algebraic limit laws hold when the relevant limits exist.

9.3 Cauchy sequences and subsequences

A sequence is Cauchy if for every ε>0 there is N such that m,n≥N implies |aₘ−aₙ|<ε. ℝ is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges. Bolzano–Weierstrass states every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence.

9.4 Series and convergence

Σaₙ converges when its partial sums converge. The Cauchy criterion says tails of partial sums become arbitrarily small. Absolute convergence implies convergence. Comparison, limit comparison, ratio, root and alternating-series tests are tools whose hypotheses must be checked.

9.5 Limits and continuity

The ε–δ definition: limₓ→a f(x)=L if for each ε>0 there is δ>0 such that 0<|x−a|<δ implies |f(x)−L|<ε. Continuity at a includes f(a)=L. Sequential continuity is equivalent in metric spaces.

9.6 Compactness and uniform continuity

In ℝ, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded. Continuous functions on compact sets attain extrema and are uniformly continuous. Uniform continuity chooses δ independently of the point x; ordinary continuity may choose δ depending on x.

9.7 Differentiation and the mean value theorem

Differentiability at a means f(a+h)=f(a)+Lh+o(h), where L=f′(a). Differentiability implies continuity. Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem connect local derivatives to global change: f(b)−f(a)=f′(c)(b−a).

9.8 Riemann integration

A bounded function on [a,b] is Riemann integrable when its upper and lower Darboux integrals agree. Every continuous function on a compact interval is integrable. The fundamental theorem links differentiation and integration.

9.9 Metric spaces and completeness

A metric d satisfies non-negativity, identity, symmetry and the triangle inequality. Open balls define topology. A metric space is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges in the space. Closed subsets of complete metric spaces are complete.

9.10 Pointwise and uniform convergence

fₙ→f uniformly if for every ε>0 there is N such that n≥N implies |fₙ(x)−f(x)|<ε for every x in the domain. Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous. Uniform convergence permits interchange with integration under standard hypotheses; differentiation needs stronger conditions.

Chapter 10 — Complex Analysis

Complex differentiation, analytic functions, contour integration, residues and conformal maps. Complex analysis studies functions of a complex variable. Complex differentiability is substantially more restrictive than real differentiability and yields powerful integral theorems, series representations and residue techniques.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

10.1 Complex numbers and geometry

Write z=x+iy=re^(iθ), with modulus |z|=√(x²+y²) and argument θ modulo 2π. The conjugate is z̄=x−iy. The identities z z̄=|z|² and e^(iθ)=cosθ+i sinθ support polar calculations and roots.

10.2 Complex limits and continuity

A limit f(z)→L as z→z₀ must be independent of the path of approach in the plane. To disprove a proposed limit, two paths giving different limits suffice. Continuity is defined using the complex modulus.

10.3 Complex differentiability and Cauchy–Riemann equations

For f=u+iv, complex differentiability implies uₓ=vᵧ and uᵧ=−vₓ. If first partial derivatives are continuous near a point and satisfy these equations, f is analytic there. The equations alone at a single point are not sufficient.

10.4 Analytic functions and harmonic functions

If f=u+iv is analytic with suitable second derivatives, u and v are harmonic: uₓₓ+uᵧᵧ=0 and vₓₓ+vᵧᵧ=0. They are harmonic conjugates. The complex derivative is f′=uₓ+ivₓ=vᵧ−iuᵧ.

10.5 Elementary complex functions

The exponential eᶻ=eˣ(cos y+i sin y) is entire and 2πi-periodic. Complex logarithm is multivalued; a branch requires a chosen domain and argument interval. Powers and roots inherit branch choices.

10.6 Contours and complex integration

For a parametrised contour z(t), ∫γ f(z)dz=∫ f(z(t))z′(t)dt. Integrals depend on orientation. A positively oriented simple closed curve has its interior on the left as it is traversed.

10.7 Cauchy's theorem and integral formula

If f is analytic on a simply connected domain, its integral around a closed contour is zero. Cauchy's integral formula gives f(a)=(1/2πi)∮ f(z)/(z−a) dz and derivative formulas for higher derivatives.

10.8 Taylor and Laurent series

Analytic functions admit Taylor expansions within the nearest singularity. Around an isolated singularity, a Laurent series includes positive and negative powers. The principal part determines the singularity type.

10.9 Singularities and residues

An isolated singularity is removable, a pole, or essential. The residue is the coefficient of (z−a)⁻¹. For a simple pole, Res(f,a)=lim(z→a)(z−a)f(z). The residue theorem converts contour integrals into 2πi times the sum of enclosed residues.

10.10 Conformal mappings

An analytic map with nonzero derivative is locally angle-preserving. Möbius maps (az+b)/(cz+d), ad−bc≠0, map generalized circles to generalized circles. Mapping domains can simplify boundary-value problems.

Chapter 11 — Abstract Algebra and Group Theory

Groups, subgroups, cyclic groups, homomorphisms, quotient groups, rings and fields. Abstract algebra studies algebraic structures through axioms and structure-preserving maps. Group theory formalises symmetry; rings and fields generalise arithmetic. Proofs require careful use of definitions and structural theorems.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

11.1 Binary operations and groups

A group (G,*) has closure, associativity, an identity e and inverses. Commutativity is an additional property, not part of the group axioms. The order of an element is the least positive n with aⁿ=e, if such n exists.

11.2 Subgroups and cyclic groups

A nonempty subset H⊆G is a subgroup if it is closed under ab⁻¹. A group generated by g is ⟨g⟩={gⁿ:n∈ℤ}; cyclic groups are abelian. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

11.3 Permutation groups

Sₙ is the group of all permutations of n objects under composition, with |Sₙ|=n!. Disjoint cycle decomposition is unique up to cycle order. A k-cycle has sign (−1)^(k−1).

11.4 Cosets and Lagrange's theorem

Left cosets of H partition a finite group G into equal-sized sets. Thus |H| divides |G|. The order of any element divides |G|. The converse of Lagrange's theorem is not true for all groups.

11.5 Normal subgroups and quotient groups

H is normal if gH=Hg for every g∈G, equivalently ghg⁻¹∈H. Only normal subgroups yield quotient groups G/H with well-defined coset multiplication.

11.6 Homomorphisms and isomorphisms

A homomorphism φ:G→K satisfies φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b). Its kernel is normal and image is a subgroup. The first isomorphism theorem states G/kerφ≅imφ.

11.7 Direct products and group actions

The direct product combines componentwise operations. A group action describes symmetries acting on a set. Orbit–stabiliser gives |Orb(x)|=[G:Stab(x)] for finite groups.

11.8 Rings and integral domains

A ring has an abelian additive group and associative multiplication distributing over addition. A commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors is an integral domain. Units are elements with multiplicative inverses.

11.9 Ideals and quotient rings

An ideal absorbs multiplication by ring elements and is an additive subgroup. Quotient rings R/I generalise quotient groups. In a commutative ring with identity, maximal ideals correspond to quotient fields.

11.10 Fields and polynomial structure

A field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element is a unit. Polynomial division over a field yields quotient and remainder; irreducible polynomials generate field extensions via quotient constructions.

Chapter 12 — Elementary and Analytic Number Theory

Divisibility, congruences, primes, arithmetic functions, Diophantine equations and analytic methods. Number theory studies the integers and their arithmetic structure. Elementary methods include divisibility, Euclid's algorithm, congruences and prime factorisation; analytic number theory studies arithmetic through series, products and estimates.

12.1 Divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm

For integers a,b with a≠0, a|b means b=ak for some integer k. The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly replaces (a,b) by (b,r), where a=qb+r and 0≤r<|b|; the final nonzero remainder is gcd(a,b). Back-substitution yields Bézout coefficients.

12.2 Primes and fundamental theorem of arithmetic

A prime p>1 has only positive divisors 1 and p. Every integer n>1 factors uniquely into primes up to order. Euclid's lemma states p|ab implies p|a or p|b. Infinitude of primes follows by considering one plus the product of any finite list.

12.3 Linear Diophantine equations

The equation ax+by=c has integer solutions exactly when gcd(a,b)|c. If (x₀,y₀) is one solution and d=gcd(a,b), all solutions are x=x₀+(b/d)t, y=y₀−(a/d)t.

12.4 Congruences and modular arithmetic

a≡b (mod m) iff m|(a−b). Congruences respect addition and multiplication. Cancellation by a is valid modulo m only when gcd(a,m)=1, unless additional divisibility conditions are established.

12.5 Chinese remainder theorem

For pairwise coprime moduli mᵢ, a system x≡aᵢ (mod mᵢ) has a unique solution modulo M=∏mᵢ. For non-coprime moduli, compatibility requires residues agree modulo each pairwise gcd.

12.6 Fermat and Euler theorems

If p is prime and p∤a, a^(p−1)≡1 (mod p). More generally, if gcd(a,n)=1, a^φ(n)≡1 (mod n), where φ is Euler's totient function.

12.7 Multiplicative arithmetic functions

A function f is multiplicative if f(mn)=f(m)f(n) when gcd(m,n)=1. Examples include φ(n), divisor count τ(n), and sum of divisors σ(n). For n=∏pᵃ, φ(n)=n∏(1−1/p), τ(n)=∏(a+1).

12.8 Quadratic residues

An integer a is a quadratic residue modulo odd prime p if x²≡a (mod p) has a solution. The Legendre symbol (a/p) records residue status. Euler's criterion gives a^((p−1)/2)≡(a/p) mod p for p∤a.

12.9 Diophantine equations and continued fractions

Integer solutions may be constrained by parity, modular residues, factorisation and inequalities. Continued fractions provide rational approximations and are central to solving Pell equations x²−Dy²=1.

12.10 Analytic number theory: zeta function and primes

For Re(s)>1, ζ(s)=Σn≥1 n^(−s)=∏p(1−p^(−s))^(−1). The Euler product encodes unique prime factorisation. The prime number theorem states π(x)~x/log x as x→∞.

Induction proves a base case and an implication from n to n+1. Strong induction assumes all earlier cases. Recursive definitions specify initial values and a rule generating later terms; termination and correctness require proof.

13.4 Algorithms and asymptotic analysis

An algorithm is a finite, unambiguous procedure. Big-O gives an asymptotic upper bound; Θ gives a tight bound and Ω a lower bound. Analyse worst-case, average-case or best-case complexity as specified.

13.5 Graphs and representations

A graph G=(V,E) has vertices and edges. Graphs may be directed, undirected, weighted, simple or multigraphs. Adjacency matrices encode edges; adjacency lists are often more space-efficient for sparse graphs.

13.6 Paths, connectivity and components

A walk may repeat vertices and edges; a trail does not repeat edges; a path does not repeat vertices. Connected components partition an undirected graph. In directed graphs, strong connectivity requires directed paths both ways.

13.7 Trees and spanning trees

A tree is connected and acyclic. A tree with n vertices has n−1 edges and a unique simple path between every pair of vertices. A spanning tree connects all vertices without cycles.

13.8 Euler and Hamilton paths

An Euler trail uses every edge exactly once; a connected graph has an Euler circuit iff every vertex has even degree, and an Euler trail iff exactly zero or two vertices have odd degree. A Hamilton path visits every vertex once; no equally simple general degree criterion exists.

13.9 Planar graphs and colouring

A planar graph can be embedded without edge crossings. For a connected planar graph, V−E+F=2. A simple planar graph with V≥3 has E≤3V−6. The four-colour theorem states every planar graph is 4-colourable.

13.10 Graph algorithms and complexity

Breadth-first search finds shortest paths in unweighted graphs; depth-first search supports connectivity and cycle detection. Dijkstra's algorithm requires nonnegative edge weights. Minimum spanning trees are found by Kruskal or Prim. P, NP and NP-completeness classify computational problems.

Chapter 14 — Combinatorics

Counting principles, permutations, combinations, recurrences, generating functions and extremal methods. Combinatorics counts finite structures and proves existence through counting arguments. It includes bijections, inclusion–exclusion, recurrences, generating functions and extremal bounds.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

14.1 Sum and product principles

If disjoint alternatives have m and n possibilities, their union has m+n. If a process has m choices followed by n choices independently, it has mn outcomes. Avoid multiplying when choices are dependent without adjusting the counts.

14.2 Permutations and combinations

Ordered selections of r distinct objects from n number n!/(n−r)!. Unordered selections number C(n,r)=n!/[r!(n−r)!]. With repetition, ordered r-tuples from n symbols number nʳ.

14.3 Multinomial coefficients and distributions

The number of arrangements of n objects with multiplicities n₁,…,nₖ is n!/(n₁!…nₖ!). The number of nonnegative integer solutions to x₁+…+xₖ=n is C(n+k−1,k−1), by stars and bars.

14.4 Binomial theorem and identities

(x+y)ⁿ=Σ C(n,k)x^(n−k)yᵏ. Pascal's identity C(n,k)=C(n−1,k)+C(n−1,k−1) follows by separating subsets according to whether they contain a fixed element.

14.5 Inclusion–exclusion

For finite sets, |A∪B|=|A|+|B|−|A∩B|. For many sets, alternate sums of intersections correct repeated counting. The principle also counts permutations with forbidden fixed points.

14.6 Pigeonhole principle

If N objects are placed into k boxes, some box contains at least ceil(N/k) objects. Generalised forms prove that sufficiently large finite structures must contain repeated patterns.

14.7 Recurrence relations

A recurrence defines a sequence from earlier terms and initial conditions. Linear constant-coefficient recurrences are solved using characteristic polynomials; repeated roots introduce polynomial factors in n.

14.8 Generating functions

The ordinary generating function of (aₙ) is A(x)=Σaₙxⁿ. Products encode convolution and combinatorial constructions. Coefficients can be extracted algebraically or through partial fractions.

14.9 Catalan numbers and structures

Cₙ=(1/(n+1))C(2n,n) counts balanced parenthesis strings, binary tree shapes and several other recursively defined objects. Their recurrence is Cₙ=Σₖ₌₀ⁿ⁻¹ CₖCₙ₋₁₋ₖ.

14.10 Extremal and probabilistic counting

Extremal combinatorics asks how large or small a structure can be under constraints. Double counting evaluates one set of incidences in two ways. The probabilistic method proves existence by showing a random construction has positive probability of the desired property.

Chapter 15 — Probability Theory

Axioms, conditional probability, random variables, distributions, expectation, limit theorems and stochastic models. Probability theory provides a mathematical framework for uncertainty. It begins with a sample space and probability measure, then develops random variables, distributions, expectation, conditional models and asymptotic limit theorems.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the central objects precisely, state and apply key theorems with their hypotheses, construct rigorous derivations, solve unfamiliar problems and interpret mathematical results in context.

15.1 Probability spaces and axioms

A probability space (Ω,F,P) consists of outcomes Ω, a sigma-algebra F of events and a probability measure P. Kolmogorov axioms: P(A)≥0, P(Ω)=1 and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events. Consequences include P(Aᶜ)=1−P(A).

15.2 Counting probability and conditional probability

For equally likely finite outcomes, P(A)=|A|/|Ω|. Conditional probability P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B) when P(B)>0. Independence means P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B), equivalently P(A|B)=P(A) when defined.

15.3 Bayes' theorem and total probability

If B₁,…,Bₖ partition Ω, P(A)=ΣP(A|Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ). Bayes' theorem reverses conditioning: P(Bⱼ|A)=P(A|Bⱼ)P(Bⱼ)/P(A). Base rates must be included when interpreting diagnostic evidence.

15.4 Random variables and distributions

A random variable X is a measurable function from Ω to ℝ. Its cumulative distribution function F(x)=P(X≤x) is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and has limits 0 and 1 at infinities. Discrete variables use probability mass functions; continuous variables use densities.

15.5 Expectation, variance and moments

E[X]=Σx p(x) or ∫xf(x)dx. Var(X)=E[(X−μ)²]=E[X²]−μ². For constants a,b, E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b and Var(aX+b)=a²Var(X). Covariance measures linear association.

15.6 Standard discrete distributions

Bernoulli(p) has mean p and variance p(1−p). Binomial(n,p) counts successes in n independent trials. Geometric(p) counts trials to first success. Poisson(λ) models counts with mean and variance λ under its assumptions.

15.7 Standard continuous distributions

Uniform(a,b), exponential(λ), normal(μ,σ²) and gamma distributions model different structures. The exponential distribution is memoryless. Normal standardisation uses Z=(X−μ)/σ.

15.8 Joint distributions and conditional expectation

Joint distributions describe dependence. Marginals sum or integrate out variables. Independence requires factorisation of the joint law. Conditional expectation E[X|Y] is a random variable and satisfies the tower property E(E[X|Y])=E[X].

15.9 Laws of large numbers and central limit theorem

Under suitable assumptions, sample averages converge to the population mean (law of large numbers). The central limit theorem states standardised sums of independent identically distributed variables with finite variance converge in distribution to N(0,1).

15.10 Transformations and generating functions

For a monotone differentiable transformation Y=g(X), density transformation uses the Jacobian: fY(y)=fX(g⁻¹(y))|d g⁻¹/dy|. Moment-generating functions, when finite near zero, encode moments and identify distributions under standard conditions.

Chapter 16 — Mathematical Statistics

Estimation, sampling distributions, hypothesis testing, regression, ANOVA and statistical inference. Mathematical statistics develops methods for learning about populations from random samples. It combines probability models with estimation, uncertainty quantification, testing and predictive modelling. The distinction between a population parameter and a sample statistic is fundamental.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the principal concepts, state and apply relevant results with their assumptions, derive mathematical conclusions, solve multi-step problems and interpret results in their disciplinary context.

16.1 Populations, samples and sampling schemes

A population is the full collection under study; a sample is the observed subset. A statistic is a measurable function of sample data that contains no unknown parameters. Simple random sampling gives each sample of a fixed size equal selection probability. Stratified, cluster and systematic designs address different sampling structures.

16.2 Sampling distributions and standard errors

A sampling distribution is the probability law of a statistic under repeated sampling. The standard error is the standard deviation of that sampling distribution. For independent observations with variance σ², the sample mean has variance σ²/n and standard error σ/√n.

16.3 Point estimation and properties

An estimator is a statistic used to estimate a parameter. Bias is E[θ̂]−θ; variance measures dispersion; mean squared error is Var(θ̂)+Bias(θ̂)². Consistency concerns convergence in probability; efficiency compares variance among estimators under specified conditions.

16.4 Method of moments and maximum likelihood

Method-of-moments estimators equate sample moments to corresponding population moments. Maximum likelihood chooses parameter values maximising L(θ)=∏f(Xᵢ;θ), or log-likelihood ℓ(θ)=Σlog f(Xᵢ;θ). Check parameter constraints and boundary maxima.

16.5 Confidence intervals

A (1−α) confidence procedure has coverage 1−α under its model and sampling assumptions. A confidence interval is produced by a random procedure; after observing data, the parameter is fixed. For a normal mean with known σ, the interval is x̄±z₁₋α/₂ σ/√n.

16.6 Hypothesis testing and p-values

A test controls a Type I error probability α under the null. Type II error β is failure to reject a false null; power is 1−β. A p-value is the probability, assuming H₀, of a test statistic at least as extreme as observed. It is not P(H₀ is true).

16.7 Likelihood ratio and large-sample theory

Under regularity conditions, likelihood-ratio statistics often have asymptotic chi-square distributions. Maximum-likelihood estimators are commonly asymptotically normal with variance governed by Fisher information. Conditions matter, especially at parameter boundaries or under non-identifiability.

16.8 Comparing means and analysis of variance

Two-sample procedures compare population means under independent-sample assumptions. Paired designs analyse within-pair differences. One-way ANOVA decomposes total variability into between-group and within-group sums of squares; its F statistic compares mean squares.

16.9 Correlation and linear regression

The least-squares line minimises Σ(yᵢ−β̂₀−β̂₁xᵢ)². Under the classical linear model, errors have zero conditional mean and suitable variance and independence assumptions. Correlation measures linear association, not causation.

16.10 Nonparametric methods and diagnostics

Rank-based methods can reduce dependence on distributional assumptions. Residual plots assess nonlinearity, unequal variance and outliers; Q–Q plots assess distributional shape. Model diagnostics do not replace understanding of study design and data collection.

Chapter 17 — Numerical Analysis and Scientific Computing

Floating-point arithmetic, error analysis, root finding, interpolation, quadrature and numerical ODEs. Numerical analysis studies algorithms for approximating mathematical problems and quantifies their accuracy, stability and computational cost. A computed answer is meaningful only when its error and conditioning are understood.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the principal concepts, state and apply relevant results with their assumptions, derive mathematical conclusions, solve multi-step problems and interpret results in their disciplinary context.

17.1 Floating-point arithmetic and error

A floating-point system stores a finite number of significant digits and a bounded exponent. Rounding and cancellation create error. Absolute error is |x−x̂|; relative error is |x−x̂|/|x| for x≠0. Machine precision describes the scale of representable rounding errors.

17.2 Conditioning and stability

Conditioning is sensitivity of the mathematical problem to perturbations in input. Stability concerns how an algorithm propagates rounding and data errors. An ill-conditioned problem may defeat even a stable algorithm; an unstable algorithm may fail on a well-conditioned problem.

17.3 Bisection and fixed-point iteration

If continuous f changes sign on [a,b], bisection retains a sign-changing half-interval; after n steps its width is (b−a)/2ⁿ. Fixed-point iteration xₙ₊₁=g(xₙ) converges locally under suitable contraction conditions, such as |g′|≤k<1 on an invariant interval.

17.4 Newton and secant methods

Newton iteration is xₙ₊₁=xₙ−f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ), with quadratic local convergence near a simple root under regularity assumptions. The secant method approximates the derivative using two previous iterates and avoids explicit derivative evaluation.

17.5 Linear systems and iterative methods

Gaussian elimination solves dense systems directly; pivoting reduces numerical risk. Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel are iterative methods whose convergence depends on matrix properties. Residual r=b−Ax measures equation satisfaction but must be interpreted with conditioning.

17.6 Polynomial interpolation

Lagrange interpolation constructs the unique polynomial of degree at most n through n+1 distinct nodes. Newton divided differences allow incremental construction. High-degree equally spaced interpolation may oscillate; piecewise splines often behave better.

17.7 Numerical differentiation

Finite differences approximate derivatives. Forward difference has O(h) truncation error; centred difference typically has O(h²). Very small h amplifies rounding error, so a balance is required.

17.8 Numerical integration

Composite trapezoidal and Simpson rules approximate definite integrals. For sufficiently smooth f, composite trapezoidal error is O(h²) and composite Simpson error is O(h⁴). Adaptive quadrature refines intervals where estimated error is large.

17.9 Numerical solution of ODEs

Euler's method has global error O(h) under standard smoothness assumptions. Classical RK4 has global error O(h⁴). Stiff systems may require implicit methods; stability regions constrain allowable step sizes.

17.10 Reproducible scientific computing

A sound computational study records algorithm, tolerances, step size, precision, stopping criterion, software environment and validation tests. Compare against exact solutions, conserved quantities or independent methods where possible.

18.1 Mathematical formulation and feasible regions

A model specifies decision variables, an objective function and constraints. Feasible solutions satisfy all constraints; an optimum is a feasible solution with best objective value. Distinguish infeasibility, unboundedness and multiple optimal solutions.

18.2 Linear programming geometry

A linear programme has a linear objective and linear constraints. Its feasible region is a convex polyhedron. If a finite optimum exists, at least one optimal extreme point exists, although an entire face may be optimal.

18.3 Simplex method

The simplex method moves between adjacent basic feasible solutions while improving the objective. A tableau records basis, reduced costs and feasibility. Pivot selection, degeneracy, cycling and numerical tolerances are important implementation issues.

18.4 Duality and economic interpretation

Every linear programme has a dual. Weak duality bounds the primal objective; strong duality gives equal optimal values when finite optima exist. Dual variables often measure marginal value of relaxing constraints, subject to regularity and basis stability.

18.5 Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis studies changes in objective coefficients and right-hand sides. Shadow prices and allowable ranges are local to the current optimal basis; large changes may alter the basis and require re-optimisation.

18.6 Transportation and assignment problems

Transportation models allocate supply to demand at minimum shipping cost. Assignment is a special case with one-to-one allocation. Network structure permits specialised algorithms and integrality properties.

18.7 Integer and mixed-integer programming

Some decisions must be integer or binary. The LP relaxation provides bounds; branch-and-bound explores subproblems and cutting planes strengthen relaxations. Integer restrictions can make an otherwise convex-looking problem computationally difficult.

18.8 Network flows and shortest paths

A flow network has capacities and conservation at intermediate nodes. Max-flow min-cut equates maximum feasible flow with minimum cut capacity. Shortest-path algorithms require assumptions about edge weights; minimum-cost flow adds costs to flow decisions.

18.9 Dynamic programming

Dynamic programming decomposes a multistage problem using Bellman's principle of optimality. A recurrence relates the value of a state to feasible actions and successor states. State design controls computational tractability.

18.10 Queues, inventory and decision analysis

Queueing models analyse arrivals, service and waiting; inventory models balance ordering, holding and shortage costs. Decision analysis uses expected utility, scenarios and sensitivity to make uncertainty explicit.

19.1 Model-building cycle

19.2 Dimensional analysis

19.8 Parameter estimation and identifiability

19.9 Verification, validation and uncertainty

Chapter 20 — Topology and Metric Spaces

Metric spaces, open and closed sets, continuity, compactness, connectedness and completeness. Topology studies properties preserved under continuous deformation, while metric spaces add a notion of distance. These ideas generalise convergence and continuity beyond the real line and provide foundations for analysis and geometry.

20.1 Metric spaces and examples

A metric d:X×X→[0,∞) satisfies d(x,y)=0 iff x=y, symmetry and the triangle inequality. Euclidean, taxicab, maximum and discrete metrics provide different geometries on the same set.

20.3 Interior, closure and boundary

The interior is the largest open subset; closure is the smallest closed superset; boundary is closure minus interior. A set is dense if its closure is the whole space. Density depends on the ambient metric topology.

20.4 Convergence and Cauchy sequences

xₙ→x when d(xₙ,x)→0. A sequence is Cauchy when pairwise distances become arbitrarily small. Completeness means every Cauchy sequence converges in the space; ℚ with usual metric is not complete.

20.5 Continuity and homeomorphisms

f is continuous if preimages of open sets are open, equivalently ε–δ continuity in metric spaces. A homeomorphism is a bijection continuous in both directions; it preserves topological properties, not necessarily distances.

20.6 Compactness

A space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. In ℝⁿ with Euclidean topology, Heine–Borel says compact iff closed and bounded. Continuous images of compact spaces are compact; continuous real-valued functions on compact spaces attain extrema.

20.7 Connectedness and path connectedness

A space is connected if it cannot be split into two disjoint nonempty open subsets. Path connected means any two points can be joined by a continuous path. Path connected implies connected, but the converse need not hold.

20.8 Product and subspace topologies

A subspace inherits open sets by intersection with ambient open sets. Product topology is generated by products of open sets in the factors. These constructions allow local properties to be studied componentwise.

20.9 Contraction mappings

On a complete metric space, a contraction T with d(Tx,Ty)≤q d(x,y), 0≤q<1, has a unique fixed point. Iteration converges to it from any starting point. This theorem underlies many existence and numerical arguments.

20.10 Compactness, sequential properties and applications

In metric spaces, compactness is equivalent to sequential compactness and total boundedness plus completeness. These equivalences may fail in arbitrary topological spaces. Compactness supports uniform continuity and existence results.

Chapter 21 — Geometry and Differential Geometry

Euclidean and affine geometry, curves, surfaces, curvature, manifolds and geodesics. Geometry studies spatial structure, while differential geometry uses calculus to investigate smooth curves, surfaces and manifolds. It connects local differential quantities such as curvature with global shape and physical applications.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the principal concepts, state and apply relevant results with their assumptions, derive mathematical conclusions, solve multi-step problems and interpret results in their disciplinary context.

21.1 Euclidean and affine geometry

Euclidean geometry studies distances and angles under an inner product. Affine geometry retains incidence, collinearity and ratios along lines but not a preferred metric. Coordinate methods express geometric conditions as algebraic equations.

21.2 Vectors, inner products and transformations

The Euclidean inner product gives length and angle: cosθ=(u·v)/(||u||||v||). Orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and distances. Affine transformations preserve straight lines and ratios on a line.

21.3 Parametrised curves and arc length

A regular curve r(t) has r′(t)≠0. Its arc length is ∫||r′(t)||dt. Reparametrisation changes the parameter but not the geometric trace; arc-length parametrisation has unit speed.

21.4 Curvature and torsion

For a unit-speed space curve, curvature κ=||r″(s)||. For general parameter t, κ=||r′×r″||/||r′||³. Torsion measures departure from planarity and, when defined, τ=−B′·N.

21.5 Frenet–Serret frame

For a regular curve with nonzero curvature, tangent T, normal N and binormal B form an orthonormal frame. Derivatives with respect to arc length satisfy T′=κN, N′=−κT+τB, B′=−τN.

21.6 Surfaces and tangent planes

A regular surface may be parametrised r(u,v) with rᵤ×rᵥ≠0. Tangent vectors span its tangent plane; the normal is proportional to rᵤ×rᵥ. Implicit surfaces F(x,y,z)=0 have normal ∇F when nonzero.

21.7 First and second fundamental forms

The first fundamental form measures intrinsic lengths on a surface: E du²+2F du dv+G dv². The second fundamental form records normal curvature through coefficients e,f,g. Their combination defines principal curvatures and Gaussian curvature.

21.8 Gaussian and mean curvature

Principal curvatures k₁,k₂ yield Gaussian curvature K=k₁k₂ and mean curvature H=(k₁+k₂)/2. K is intrinsic by Gauss's Theorema Egregium; H depends on embedding and orientation convention.

21.9 Geodesics and shortest paths

Geodesics are curves with zero tangential acceleration, equivalently locally stationary length under suitable conditions. On a plane they are straight lines; on a sphere they are great circles. A geodesic need not be globally shortest between distant points.

21.10 Manifolds and coordinate charts

An n-dimensional smooth manifold is locally modelled on open subsets of ℝⁿ with compatible charts. Tangent spaces collect velocity directions; smooth maps have differentials. Coordinate changes allow local calculus on globally curved spaces.

Chapter 22 — Applied Mathematics and Mathematical Physics

Conservation laws, continuum models, waves, diffusion, potential theory, quantum and computational methods. Applied mathematics and mathematical physics use mathematical structures to describe physical systems. This chapter integrates differential equations, vector calculus, linear algebra, probability and numerical methods with conservation principles and physical interpretation.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to define the principal concepts, state and apply relevant results with their assumptions, derive mathematical conclusions, solve multi-step problems and interpret results in their disciplinary context.

22.1 Modelling principles and conservation laws

A conservation law expresses rate of change of a conserved quantity as net flux plus sources. Integral balances can be converted to local differential equations using divergence theorems when fields are sufficiently smooth.

22.2 Continuum mechanics and balance equations

Continuum models represent matter by fields such as density, velocity and stress. Mass conservation yields a continuity equation; momentum balance yields equations involving stress divergence and body forces. Constitutive laws close the system.

22.3 Classical wave propagation

The wave equation uₜₜ=c²Δu models small-amplitude waves in idealised media. Wave speed depends on material parameters. Boundary conditions and initial displacement and velocity determine modal amplitudes.

22.4 Diffusion and heat transfer

The diffusion equation uₜ=κΔu describes smoothing and transport down concentration or temperature gradients. Its solutions dissipate high-frequency modes. Boundary conditions represent insulation, fixed temperature or heat exchange.

22.5 Potential theory and electrostatics

An electrostatic potential V satisfies E=−∇V. In source-free regions ΔV=0; with charge density ρ, Poisson's equation is ΔV=−ρ/ε₀ under the usual electrostatic convention. Boundary conditions determine the potential.

22.6 Fluid mechanics and incompressibility

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, ∇·u=0 and the Navier–Stokes equations balance inertia, pressure, viscosity and body forces. Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous effects and guides regime analysis.

22.7 Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics

The Euler–Lagrange equation d/dt(∂L/∂q̇)−∂L/∂q=0 follows from stationary action. For L=T−V, it yields classical equations. Hamiltonian mechanics uses canonical coordinates and equations q̇=∂H/∂p, ṗ=−∂H/∂q.

22.8 Fourier and spectral methods in physics

Fourier transforms decompose fields into spatial frequencies. Differential operators become multiplication by frequency variables under suitable conventions, simplifying linear constant-coefficient PDEs. Spectral methods can achieve rapid convergence for smooth solutions.

22.9 Quantum and mathematical operator models

In elementary quantum mechanics, states are vectors in a Hilbert space and observables are represented by self-adjoint operators. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is iℏψₜ=Hψ. Boundary conditions and operator domains are mathematically significant.

22.10 Asymptotic methods and computational physics

Perturbation methods approximate solutions when a dimensionless parameter is small or large. Finite difference, finite element and spectral methods discretise governing equations. Convergence, stability, consistency and conservation properties guide numerical assessment.
