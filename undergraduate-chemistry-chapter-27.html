Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Start with the analytical question: identity, concentration, purity, structure or compliance. Define analyte, matrix, range, selectivity, detection limit, required uncertainty and throughput. Method choice should be fit for purpose; a highly sophisticated instrument is not automatically optimal.

A representative sample is essential. Design sampling for heterogeneity, location, time, sample size and preservation. Homogenisation, digestion, extraction, filtration, dilution and derivatisation can introduce contamination or analyte loss; document each step and maintain chain of custody.

Accuracy describes closeness to a reference value; precision describes agreement among replicate results. Random errors cause scatter, systematic errors cause bias. Use blanks, replicate analyses, certified reference materials, spikes and control charts to monitor performance.

Calibration relates instrument response to analyte amount. External standards are prepared separately; internal standards correct for variability in preparation or injection; standard addition can compensate for matrix effects. Examine residuals and avoid extrapolating beyond the validated range.

LOD is the lowest amount distinguishable from background under defined conditions; LOQ is the lowest amount measured with acceptable performance. Common estimates use blank variability and calibration slope, but method limits must be verified in the actual matrix.

Validation demonstrates fitness for purpose. Selectivity, linearity, range, accuracy, precision, LOD, LOQ, robustness and stability may be assessed. Quality assurance includes training, documented procedures, instrument maintenance, traceability and corrective action.

UV-visible spectroscopy measures electronic absorption. Quantification commonly uses A=εlc. Choose wavelength, cuvette, blank and concentration range carefully. Turbidity, speciation, stray light and spectral overlap can bias absorbance.

IR absorption requires a vibration that changes dipole moment; Raman scattering requires a change in polarisability. The techniques are complementary. Interpret functional-group regions and fingerprint regions while considering resolution, baseline, sample preparation and spectral libraries.

Atomic absorption measures absorption by free atoms; atomic emission measures light emitted by excited atoms or ions. Flame and furnace atomisation, ICP excitation and detector selection influence sensitivity. Correct for spectral, chemical and matrix interferences.

NMR chemical shifts, integration, multiplicity and coupling constants provide structural evidence. Quantitative NMR requires a suitable standard, calibrated pulse, sufficient relaxation delay and non-overlapping signals. Solvent, temperature and exchange can affect spectra.

Chromatography separates analytes through differing distributions between mobile and stationary phases. Retention, selectivity and efficiency control resolution. Optimise stationary-phase chemistry, mobile-phase composition, temperature and flow rather than relying only on longer run time.

GC suits volatile, thermally stable analytes or compounds made volatile by derivatisation. HPLC and UHPLC suit less volatile or thermally labile species. Detector choice (FID, UV, fluorescence, conductivity or MS) depends on analyte properties and matrix.

Mass spectrometry ionises molecules and separates ions by mass-to-charge ratio. EI, ESI and MALDI create ions through different mechanisms. Accurate mass, isotope patterns and fragmentation help identify compounds, but a spectrum alone may not uniquely identify an unknown.

Potentiometry measures potential at near-zero current; voltammetry records current as potential varies; amperometry measures current at fixed potential; conductometry measures ionic conductance. TGA tracks mass change with temperature; DSC tracks heat flow and transitions.

Chemometrics applies multivariate statistics to chemical data. PCA explores patterns and outliers; regression predicts properties from spectra or sensor arrays. Prevent overfitting through proper train/test separation, cross-validation and external validation; report preprocessing and scaling.
