Unit overview

Electromagnetic theory unifies electric and magnetic phenomena through fields and Maxwell's equations. The course develops the differential and integral formulations, constitutive relations, boundary conditions, electromagnetic potentials, wave propagation and energy transport. It emphasises how symmetry, conservation and material response shape real field systems.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Core textbook content

Electromagnetic theory unifies electric and magnetic phenomena through fields and Maxwell's equations. The course develops the differential and integral formulations, constitutive relations, boundary conditions, electromagnetic potentials, wave propagation and energy transport. It emphasises how symmetry, conservation and material response shape real field systems. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Electric field and Gauss's law

The electric field E is force per unit positive test charge in the limit that the test charge does not disturb the source distribution. Coulomb's law gives the field of point charges; superposition extends it to continuous charge distributions. Gauss's law states ∮E·dA=Q_enclosed/ε₀. It is always valid electrostatically, but it is most useful for finding E when symmetry makes the flux integral simple. Differentially, ∇·E=ρ/ε₀.

Worked example / application. A spherical Gaussian surface enclosing charge Q has E(4πr²)=Q/ε₀, giving E=Q/(4πε₀r²) radially outward for Q>0.

2. Electric potential and conservative fields

In electrostatics, ∇×E=0, so E can be written E=−∇V. Potential difference is the negative line integral of E along a path. Potential is scalar and is often easier to calculate than vector field; the field is recovered by differentiation. The zero of potential is a reference choice, often infinity for localised charge distributions. In conductors at electrostatic equilibrium, the interior field vanishes and the conductor is equipotential.

Worked example / application. For a point charge Q, V(r)=Q/(4πε₀r) relative to zero at infinity, and E=−dV/dr r̂=Q/(4πε₀r²)r̂.

3. Conductors, dielectrics and polarisation

Free charges in a conductor redistribute until electrostatic equilibrium is reached. Electric field inside an ideal conductor is zero; excess charge resides on its surface. Dielectrics polarise in an applied field, producing bound charge and modifying the internal field. The displacement field D=ε₀E+P is useful because ∇·D=ρ_free. In a linear isotropic dielectric P=ε₀χ_eE and D=εE. Real materials may be dispersive, anisotropic, nonlinear or lossy.

Worked example / application. A parallel-plate capacitor filled by a dielectric of relative permittivity ε_r has capacitance C=ε_rε₀A/d under ideal uniform-field assumptions.

4. Capacitance and electrostatic energy

Capacitance is C=Q/V for a linear two-terminal capacitor. For parallel plates C=εA/d when fringing is negligible. Stored energy is U=½CV²=½QV=Q²/(2C). The energy density in a linear dielectric is u=½E·D. The field description reveals that energy is stored in space, not simply on the metal plates. Dielectric breakdown limits the maximum operating field.

Worked example / application. A 10 μF capacitor charged to 12 V stores U=½(10×10⁻⁶)(12²)=7.2×10⁻⁴ J.

5. Steady currents and Ohm's law

Current density J describes charge flow per unit area. Charge conservation gives the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t+∇·J=0. In an ohmic isotropic conductor J=σE, where σ is conductivity; resistivity is ρ_e=1/σ. Microscopic drift, scattering and material structure determine conductivity. Ohm's law is a constitutive relation, not a universal identity for all materials or all fields. Boundary conditions require continuity of normal current in steady state when no charge accumulates at an interface.

Worked example / application. A wire of length 2.0 m, cross-section 1.0 mm² and resistivity 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ωm has R=ρL/A=0.034 Ω.

6. Magnetostatic fields and Ampère's law

Steady currents generate magnetic fields. The Biot–Savart law gives contributions from current elements, while Ampère's law ∮B·dl=μ₀I_enclosed is especially useful for symmetric systems. Differentially, ∇×B=μ₀J for magnetostatics. Magnetic field lines form closed loops because ∇·B=0; isolated magnetic monopoles have not been observed. Materials respond through magnetisation M, and H=B/μ₀−M is often useful in matter.

Worked example / application. For a long straight wire, symmetry gives B(2πr)=μ₀I, so B=μ₀I/(2πr), directed azimuthally by the right-hand rule.

7. Lorentz force and charged-particle motion

A charge q moving with velocity v experiences Lorentz force F=q(E+v×B). The magnetic part is perpendicular to velocity and does no work; it changes direction rather than speed. In uniform B with v perpendicular to B, the orbit is circular with radius r=mv/(|q|B) and cyclotron angular frequency ω=|q|B/m in the non-relativistic limit. Parallel velocity produces helical motion. Electric fields can change kinetic energy.

Worked example / application. An electron moving at 2.0×10⁶ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a 0.010 T field has radius r=mv/(eB)≈1.14×10⁻³ m.

8. Electromagnetic induction and Faraday's law

A changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force: ℰ=−dΦ_B/dt. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: induced effects oppose the change in flux. In integral form ∮E·dl=−d/dt∫B·dA; different choices of a moving circuit require careful treatment of motional and transformer contributions. Induction underlies generators, transformers, induction heating and many sensors.

Worked example / application. A 200-turn coil experiences flux per turn changing from 4.0×10⁻⁴ Wb to 1.0×10⁻⁴ Wb in 0.020 s. Mean induced emf magnitude is N|ΔΦ/Δt|=3.0 V.

9. Maxwell's equations: integral and differential forms

In vacuum SI units, Gauss's electric law is ∇·E=ρ/ε₀; Gauss's magnetic law is ∇·B=0; Faraday's law is ∇×E=−∂B/∂t; and Ampère–Maxwell law is ∇×B=μ₀J+μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. Integral forms express flux and circulation over surfaces and boundaries. The displacement-current term is required for charge conservation and predicts electromagnetic waves. The equations are local field laws and must be supplemented by material constitutive relations.

Worked example / application. Taking divergence of Ampère–Maxwell gives 0=μ₀∇·J+μ₀ε₀∂(∇·E)/∂t. Using Gauss's law yields ∂ρ/∂t+∇·J=0, the continuity equation.

10. Electromagnetic potentials and gauge freedom

Because ∇·B=0, one may write B=∇×A for a vector potential A. Faraday's law then permits E=−∇φ−∂A/∂t. Potentials are not unique: A→A+∇χ and φ→φ−∂χ/∂t leave E and B unchanged. Gauge choices such as Coulomb or Lorenz gauge simplify different problems. Potentials are mathematically convenient and also have physical significance in quantum phenomena such as the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

Worked example / application. In Lorenz gauge, ∇·A+(1/c²)∂φ/∂t=0, the scalar and vector potentials satisfy wave equations sourced by charge and current.

11. Electromagnetic waves in vacuum

Combining Maxwell's equations in source-free vacuum yields wave equations ∇²E−μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t²=0 and similarly for B. The wave speed is c=1/√(μ₀ε₀). Plane waves are transverse: E⊥B⊥k, and E=cB in vacuum for field amplitudes. The propagation direction follows E×B. Frequency and wavelength satisfy c=fλ. Electromagnetic waves transport energy and momentum without requiring a material medium.

Worked example / application. A radio wave at 100 MHz in vacuum has wavelength λ=c/f=3.0 m.

12. Waves in matter, reflection and transmission

In a linear medium, wave speed depends on permittivity and permeability: v=1/√(με) for a simple lossless medium. At an interface, tangential E and H and normal D and B obey boundary conditions determined by surface charge and current. Reflection and transmission depend on impedance and incidence angle. Conductivity causes attenuation; dispersion makes phase velocity frequency-dependent. A material's refractive index and impedance are distinct properties and both influence wave behaviour.

Worked example / application. For normal incidence between non-magnetic media of refractive indices n₁ and n₂, the electric-field reflection coefficient is r=(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂). Power reflectance is |r|² for lossless media.

13. Poynting theorem and electromagnetic energy

The Poynting vector S=E×H gives electromagnetic energy flux density. Poynting's theorem states that the decrease in field energy equals outward energy flux plus work done on charges, subject to the chosen macroscopic field definitions. In vacuum, energy density is u=½ε₀E²+B²/(2μ₀). The Poynting vector also relates to radiation pressure and momentum flow. In circuits, energy transfer is through electromagnetic fields around conductors, not simply by electrons carrying energy along the wire at drift speed.

Worked example / application. For a plane wave in vacuum with electric amplitude E₀, average intensity is I=½ε₀cE₀² for sinusoidal peak amplitude.

14. Boundary conditions and surface sources

Integrating Maxwell's equations across an infinitesimal pillbox or loop yields boundary conditions. The normal component of D jumps by free surface charge density; the normal component of B is continuous. The tangential component of E is continuous for ordinary stationary interfaces; tangential H jumps by surface current density. These relations are essential for capacitors, waveguides, antennas and layered materials. State the direction of the unit normal and distinguish free from bound sources.

Worked example / application. At a dielectric interface with no free surface charge, D₁n=D₂n. Since D=εE in each medium, normal electric field generally changes when permittivity changes.

15. Waveguides, transmission lines and radiation

Transmission lines guide electromagnetic energy when circuit dimensions are not negligible compared with wavelength. Distributed inductance and capacitance produce propagation, characteristic impedance and reflections from impedance mismatch. Waveguides support discrete modes and cutoff frequencies. Antennas convert guided currents into radiated fields; radiation depends on geometry, frequency and current distribution. The near field and far field have different spatial dependences and energy-flow characteristics.

Worked example / application. For a lossless transmission line, characteristic impedance Z₀=√(L′/C′), where L′ and C′ are inductance and capacitance per unit length.

16. Integrated problem: coaxial cable fields

A long coaxial cable has inner conductor radius a carrying current I and outer conductor carrying the return current. In the region a<r<b, Ampère's law gives Bφ=μ₀I/(2πr). If a potential difference V is applied and the dielectric permittivity is ε, the radial electric field is E_r=V/[r ln(b/a)]. The Poynting vector E×H points along the cable, showing energy transport in the dielectric region. Ideal expressions neglect conductor resistance, edge effects and dielectric loss.

Worked example / application. For a=1 mm, b=5 mm and I=2 A, B at r=2 mm is μ₀(2)/(2π·0.002)=2.0×10⁻⁴ T. The field circles the cable axis.
