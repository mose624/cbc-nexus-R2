Unit overview

Electromagnetic theory unifies electric and magnetic phenomena through fields and Maxwell's equations. The course develops the differential and integral formulations, constitutive relations, boundary conditions, electromagnetic potentials, wave propagation and energy transport. It emphasises how symmetry, conservation and material response shape real field systems.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Core textbook content

The course develops the differential and integral formulations, constitutive relations, boundary conditions, electromagnetic potentials, wave propagation and energy transport. It emphasises how symmetry, conservation and material response shape real field systems. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Electric field and Gauss's law

The electric field E is force per unit positive test charge in the limit that the test charge does not disturb the source distribution. Coulomb's law gives the field of point charges; superposition extends it to continuous charge distributions. Gauss's law states ∮E·dA=Q_enclosed/ε₀. It is always valid electrostatically, but it is most useful for finding E when symmetry makes the flux integral simple. Differentially, ∇·E=ρ/ε₀.

Worked example / application. A spherical Gaussian surface enclosing charge Q has E(4πr²)=Q/ε₀, giving E=Q/(4πε₀r²) radially outward for Q>0.

2. Electric potential and conservative fields

In electrostatics, ∇×E=0, so E can be written E=−∇V. Potential difference is the negative line integral of E along a path. Potential is scalar and is often easier to calculate than vector field; the field is recovered by differentiation. The zero of potential is a reference choice, often infinity for localised charge distributions. In conductors at electrostatic equilibrium, the interior field vanishes and the conductor is equipotential.

Worked example / application. For a point charge Q, V(r)=Q/(4πε₀r) relative to zero at infinity, and E=−dV/dr r̂=Q/(4πε₀r²)r̂.

3. Conductors, dielectrics and polarisation

Free charges in a conductor redistribute until electrostatic equilibrium is reached. Electric field inside an ideal conductor is zero; excess charge resides on its surface. Dielectrics polarise in an applied field, producing bound charge and modifying the internal field. The displacement field D=ε₀E+P is useful because ∇·D=ρ_free. In a linear isotropic dielectric P=ε₀χ_eE and D=εE. Real materials may be dispersive, anisotropic, nonlinear or lossy.

Worked example / application. A parallel-plate capacitor filled by a dielectric of relative permittivity ε_r has capacitance C=ε_rε₀A/d under ideal uniform-field assumptions.

4. Capacitance and electrostatic energy

Capacitance is C=Q/V for a linear two-terminal capacitor. For parallel plates C=εA/d when fringing is negligible. Stored energy is U=½CV²=½QV=Q²/(2C). The energy density in a linear dielectric is u=½E·D. The field description reveals that energy is stored in space, not simply on the metal plates. Dielectric breakdown limits the maximum operating field.

Worked example / application. A 10 μF capacitor charged to 12 V stores U=½(10×10⁻⁶)(12²)=7.2×10⁻⁴ J.

5. Steady currents and Ohm's law

Current density J describes charge flow per unit area. Charge conservation gives the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t+∇·J=0. In an ohmic isotropic conductor J=σE, where σ is conductivity; resistivity is ρ_e=1/σ. Microscopic drift, scattering and material structure determine conductivity. Ohm's law is a constitutive relation, not a universal identity for all materials or all fields. Boundary conditions require continuity of normal current in steady state when no charge accumulates at an interface.

Worked example / application. A wire of length 2.0 m, cross-section 1.0 mm² and resistivity 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ωm has R=ρL/A=0.034 Ω.

6. Magnetostatic fields and Ampère's law

Steady currents generate magnetic fields. The Biot–Savart law gives contributions from current elements, while Ampère's law ∮B·dl=μ₀I_enclosed is especially useful for symmetric systems. Differentially, ∇×B=μ₀J for magnetostatics. Magnetic field lines form closed loops because ∇·B=0; isolated magnetic monopoles have not been observed. Materials respond through magnetisation M, and H=B/μ₀−M is often useful in matter.

Worked example / application. For a long straight wire, symmetry gives B(2πr)=μ₀I, so B=μ₀I/(2πr), directed azimuthally by the right-hand rule.

7. Lorentz force and charged-particle motion

A charge q moving with velocity v experiences Lorentz force F=q(E+v×B). The magnetic part is perpendicular to velocity and does no work; it changes direction rather than speed. In uniform B with v perpendicular to B, the orbit is circular with radius r=mv/(|q|B) and cyclotron angular frequency ω=|q|B/m in the non-relativistic limit. Parallel velocity produces helical motion. Electric fields can change kinetic energy.

Worked example / application. An electron moving at 2.0×10⁶ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a 0.010 T field has radius r=mv/(eB)≈1.14×10⁻³ m.

8. Electromagnetic induction and Faraday's law

A changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force: ℰ=−dΦ_B/dt. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: induced effects oppose the change in flux. In integral form ∮E·dl=−d/dt∫B·dA; different choices of a moving circuit require careful treatment of motional and transformer contributions. Induction underlies generators, transformers, induction heating and many sensors.

Worked example / application. A 200-turn coil experiences flux per turn changing from 4.0×10⁻⁴ Wb to 1.0×10⁻⁴ Wb in 0.020 s. Mean induced emf magnitude is N|ΔΦ/Δt|=3.0 V.

9. Maxwell's equations: integral and differential forms

In vacuum SI units, Gauss's electric law is ∇·E=ρ/ε₀; Gauss's magnetic law is ∇·B=0; Faraday's law is ∇×E=−∂B/∂t; and Ampère–Maxwell law is ∇×B=μ₀J+μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. Integral forms express flux and circulation over surfaces and boundaries. The displacement-current term is required for charge conservation and predicts electromagnetic waves. The equations are local field laws and must be supplemented by material constitutive relations.

Worked example / application. Taking divergence of Ampère–Maxwell gives 0=μ₀∇·J+μ₀ε₀∂(∇·E)/∂t. Using Gauss's law yields ∂ρ/∂t+∇·J=0, the continuity equation.

10. Electromagnetic potentials and gauge freedom

Because ∇·B=0, one may write B=∇×A for a vector potential A. Faraday's law then permits E=−∇φ−∂A/∂t. Potentials are not unique: A→A+∇χ and φ→φ−∂χ/∂t leave E and B unchanged. Gauge choices such as Coulomb or Lorenz gauge simplify different problems. Potentials are mathematically convenient and also have physical significance in quantum phenomena such as the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

Worked example / application. In Lorenz gauge, ∇·A+(1/c²)∂φ/∂t=0, the scalar and vector potentials satisfy wave equations sourced by charge and current.

11. Electromagnetic waves in vacuum

Combining Maxwell's equations in source-free vacuum yields wave equations ∇²E−μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t²=0 and similarly for B. The wave speed is c=1/√(μ₀ε₀). Plane waves are transverse: E⊥B⊥k, and E=cB in vacuum for field amplitudes. The propagation direction follows E×B. Frequency and wavelength satisfy c=fλ. Electromagnetic waves transport energy and momentum without requiring a material medium.

Worked example / application. A radio wave at 100 MHz in vacuum has wavelength λ=c/f=3.0 m.

12. Waves in matter, reflection and transmission

In a linear medium, wave speed depends on permittivity and permeability: v=1/√(με) for a simple lossless medium. At an interface, tangential E and H and normal D and B obey boundary conditions determined by surface charge and current. Reflection and transmission depend on impedance and incidence angle. Conductivity causes attenuation; dispersion makes phase velocity frequency-dependent. A material's refractive index and impedance are distinct properties and both influence wave behaviour.

Worked example / application. For normal incidence between non-magnetic media of refractive indices n₁ and n₂, the electric-field reflection coefficient is r=(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂). Power reflectance is |r|² for lossless media.

13. Poynting theorem and electromagnetic energy

The Poynting vector S=E×H gives electromagnetic energy flux density. Poynting's theorem states that the decrease in field energy equals outward energy flux plus work done on charges, subject to the chosen macroscopic field definitions. In vacuum, energy density is u=½ε₀E²+B²/(2μ₀). The Poynting vector also relates to radiation pressure and momentum flow. In circuits, energy transfer is through electromagnetic fields around conductors, not simply by electrons carrying energy along the wire at drift speed.

Worked example / application. For a plane wave in vacuum with electric amplitude E₀, average intensity is I=½ε₀cE₀² for sinusoidal peak amplitude.

14. Boundary conditions and surface sources

Integrating Maxwell's equations across an infinitesimal pillbox or loop yields boundary conditions. The normal component of D jumps by free surface charge density; the normal component of B is continuous. The tangential component of E is continuous for ordinary stationary interfaces; tangential H jumps by surface current density. These relations are essential for capacitors, waveguides, antennas and layered materials. State the direction of the unit normal and distinguish free from bound sources.

Worked example / application. At a dielectric interface with no free surface charge, D₁n=D₂n. Since D=εE in each medium, normal electric field generally changes when permittivity changes.

15. Waveguides, transmission lines and radiation

Transmission lines guide electromagnetic energy when circuit dimensions are not negligible compared with wavelength. Distributed inductance and capacitance produce propagation, characteristic impedance and reflections from impedance mismatch. Waveguides support discrete modes and cutoff frequencies. Antennas convert guided currents into radiated fields; radiation depends on geometry, frequency and current distribution. The near field and far field have different spatial dependences and energy-flow characteristics.

Worked example / application. For a lossless transmission line, characteristic impedance Z₀=√(L′/C′), where L′ and C′ are inductance and capacitance per unit length.

16. Integrated problem: coaxial cable fields

A long coaxial cable has inner conductor radius a carrying current I and outer conductor carrying the return current. In the region a<r<b, Ampère's law gives Bφ=μ₀I/(2πr). If a potential difference V is applied and the dielectric permittivity is ε, the radial electric field is E_r=V/[r ln(b/a)]. The Poynting vector E×H points along the cable, showing energy transport in the dielectric region. Ideal expressions neglect conductor resistance, edge effects and dielectric loss.

Worked example / application. For a=1 mm, b=5 mm and I=2 A, B at r=2 mm is μ₀(2)/(2π·0.002)=2.0×10⁻⁴ T. The field circles the cable axis.

Advanced topic 1: Vector calculus and field geometry

A vector field assigns a direction and magnitude to every point. Divergence measures local source density and curl measures local circulation; gradient converts a scalar potential into a vector field.

For a differentiable field A, the divergence theorem relates the volume integral of ∇·A to the flux through the closed boundary. Stokes' theorem relates the surface integral of ∇×A to circulation around the boundary curve. These identities are the mathematical bridge between local differential laws and global integral laws.

In a numerical or analytical solution, choose Cartesian coordinates for planar geometry, cylindrical coordinates for wires and coaxial systems, and spherical coordinates for point sources and spherical boundaries. Symmetry can eliminate components before differentiation, reducing algebra and clarifying physical meaning.

Field plots must include axes, units, direction conventions and boundary geometry. A dense collection of field lines is not a quantitative measure unless line density has been defined consistently.

A common error is to confuse a zero divergence with a zero field. A uniform nonzero electric field has zero divergence in a charge-free region. Likewise, a zero curl does not imply zero field; it implies a conservative field locally under suitable topological conditions.

Advanced topic 2: Electrostatic potential and energy

Electrostatic fields are conservative in a simply connected region with no time-varying magnetic flux. The potential difference is the negative line integral of electric field, and the work done by the field on a charge q is q times the potential drop.

Starting from dW=qE·dl and defining ΔV=−∫E·dl gives ΔU=qΔV for the change in potential energy. Since E=−∇V, the electrostatic potential obeys Poisson's equation ∇²V=−ρ/ε in a homogeneous linear dielectric and Laplace's equation where ρ=0.

For point charges, superposition applies to potential as a scalar sum and to electric field as a vector sum. This distinction is useful: potential calculations often require less vector algebra, after which differentiation recovers the field.

Applications include high-voltage insulation, capacitive sensors, electrostatic precipitators, electron optics and energy storage. Equipotential surfaces meet electric field lines at right angles, because no work is done moving a test charge along an equipotential.

Do not set potential equal to zero everywhere merely because the field is zero at one point. Potential is defined relative to a reference, and a zero field means only that the local potential gradient vanishes.

Advanced topic 3: Gauss law and symmetry

Gauss's law states that total electric displacement flux through a closed surface equals enclosed free charge. It is valid for every closed surface, but its practical computational advantage depends on symmetry.

For a spherical charge distribution, choose a concentric sphere. Symmetry makes the radial field constant on the Gaussian surface, so E(4πr²)=Qenc/ε. For a long uniform line charge, a coaxial cylinder gives E(2πrL)=λL/ε. For an infinite sheet, a pillbox gives 2EA=σA/ε.

The method distinguishes the field inside and outside charged conductors, uniformly charged insulating spheres, cylinders and planes. Piecewise charge distributions require evaluating enclosed charge separately in each region.

When drawing a Gaussian surface, label its normal, dimensions, field direction and region. State which portions contribute to the flux and why. The electric field need not be perpendicular to every part of an arbitrary surface.

A frequent mistake is to assume Gauss's law only works for symmetric systems. The law is universal; symmetry is what makes the field easy to take outside the integral.

Advanced topic 4: Conductors and electrostatic boundary problems

In electrostatic equilibrium, free charges in a conductor have rearranged until the internal electric field vanishes. The conductor is equipotential, and excess free charge resides on its surface. The field immediately outside is normal to the surface in the ideal static model.

A thin pillbox across a conductor surface gives the normal-field condition Dn,out−Dn,in=ρs. Since the internal electrostatic field is zero, the external normal field is set by the surface charge density. A tangential field would drive surface charge and contradict equilibrium.

Sharp curvature can produce high local surface charge density and intense fields. This explains corona discharge near high-voltage conductors and why rounded electrodes and smooth insulation interfaces are important engineering measures.

Electrostatic shielding uses a conducting enclosure to make the field within its material zero; a cavity with no internal charge is shielded under electrostatic equilibrium. Practical shielding performance depends on apertures, seams, grounding and time-varying effects.

Do not apply the zero-field-inside result to every time-varying conductor. Real conductors have finite conductivity and skin depth; transient fields can penetrate and dissipate energy.

Advanced topic 5: Capacitance and dielectric energy

Capacitance is the ratio of charge magnitude to potential difference, C=Q/V, for a linear two-terminal geometry. It depends on geometry and permittivity, not on the charge stored in the ideal linear regime.

For parallel plates of area A and separation d, neglecting fringing, E=V/d and Q=εEA. Therefore C=εA/d. The work required to add charge dq at potential q/C is dU=(q/C)dq; integration from zero to Q gives U=Q²/(2C)=CV²/2.

A dielectric inserted between isolated plates increases capacitance and reduces voltage for fixed free charge. If the plates remain connected to a voltage source, charge flows and stored energy changes differently. The boundary condition and energy exchange with the source must be stated.

Capacitors are used in timing circuits, filters, power conditioning, sensing and pulsed-energy systems. Dielectric breakdown, leakage, equivalent series resistance and frequency dependence limit practical performance.

A common error is to say inserting a dielectric always decreases stored energy. The answer depends on whether charge or voltage is held constant and whether the source remains connected.

Advanced topic 6: Biot–Savart law and current distributions

The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field contribution from a steady current element. Its vector direction follows the cross product between current element and displacement direction, so geometry and orientation matter.

For a straight wire, integration of μ₀I dl×rhat/(4πr²) yields B=μ₀I/(2πr) for an infinitely long conductor. For a circular loop on its axis, symmetry cancels transverse components and the axial contributions add to give B=μ₀IR²/[2(R²+x²)^(3/2)] for a single turn.

The law is useful when current geometry is known and Ampère's law lacks sufficient symmetry. Superposition allows fields from several wires, loops or coils to be added vectorially.

Applications include electromagnets, Helmholtz coils, magnetic sensors and current probes. A pair of Helmholtz coils can create a relatively uniform field near their midpoint when separation equals coil radius.

The magnetostatic Biot–Savart expression assumes steady currents. Rapidly changing currents require retarded fields and a full time-dependent electromagnetic treatment.

Advanced topic 7: Ampère law and magnetic symmetry

Ampère's circuital law for magnetostatics states that the circulation of H around a closed contour equals the free current passing through a surface bounded by that contour. In vacuum, B=μ₀H.

For an infinitely long straight wire, a circular contour gives H(2πr)=I, hence B=μ₀I/(2πr). Inside a long solenoid, the field is approximately μ₀nI in vacuum, where n is turns per unit length. For an ideal toroid, H(2πr)=NI.

The best contour follows the symmetry of the field. The law is especially powerful for infinite wires, solenoids and toroids, where the field magnitude is constant along selected segments and zero or perpendicular on others.

Real coils have finite length, winding gaps, leakage flux and nonuniform fields near their ends. Measurements should distinguish the ideal central field from edge effects.

Do not use the magnetostatic form for arbitrary time-varying fields without including displacement current. Maxwell's correction is essential.

Advanced topic 8: Magnetic force and charged-particle motion

A charge q moving with velocity v in electric and magnetic fields experiences Lorentz force F=q(E+v×B). The magnetic component is perpendicular to velocity and therefore does no instantaneous work on a point charge.

For v perpendicular to a uniform B, equate magnetic force |q|vB to centripetal force mv²/r. The orbit radius is r=mv/(|q|B), and angular cyclotron frequency is |q|B/m in the nonrelativistic limit. An electric field can change kinetic energy; a static magnetic field changes direction.

Crossed electric and magnetic fields can select particle speeds. In a velocity selector, undeflected motion occurs when E=vB, giving v=E/B. This principle is used in mass spectrometers and charged-particle instruments.

Applications include mass analysis, particle accelerators, plasma confinement and electron-beam steering. Relativistic particles require momentum p rather than mv in the orbit-radius relation.

The sign of q determines the direction of curvature. Always draw v, B and q before assigning a force direction; the right-hand rule is for positive charge.

Advanced topic 9: Magnetic materials and hysteresis

Magnetic materials respond through microscopic magnetic moments and domain structure. Diamagnetic response opposes the applied field weakly; paramagnetic response aligns moments weakly; ferromagnets can develop strong spontaneous magnetisation and hysteresis.

In a linear isotropic material, B=μH. Ferromagnets are generally nonlinear and history-dependent, so a single constant μ is inadequate over a full magnetisation cycle. The area of a B–H hysteresis loop represents energy dissipated per unit volume per cycle under the stated field convention.

Soft magnetic materials have narrow hysteresis loops and are preferred for transformer cores; hard magnetic materials retain magnetisation and are used for permanent magnets. Laminations reduce eddy-current losses, while suitable alloys reduce hysteresis loss.

The temperature dependence of magnetic order is important. Above the Curie temperature, a ferromagnet loses long-range spontaneous magnetisation and becomes paramagnetic.

Do not confuse permeability with conductivity. Permeability describes magnetic constitutive response; conductivity controls conduction current and strongly influences skin depth.

Advanced topic 10: Faraday induction and Lenz law

Faraday's law states that the induced emf around a closed path equals the negative time derivative of magnetic flux through the path. Flux is the surface integral of B over an oriented surface.

For a coil of N turns, flux linkage is NΦ and emf is −d(NΦ)/dt. The flux can change because B changes, the coil area changes, the orientation changes, or the circuit moves through a nonuniform field. The negative sign expresses opposition to the change in flux linkage.

In motional emf, charges in a moving conductor experience magnetic Lorentz force. For a straight rod of length ℓ moving perpendicular to B and its length, the magnitude is Bℓv. This result is consistent with the flux rule for the moving circuit.

Applications include generators, induction cooktops, dynamic microphones, magnetic braking, metal detectors and inductive sensors. The direction of induced current must be established from Lenz's law and circuit orientation.

Lenz's law does not say the induced field always opposes the original field. It opposes the change in magnetic flux; when flux is decreasing, the induced field can reinforce the original field.

Advanced topic 11: Inductors and magnetic circuits

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field and opposes changes in current. Its inductance depends on flux linkage per current in a linear regime. A magnetic circuit model uses magnetomotive force NI, reluctance and flux as approximate analogues of voltage, resistance and current.

For a uniform core of length ℓ, area A and permeability μ, reluctance is ℓ/(μA), and flux is approximately NI divided by reluctance. This model neglects fringing, leakage, nonlinear saturation and hysteresis. Air gaps often dominate reluctance because their permeability is much lower than that of a ferromagnetic core.

Advanced topic 12: Maxwell displacement current

The displacement-current density is the time derivative of electric displacement. It is not generally a conduction current of mobile charges crossing a dielectric, but it contributes to magnetic circulation in the same way required by the field equations.

Taking the divergence of Ampère–Maxwell law and using Gauss's law yields the continuity equation. This derivation shows that the displacement term is not an optional correction: it is necessary for consistency with local charge conservation when charge density changes.

For a charging parallel-plate capacitor, the displacement current in the gap equals the conduction current in the leads in the ideal lumped model. The electric field between plates changes as charge accumulates, and its displacement flux changes at the same rate.

The concept is central to electromagnetic wave propagation, capacitors, high-frequency circuits and the consistency of field theory. It also illustrates that electromagnetic fields carry dynamics through regions without conduction current.

Avoid describing displacement current as electrons flowing through vacuum. It is a term in the field equation associated with changing electric displacement.

Advanced topic 13: Maxwell equations and conservation

Maxwell's equations link sources and fields through divergence and curl. They also encode charge conservation, magnetic flux continuity and electromagnetic induction. The equations are mutually constrained rather than four unrelated statements.

Gauss's law relates charge density to electric displacement divergence. The magnetic divergence equation excludes magnetic monopole sources in the classical model. Faraday's law couples changing B to circulating E. Ampère–Maxwell law couples conduction current and changing D to circulating H.

Integral forms are useful for flux and circulation over finite geometries; differential forms are useful for local field behaviour. The divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem provide the conversion between them.

Conservation laws provide independent checks on numerical models. A computed solution that violates charge continuity or energy balance may be under-resolved or incorrectly implemented.

When quoting equations, identify whether sources are free or total and whether fields are microscopic or macroscopic. The D/H formulation is particularly useful in matter.

Advanced topic 14: Scalar and vector electromagnetic potentials

The magnetic field can be written as the curl of a vector potential A because its divergence vanishes. Faraday's law then permits the electric field to be written as the negative gradient of scalar potential V minus the time derivative of A.

Gauge transformations A′=A+∇χ and V′=V−∂χ/∂t leave E and B unchanged. In the Lorenz gauge, the potentials satisfy inhomogeneous wave equations with charge and current sources. In the Coulomb gauge, the divergence of A is zero.

Potentials can simplify radiation calculations, boundary-value problems and computational formulations. In quantum physics, potentials also enter phase evolution, making gauge structure more than a classical mathematical convenience.

A useful derivation begins with B=∇×A, substitutes into Faraday's law, and notes that a curl-free vector field can be represented locally as a scalar gradient. This produces the potential expression for E.

Do not claim that potentials are unique physical fields in the same way E and B are. Different gauge choices can describe the same observable electromagnetic fields.

Advanced topic 15: Electromagnetic wave derivation

In a source-free vacuum, the divergence equations make E and B transverse in a plane wave. Taking the curl of Faraday's law and substituting Ampère–Maxwell law produces the vector wave equation.

Advanced topic 16: Waves in dielectrics and conductors

In a simple lossless dielectric, wave speed is 1/√(με), refractive index is c/v and intrinsic impedance is √(μ/ε). In conductors, conduction current causes attenuation and field penetration is limited by skin depth.

Advanced topic 17: Polarisation and optical interfaces

Polarisation describes the orientation and time evolution of the transverse electric field. Two orthogonal components with different amplitudes and phase difference produce linear, circular or elliptical polarisation.

An ideal polariser projects the incident electric field onto its transmission axis. For already linearly polarised light, Malus's law gives transmitted intensity proportional to cos squared of the angle. At Brewster incidence, the reflected p-polarised component vanishes for an ideal lossless dielectric interface.

Polarisation is used in glare reduction, stress analysis, LCD displays, remote sensing, microscopy, optical communication and material characterisation. Birefringent crystals introduce phase retardation between orthogonal components.

A practical experiment uses two polarising sheets, a photodetector and a rotating analyser. Plot intensity against analyser angle and compare with a cos² model, including background offset and finite extinction ratio.

Advanced topic 18: Poynting theorem and field energy

The Poynting vector E×H describes electromagnetic power flow per unit area. Poynting's theorem states that outward energy flux plus the rate of change of stored field energy equals negative work done by fields on charges in the volume.

The derivation combines the dot products of E with Ampère–Maxwell law and H with Faraday's law, then applies the vector identity for divergence of a cross product. For linear nondispersive media, energy density is one-half E·D plus one-half B·H.

The theorem explains power flow in cables, waveguides, antennas, transformers and radiating systems. It also shows how electromagnetic energy becomes heat in a conductor through J·E.

For sinusoidal steady state, use the time-average Poynting vector and state whether phasors are peak or RMS. A factor of one-half appears for peak complex amplitudes.

A common error is to treat the Poynting vector as merely a direction indicator. Its magnitude is an energy flux density with units W/m².

Advanced topic 19: Radiation momentum and pressure

Electromagnetic radiation carries momentum. In vacuum, momentum density is S/c², and a pulse of energy U has momentum U/c. Absorption and reflection transfer momentum to matter.

Radiation pressure follows from momentum flux. For normally incident radiation absorbed completely, pressure is I/c; for ideal reflection it is 2I/c. Partial absorption and oblique incidence require accounting for the normal component of momentum transfer.

Solar sails, optical tweezers, laser cooling and precision force measurements exploit radiation momentum. Although the force from ordinary sunlight is small, a large area and long exposure can create a measurable acceleration.

The photon model gives the same relation because each photon has energy hν and momentum hν/c. A beam's photon rate and energy flux therefore determine its momentum flux.

Do not confuse radiation pressure with gas pressure. Radiation pressure arises from momentum transfer by electromagnetic energy, not molecular collisions.

Advanced topic 20: Boundary conditions and interfaces

Boundary conditions follow from Maxwell's integral laws applied to a vanishingly thin pillbox or loop straddling an interface. They specify which field components are continuous and which may jump because of surface sources.

The normal displacement jump equals free surface charge; normal B is continuous. Tangential E is continuous for finite fields in the limiting loop, while tangential H jumps by the free surface-current density. These relations depend on the selected normal orientation.

Boundary conditions determine reflection, refraction, surface waves, waveguide modes and fields near electrodes. They must be imposed together with material constitutive relations and source conditions.

For a perfect conductor, tangential E at the surface is zero in the ideal model. In a dielectric interface without free surface charge or current, the appropriate normal and tangential components are continuous.

Do not assume the entire vector E or H is continuous across every boundary. Surface charge and surface current produce permitted discontinuities.

Advanced topic 21: Induction devices and transformer action

A generator uses mechanical motion to change magnetic flux through a coil. A transformer uses time-varying flux created by a primary winding to induce voltage in a secondary winding. Both are consequences of Faraday's law.

For a rotating N-turn coil in uniform B, flux linkage is NBA cosωt and induced emf has peak NBAω. For an ideal transformer, voltage ratio equals turns ratio, while current ratio is inverse so that input and output powers balance.

Real transformers have winding resistance, leakage inductance, core hysteresis and eddy-current losses. Laminated cores interrupt large eddy-current loops, and high-resistivity magnetic materials reduce heating.

Applications include power generation, grid voltage conversion, induction heating, wireless charging and current transformers. Transformer design balances core flux, frequency, turns, conductor area and insulation.

Advanced topic 22: AC response and resonance

Advanced topic 23: Transmission lines and reflections

A transmission line has distributed inductance and capacitance, so voltage and current propagate as waves. Characteristic impedance is √(L′/C′) in an ideal lossless line, and wave speed is 1/√(L′C′).

At a load, mismatch produces a reflected wave with voltage coefficient (ZL−Z₀)/(ZL+Z₀). A matched load has zero reflection. The voltage standing-wave ratio quantifies the standing-wave envelope created by incident and reflected waves.

Coaxial cable, microstrip, twin-lead and twisted-pair systems are common transmission-line geometries. Their field distributions and losses depend on conductor geometry, dielectric and frequency.

Impedance matching is essential in RF systems, high-speed digital interconnects, radar, satellite communication and laboratory instrumentation. Time-domain reflectometry can locate impedance discontinuities from reflected pulses.

Advanced topic 24: Antennas and radiation

Advanced topic 25: Waveguides and resonant cavities

Waveguides confine electromagnetic fields and support modes determined by boundary conditions. Metallic rectangular guides support TE and TM modes with frequency-dependent cutoff; hollow single-conductor guides do not support a TEM mode.

For a rectangular guide, cutoff frequency depends on transverse dimensions and mode indices. Above cutoff, a mode propagates; below cutoff, it decays evanescently. Cavities impose standing-wave boundary conditions in all three dimensions and have discrete resonant frequencies.

Waveguides and cavities are used in microwave links, radar, particle accelerators, filters, resonators and precision spectroscopy. Finite conductivity and dielectric losses determine attenuation and quality factor.

Mode identification requires specifying longitudinal field components and indices. The dominant mode is the one with the lowest cutoff frequency allowed by the geometry.

Advanced topic 26: Relativistic field transformations

Electric and magnetic fields are observer-dependent components of a unified electromagnetic field. A change of inertial frame mixes E and B while preserving the physical predictions of the Lorentz force law.

For a boost, parallel components remain in their corresponding directions while perpendicular components mix with factors involving γ and v×B or v×E/c². The exact signs depend on whether the transformation maps fields into a frame moving with +v or −v.

Advanced topic 27: Dielectric and magnetic constitutive response

Advanced topic 29: Numerical field computation

Numerical methods solve field equations in geometries where closed-form analytical solutions are unavailable. Finite differences approximate derivatives on grids; finite elements use local basis functions; finite volumes enforce flux balance over cells.

For electrostatic potential in a charge-free two-dimensional region, the five-point Laplace stencil sets each interior grid value equal to the average of its four nearest neighbours. Poisson's equation adds a source term. Boundary values encode electrodes, conductors and interfaces.

Time-domain electromagnetic solvers update E and H fields on staggered grids. Stability requires a time step satisfying a Courant condition. Absorbing layers reduce artificial reflections from finite computational boundaries.

Verification includes mesh refinement, time-step refinement, conservation checks, comparison with known analytical cases and solver residuals. A visually smooth field plot alone does not establish accuracy.

Advanced topic 30: Electromagnetic safety and exposure

Electromagnetic safety depends on frequency, field strength, exposure duration, coupling geometry and the biological or equipment context. Static magnetic fields, low-frequency fields, RF fields and ionising radiation have different interaction mechanisms.

In conductors and biological tissue, time-varying fields can induce currents and heating. RF exposure assessment considers absorbed power and spatial distribution, while strong static magnetic fields create projectile and implant hazards. Laboratory controls include barriers, interlocks, current limits, insulated leads, warning signs, controlled access and emergency shutdown. Strong magnets must be kept away from ferromagnetic objects and incompatible medical devices.

Engineering design follows applicable exposure standards and equipment regulations, with risk assessment appropriate to the frequency and application. Measurements should use calibrated instruments with suitable bandwidth and probe geometry.
