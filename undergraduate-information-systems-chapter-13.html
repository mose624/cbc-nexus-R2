Foundations of IT governance

IT governance is the system of leadership, organisational structures and processes that ensure information technology supports enterprise objectives, creates value and manages risk. It clarifies who makes technology decisions, who is accountable for outcomes and how performance is monitored. Governance is not simply an IT department responsibility: boards, executives, business owners, technology leaders, risk specialists and users all have roles.

Governance connects business strategy with technology investment and operational delivery. It establishes decision rights for architecture, data, cybersecurity, sourcing, applications and digital transformation. Effective governance prevents isolated projects from creating incompatible systems, uncontrolled expenditure or unacceptable exposure. It also recognises that technology decisions affect customers, employees, suppliers and the public.

Governance, management and administration

Governance evaluates stakeholder needs, sets direction and monitors performance. Management plans, builds, runs and monitors activities in accordance with that direction. Administration performs operational tasks such as account provisioning, system configuration, records maintenance and routine reporting. These functions interact but should not be confused.

A board may approve risk appetite and strategic priorities; an executive committee may allocate investment; an IT service manager may coordinate delivery; and administrators may implement access controls. Clear separation supports accountability and reduces conflicts of interest. In small organisations, one person may perform several roles, but review and approval controls should still be designed.

Business alignment and value realisation

IT-business alignment means that technology capabilities, investments and services support organisational strategy and measurable outcomes. Alignment is continuous because markets, regulation, user expectations and technology change. A project can meet its technical specification yet fail to create value if it does not solve the business problem or achieve adoption.

Value realisation begins with a defined business case, baseline measures, expected benefits, owners and a review schedule. Benefits may include improved service quality, lower operating cost, faster processing, better decisions, resilience or new revenue. Track both financial and non-financial outcomes and distinguish outputs delivered from benefits actually achieved.

Governance frameworks and standards

Frameworks provide structured guidance for governance, risk, control, service management and security. COBIT is commonly used to govern and manage enterprise information and technology; ITIL provides practices for IT service management; ISO/IEC 38500 addresses governance of IT; ISO/IEC 20000 concerns service management systems; and ISO/IEC 27001 specifies requirements for an information security management system.

A framework is not a substitute for judgement. Organisations should select practices according to size, complexity, legal duties, threat exposure and service criticality. A useful implementation adapts the framework to actual processes, assigns accountable owners and measures whether controls work. Copying a large organisation's documentation into a small organisation can create bureaucracy without improving outcomes.

COBIT principles and governance system

COBIT provides a framework for enterprise governance and management of information and technology. It helps organisations connect stakeholder needs, enterprise goals, alignment goals, processes, organisational structures, information flows, people, services and infrastructure. It distinguishes governance objectives from management objectives and supports performance assessment.

COBIT implementation should begin with enterprise context and priority issues. A governance design considers decision structures, policies, information, culture, skills and technology. Capability assessments can identify gaps and guide improvement, but a maturity score alone does not demonstrate effectiveness. Evidence should show that activities achieve intended outcomes and risks are controlled.

IT strategy and enterprise architecture governance

IT strategy defines how technology capabilities will support the organisation over time. Enterprise architecture describes the relationships among business capabilities, processes, information, applications, technology platforms and standards. Architecture governance helps ensure that projects fit the target direction while allowing justified exceptions.

Architecture principles might require interoperability, reuse, data ownership, security by design and approved integration patterns. An architecture review board evaluates significant designs, dependencies, technical debt and exceptions. The review should be timely and proportionate: excessive approval delays can encourage teams to bypass governance rather than improve design quality.

Decision rights, committees and accountability

Decision rights specify who may propose, approve, execute, review or challenge a decision. Common governance bodies include board risk committees, executive technology committees, architecture boards, data governance councils and change advisory groups. Each body needs a defined mandate, membership, quorum, escalation route and record of decisions.

A RACI matrix can clarify who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for an activity. It should normally identify one accountable role for each decision. Committees should not diffuse accountability: the named executive or service owner remains responsible for ensuring that decisions are implemented and outcomes reviewed.

IT policies, standards, procedures and guidelines

Policies express management intent and mandatory rules, such as acceptable use, access control, data classification, incident reporting and supplier security. Standards define specific requirements; procedures explain repeatable steps; guidelines recommend good practice. These documents should be consistent, accessible and approved by appropriate authorities.

Policy governance includes ownership, version control, communication, staff acknowledgement, exception handling and periodic review. Requirements should be testable. IT risk management and risk appetite

IT risk is the possibility that technology-related events affect objectives. Risks include cyberattack, service outage, data loss, privacy breach, project failure, supplier disruption, skills shortages, poor data quality and technology obsolescence. Risk management identifies, analyses, evaluates, treats and monitors risks within the organisation's appetite and tolerance.

A risk register records a clear cause-event-effect statement, likelihood, impact, inherent risk, existing controls, residual risk, owner, treatment and due date. Risk scoring supports prioritisation but should not create false precision. Critical risks require escalation and active monitoring, especially where consequences could affect safety, legal compliance or essential services.

Internal control principles and control design

Internal controls are policies, procedures and activities designed to provide reasonable assurance that objectives are achieved, reporting is reliable, operations are effective and obligations are met. Preventive controls aim to stop unwanted events; detective controls identify them; corrective controls restore or remediate after an event.

Control design should address a defined risk and be proportionate to its impact. Examples include segregation of duties, approval limits, access restrictions, reconciliations, backups, logging, change approvals and independent review. A control that exists only on paper is not effective; organisations need evidence that it is performed consistently and exceptions are resolved.

IT audit: purpose, scope and independence

IT audit is an independent and systematic examination of technology-related controls, systems, processes and evidence. It assesses whether controls are suitably designed and operating effectively, whether information is reliable, and whether systems comply with relevant requirements. Audits may be internal, external, regulatory, financial, operational or specialised security reviews.

Audit independence is supported through objective reporting lines, freedom from operational responsibility for the area being audited and disclosure of conflicts. Auditors should define objectives, scope, criteria, period, locations, systems and limitations. Management remains responsible for controls and remediation; auditors provide assurance and recommendations rather than owning the process.

Risk-based IT audit planning

Risk-based audit planning directs effort toward areas with the greatest significance to organisational objectives. Inputs include enterprise risk assessments, prior audit findings, major changes, incidents, regulatory obligations, outsourced services and management concerns. Audit plans should consider both likelihood and consequence, as well as control maturity and the reliability of previous evidence.

A risk-based plan is flexible. A major cloud migration, acquisition, ransomware incident or new payment platform may require an audit outside the original annual schedule. Audit committees should understand coverage gaps and resource constraints. Lower-risk areas may receive periodic review, while critical systems and high-impact controls may require more frequent assurance.

IT audit lifecycle and evidence

An audit typically moves through planning, understanding the process, risk and control assessment, testing, evaluation, reporting and follow-up. Evidence may include system configurations, access listings, logs, tickets, contracts, policies, interviews, observations, reconciliations and reperformance. Evidence should be relevant, reliable, sufficient and traceable to the audit objective.

Auditors document procedures, samples, results, exceptions and conclusions. Inquiry alone is usually weaker than corroborated evidence. For example, a manager's statement that access is reviewed quarterly should be supported by dated review records, evidence of challenge and proof that inappropriate access was removed. Protect audit evidence because it may contain confidential or personal information.

IT general controls and application controls

IT general controls support the reliable operation of systems and include access management, change management, operations, backup and recovery, system development and security administration. Application controls operate within specific applications and may include input validation, processing checks, authorisation, calculations, interface controls and output review.

Weak general controls can undermine reliance on application controls. If unauthorised users can change application code or database records, otherwise well-designed transaction checks may be bypassed. Auditors assess the relationship between general and application controls and tailor substantive testing where control reliance is not justified.

Identity, access and privileged account governance

Access governance ensures that users receive appropriate permissions for legitimate duties and that access is reviewed, changed and removed when circumstances change. Core principles include least privilege, need to know, unique identities, strong authentication, segregation of duties and periodic recertification.

Privileged accounts require additional safeguards because they can alter systems, data and security settings. Use controlled elevation, separate administrator accounts, multi-factor authentication, session logging, emergency access procedures and independent review. Joiner-mover-leaver processes should connect HR events to timely account changes and retain evidence of approvals.

Change management and configuration governance

Change management controls modifications to applications, infrastructure, configurations and data. It assesses business need, risk, testing, approval, scheduling, communication, rollback and post-implementation review. Emergency changes need a rapid but documented path and retrospective review to ensure that urgency does not become a permanent bypass.

Configuration management maintains reliable information about components, versions, relationships and dependencies. A configuration management database or equivalent inventory can support incident diagnosis, impact assessment, asset lifecycle planning and audit. Data quality matters: an inaccurate inventory may create a false sense of control.

IT service management and the service lifecycle

IT Service Management (ITSM) organises people, processes, partners and technology to deliver and support services that create value. Service management considers the complete lifecycle from demand and design through transition, operation, improvement and retirement. A service is more than a technical component: it enables an outcome for a customer or user.

Service definitions should identify customers, users, outcomes, service hours, dependencies, support routes, service levels and responsibilities. Service catalogues help users understand what is available and how to request it. Good service management balances reliability, cost, speed, security and experience rather than optimising one measure in isolation.

Incident, problem and service request management

An incident is an unplanned interruption or reduction in service quality. Incident management aims to restore normal service quickly and minimise impact. A problem is a cause or potential cause of one or more incidents; problem management investigates causes and reduces recurrence. A service request is a predefined user request such as access, information or standard equipment.

Incident prioritisation commonly considers impact and urgency. Communication should tell affected users what is known, what work is underway and when the next update will arrive. Major incidents require coordinated leadership, technical response, stakeholder communication and a post-incident review focused on learning rather than blame.

Change enablement, release and deployment management

Change enablement seeks to maximise successful changes by assessing risk, authorising appropriate changes and managing schedules. Standard changes are low-risk and pre-authorised when criteria are met; normal changes receive assessment and approval; emergency changes use an expedited route with appropriate controls.

Release management makes new or changed services and features available for use, while deployment moves components into target environments. Use version control, automated testing, separation of duties, release notes, rollback planning and post-deployment verification. Measure change success through failure rate, rework, incidents and time to restore service.

Business continuity and disaster recovery

Business continuity management prepares an organisation to maintain or restore priority activities during disruption. Disaster recovery focuses on restoring technology services, infrastructure and data after a disruptive event. Both require business impact analysis, risk assessment, recovery strategies, plans, assigned roles, communication and exercises.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is the target time within which a service should be restored. Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is the maximum targeted data-loss interval measured in time. A service with a short RTO and RPO may require replication and resilient architecture, while a less critical service may use less costly recovery arrangements.

University examination and revision questions

Answer using precise definitions, relevant frameworks, evidence-based examples and clear calculations where required.

Question 1. Define IT governance.

Model answer: The leadership, organisational structures and processes that direct and monitor IT so it supports enterprise objectives, creates value and manages risk.

Question 2. Distinguish governance from management.

Model answer: Governance evaluates needs, sets direction and monitors; management plans, builds, runs and monitors activities within that direction.

Question 3. What is IT-business alignment?

Model answer: Ensuring technology capabilities and investments support organisational strategy and measurable business outcomes.

Question 4. Name three frameworks relevant to IT governance or service management.

Model answer: COBIT, ITIL, ISO/IEC 38500, ISO/IEC 20000 and ISO/IEC 27001.

Question 5. What is a RACI matrix?

Model answer: A responsibility assignment tool identifying who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed.

Question 6. Define risk appetite.

Model answer: The amount and type of risk an organisation is willing to pursue or retain while achieving its objectives.

Question 7. Differentiate preventive, detective and corrective controls.

Model answer: Preventive controls stop events; detective controls identify them; corrective controls restore or remediate after occurrence.

Question 8. What is IT audit?

Model answer: An independent examination of IT-related controls, systems, processes and evidence against defined criteria.

Question 9. Why is auditor independence important?

Model answer: It supports objective conclusions and reduces conflicts that could impair assurance.

Question 10. List the main stages of an IT audit.

Model answer: Planning, understanding, risk assessment, control testing, evaluation, reporting and follow-up.

Question 11. What are IT general controls?

Model answer: Controls over access, change, operations, security, backup and development that support reliable IT environments.

Question 12. Give four examples of application controls.

Model answer: Input validation, transaction authorisation, processing checks, interface reconciliation and output review.

Question 13. Explain least privilege.

Model answer: Users receive only the minimum access needed for their authorised duties.

Question 14. What is change management?

Model answer: A controlled process for assessing, approving, testing, scheduling and reviewing modifications to services and systems.

Question 15. Define IT Service Management.

Model answer: The organisational capabilities and practices used to deliver, support and improve IT-enabled services.

Question 16. Differentiate an incident and a problem.

Model answer: An incident is an interruption or reduction in service quality; a problem is a cause or potential cause of incidents.

Question 17. What is a service request?

Model answer: A predefined user request such as information, access or a standard service action.

Question 18. Define an SLA.

Model answer: An agreement documenting service expectations, measures, responsibilities and escalation arrangements.

Question 19. What is a business impact analysis?

Model answer: A process for identifying critical activities and assessing the consequences of disruption over time.

Question 20. Differentiate RTO and RPO.

Model answer: RTO is the target time to restore a service; RPO is the maximum targeted data-loss interval.

Question 21. Why does cloud outsourcing not remove customer accountability?

Model answer: The customer retains responsibility for controls and obligations assigned to its role under the service and shared-responsibility model.

Question 22. What is data governance?

Model answer: Decision rights and accountability for data quality, ownership, access, classification, lineage, retention and use.

Question 23. What should an audit finding contain?

Model answer: The condition, criterion, cause, consequence or risk, evidence and recommended corrective action.

Question 24. Why is follow-up audit testing necessary?

Model answer: It verifies that remediation is implemented and actually reduces the identified risk.

Question 25. Give two leading and two lagging IT governance indicators.

Model answer: Leading: timely access reviews and completed recovery tests. Lagging: confirmed security incidents and service outages.

Question 26. A service was available for 7,182 minutes in a 7,200-minute month. Calculate availability.

Model answer: Availability = 7,182 ÷ 7,200 × 100 = 99.75%.

Question 27. A service has an RTO of 4 hours and an RPO of 30 minutes. Explain the targets.

Model answer: The service should be restored within four hours, and recovered data should be no more than 30 minutes behind the disruption point.

Question 28. A control sample contains 80 access records; 6 lack evidence of approval. Calculate the exception rate.

Model answer: 6 ÷ 80 × 100 = 7.5%. The auditor should investigate the nature and risk of the exceptions.

Question 29. Why should audit recommendations have named owners?

Model answer: Ownership establishes accountability for action, resources, milestones and escalation.

Question 30. Explain continual improvement in ITSM.

Model answer: Regularly use performance data, feedback, incidents and reviews to identify, prioritise, implement and verify service improvements.
