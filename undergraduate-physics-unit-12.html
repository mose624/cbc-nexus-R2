Unit overview

Statistical physics builds a bridge between microscopic mechanics and macroscopic thermodynamics. It uses probability theory and state counting to explain equilibrium, entropy, heat capacity, radiation, quantum gases, magnetic ordering and transport. The chapter develops the microcanonical, canonical and grand canonical ensembles, derives the major distribution functions, and applies them to solids, electrons, photons, phase transitions and numerical simulations.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: calculus, probability, classical mechanics, introductory quantum mechanics and thermodynamics. Conventions: kB is Boltzmann's constant, h is Planck's constant, ℏ=h/(2π), β=1/(kBT), and energies are measured in joules unless stated otherwise.

1. Statistical physics: scope and purpose

Statistical physics explains macroscopic thermodynamic behaviour from the collective properties of microscopic constituents. A macroscopic sample contains an enormous number of particles, so tracking every trajectory is impractical and unnecessary. Instead, probability distributions, ensembles and averages connect microscopic laws to measurable pressure, temperature, magnetisation, heat capacity and entropy.

The central distinction is between a microstate and a macrostate. A microstate specifies the detailed microscopic configuration, such as particle positions and momenta or quantum occupation numbers. A macrostate specifies a small set of bulk constraints. Many different microstates can correspond to the same macrostate; the multiplicity of those microstates is fundamental to entropy.

Statistical physics is not merely a method of averaging. It provides a quantitative explanation of equilibrium, fluctuations, phase transitions, thermal radiation, electronic properties and the behaviour of matter at low temperatures. It also clarifies when classical approximations fail and quantum statistics become essential.

2. Microstates, macrostates and multiplicity

For a system with discrete accessible states, a microstate is one complete allowed specification at the microscopic level. A macrostate may be described by energy, volume, particle number, magnetisation or other macroscopic constraints. Multiplicity Ω is the number of microstates compatible with a specified macrostate.

If all accessible microstates are equally likely in an isolated system at equilibrium, the macrostate with the greatest multiplicity is overwhelmingly probable for a large system. This explains why equilibrium is associated with sharply concentrated macroscopic observables, even though microscopic motion continues.

Multiplicity depends on what is held fixed and on whether particles are distinguishable, indistinguishable, localised or free to move. Counting must respect physical constraints and quantum state occupancy. Incorrect counting can produce wrong entropy factors, missing factorials and incorrect thermodynamic predictions.

3. Probability, distributions and expectation values

A probability distribution assigns a probability to each possible microscopic outcome. Probabilities are non-negative and sum to unity. For discrete states i, the expectation value of an observable A is ⟨A⟩=ΣᵢpᵢAᵢ. For continuous variables, sums are replaced by integrals over a normalised probability density.

The variance σ²=⟨A²⟩−⟨A⟩² measures fluctuations around the mean. A small relative fluctuation means a macroscopic observable is sharply defined. For many independent contributions, relative fluctuations often decrease approximately as N⁻¹/², explaining the stability of bulk measurements.

An ensemble average is a probability-weighted prediction over many hypothetical replicas or repeated preparations. It should not be confused with a time average unless suitable ergodic assumptions apply. Experimental comparison also requires accounting for finite sample size, detector resolution and correlated observations.

4. The microcanonical ensemble

The microcanonical ensemble describes an isolated system with fixed energy E, volume V and particle number N. Its accessible microstates lie within a narrow energy shell. Under the equal a priori probability postulate, each accessible microstate has probability 1/Ω.

The entropy is S=kB ln Ω. The logarithm makes entropy additive for independent systems because multiplicities multiply: ΩAB=ΩAΩB, so SAB=SA+SB. Boltzmann's constant converts the dimensionless logarithm into SI entropy units of joules per kelvin.

Temperature follows from the entropy derivative: 1/T=(∂S/∂E)V,N. Pressure follows from 1/T times the entropy derivative with respect to volume, and chemical potential is related to the particle-number derivative. These relations connect microscopic counting to thermodynamic state variables.

5. Boltzmann entropy and the second law

Boltzmann's formula S=kB ln Ω assigns greater entropy to macrostates with greater compatible multiplicity. It gives a statistical interpretation of the thermodynamic entropy introduced in phenomenological thermodynamics. The logarithm is essential for extensivity when independent systems are combined.

The second law is statistical: an isolated system overwhelmingly evolves toward macrostates occupying vastly more microstates. Microscopic equations may be time-reversal invariant, but overwhelmingly probable macroscopic behaviour has a direction because low-multiplicity macrostates occupy an extraordinarily small fraction of accessible phase space.

Entropy can fluctuate downward for small systems over short observation intervals, but large sustained decreases are extraordinarily improbable. Statistical physics therefore reconciles microscopic reversibility with the practical irreversibility of mixing, diffusion and thermal equilibration.

6. Phase space and Liouville's theorem

Classical phase space contains one coordinate for every independent position and momentum component. A single classical microstate is a point in this high-dimensional space. An ensemble is represented by a density ρ(q,p,t), whose integral over a region gives the probability of finding the system there.

Hamiltonian dynamics preserves phase-space volume. Liouville's theorem states that the phase-space density is constant along a Hamiltonian trajectory, even though the shape of an ensemble distribution may stretch and fold. This property underlies classical statistical mechanics and the invariant phase-space measure.

The phase-space volume associated with a classical state must be made dimensionless when used in state counting. For N particles, a conventional cell scale involving h is introduced, and indistinguishability is handled by a factor N! in the classical partition function.

7. The canonical ensemble and Boltzmann factor

The canonical ensemble describes a system in thermal contact with a large reservoir at fixed temperature T. The system can exchange energy with the reservoir while its volume and particle number remain fixed. The probability of a microstate of energy Ei is proportional to exp(−βEi), where β=1/(kBT).

Normalisation introduces the canonical partition function Z=Σᵢexp(−βEi). The probability is pi=exp(−βEi)/Z. Low-energy states are more probable, while higher-energy states remain populated according to their Boltzmann suppression.

The Boltzmann factor is derived by considering the multiplicity of the reservoir after the system acquires energy Ei. Expanding the reservoir entropy around its equilibrium energy gives a term linear in Ei, producing exp(−Ei/kBT). This derivation identifies the assumptions of a large reservoir and weak coupling.

8. Canonical partition function and thermodynamic properties

The partition function contains the equilibrium statistical information for a canonical system. Once Z(T,V,N) is known, the Helmholtz free energy is F=−kBT ln Z. The mean energy is U=−∂ln Z/∂β, with the derivative taken at fixed volume and particle number.

Entropy follows from S=kB(ln Z+βU). Pressure is obtained from p=kBT(∂ln Z/∂V)T,N. Heat capacity follows from the temperature derivative of mean energy and is related to energy fluctuations.

The partition function factorises for independent subsystems because their energies add and the sums over states separate. This makes large systems tractable. Interactions generally prevent simple factorisation and require approximations, numerical methods or more advanced theories.

9. Energy fluctuations and heat capacity

In the canonical ensemble, the energy variance is ⟨(ΔE)²⟩=⟨E²⟩−⟨E⟩². Differentiating the partition function shows that this variance equals kBT²CV for a system with fixed volume and particle number.

This fluctuation relation gives a microscopic interpretation of heat capacity: a system with large energy fluctuations responds strongly to a temperature change. It also provides a route to calculate heat capacity from simulation data without repeatedly differentiating noisy energy measurements.

For macroscopic systems away from critical points, absolute fluctuations can be large while relative fluctuations are small. Near a critical point, correlation lengths and fluctuations can grow substantially, producing anomalous response functions and critical behaviour.

10. The grand canonical ensemble

The grand canonical ensemble describes a system that exchanges both energy and particles with a reservoir. Temperature, volume and chemical potential are fixed, while energy and particle number fluctuate. The statistical weight of a state with energy Ei and particle number Ni is proportional to exp[−β(Ei−μNi)].

The grand partition function is Ξ=ΣNΣᵢexp[−β(Ei−μN)]. The grand potential is ΩG=−kBT ln Ξ. The mean particle number is obtained from a derivative with respect to chemical potential, while number fluctuations are linked to particle-number response.

This ensemble is useful for gases, adsorption, electron systems, photon gases and systems where particle number is not fixed. Photons in thermal equilibrium have zero chemical potential because the number of photons is not conserved in ordinary emission and absorption processes.

11. Chemical potential and particle exchange

Chemical potential μ measures the change in an appropriate thermodynamic potential when one particle is added while the relevant external variables are held fixed. In the grand canonical description, it controls the energetic cost or benefit of particle exchange with a reservoir.

For two systems able to exchange particles, equilibrium requires equality of their chemical potentials. If chemical potentials differ, particle transfer tends to reduce the appropriate total thermodynamic potential. This is the statistical basis of diffusion and equilibrium composition.

The chemical potential is not generally equal to a single-particle energy. It includes the effects of temperature, interactions, density and quantum statistics. For ideal classical gases it can be expressed in terms of density and thermal wavelength.

12. Classical Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics

Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics apply when quantum wave packets overlap negligibly and occupation of individual quantum states is small. Particles behave classically and quantum exchange effects can be neglected. The occupation probability is approximately proportional to exp[−β(ε−μ)].

The classical regime is often characterised by nλth³ much less than one, where n is number density and λth is the thermal de Broglie wavelength. Low density or high temperature favours this regime.

The classical ideal gas partition function includes a factor 1/N! for indistinguishable particles. This correction resolves the Gibbs paradox and ensures entropy is extensive. Classical statistics remains useful for dilute gases and many engineering applications.

13. Thermal de Broglie wavelength and quantum degeneracy

The thermal de Broglie wavelength λth=h/√(2πmkBT) estimates the spatial scale of a particle's thermal wave character. It increases as temperature falls and as particle mass decreases.

When the mean interparticle spacing becomes comparable to λth, quantum wavefunctions overlap and exchange symmetry matters. The dimensionless degeneracy parameter nλth³ indicates whether classical statistics is adequate.

Electrons in ordinary metals are quantum-degenerate at room temperature because their density is high and their Fermi temperature is much greater than room temperature. Dilute atomic gases can be driven into quantum degeneracy by cooling and trapping.

14. Identical particles and quantum state counting

Identical quantum particles cannot be distinguished by labels in the physical description. Their many-particle wavefunctions must be symmetric under exchange for bosons and antisymmetric for fermions. This exchange symmetry changes the allowed occupation patterns.

Bosons can share a single quantum state without restriction, whereas fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle and each one-particle state can hold at most one fermion per spin state. These rules generate Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac statistics.

The distinction is not a small correction in degenerate systems. It explains blackbody radiation, the stability of matter, electron degeneracy pressure, superfluidity, superconductivity and the electronic properties of solids.

15. Bose-Einstein distribution

For bosons in thermal equilibrium, the mean occupation of a one-particle state of energy ε is n̄(ε)=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))−1]. The chemical potential cannot exceed the lowest single-particle energy in a stable ideal gas.

The denominator contains a minus one because multiple bosons may occupy the same state. At high temperature and low occupation, the distribution approaches the Maxwell-Boltzmann form.

Bose-Einstein statistics describe photons, phonons and integer-spin atoms. For an ideal uniform three-dimensional gas, cooling below a critical temperature produces macroscopic occupation of the ground state, known as Bose-Einstein condensation.

16. Fermi-Dirac distribution and Pauli exclusion

For fermions, the mean occupation is n̄(ε)=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))+1]. The plus one follows from the restriction that a fermionic quantum state can be occupied only once per allowed spin state.

At zero temperature, states below the Fermi energy are occupied and states above it are empty for an ideal ground-state Fermi gas. At finite temperature, the sharp step is broadened over an energy scale of order kBT.

Fermi-Dirac statistics explain electron populations in metals and semiconductors, white-dwarf electron degeneracy pressure and neutron-star matter. The Fermi energy, Fermi momentum and density of states are central quantities in these applications.

20. Equipartition theorem and its limitations

Key mathematical derivations

The following derivations connect the probability postulates to measurable thermodynamic quantities. Work through each algebraic step and identify the physical assumptions, rather than memorising the final expression.

Derivation: canonical probability from a heat reservoir

Consider a small system S in weak thermal contact with a much larger reservoir R. The combined isolated system has fixed total energy Etotal. If the small system occupies a microstate i with energy Ei, the reservoir has energy Etotal−Ei. The probability of i is proportional to the number of reservoir states available at that energy: pi∝ΩR(Etotal−Ei). Using Boltzmann entropy, lnΩR=SR/kB, and expanding about Etotal gives SR(Etotal−Ei)≈SR(Etotal)−Ei(∂SR/∂E)Etotal. Since (∂S/∂E)=1/T, the result is pi∝exp[−Ei/(kBT)]. Normalising over all system states gives pi=exp(−βEi)/Z, with Z=Σi exp(−βEi). The derivation assumes a reservoir much larger than the system, weak coupling and a well-defined reservoir temperature.

Derivation: mean energy and heat capacity from the partition function

Start with Z=Σi exp(−βEi). Differentiating gives ∂Z/∂β=−ΣiEi exp(−βEi). Dividing by Z yields ∂lnZ/∂β=−⟨E⟩, so U=−∂lnZ/∂β. A second derivative gives ∂²lnZ/∂β²=⟨E²⟩−⟨E⟩². Because β=1/(kBT), differentiating U with respect to T gives CV=(⟨E²⟩−⟨E⟩²)/(kBT²). Thus CV is non-negative for a canonical system with ordinary stable conditions and is directly connected to the variance of energy. This result is both a theoretical identity and a practical simulation diagnostic.

Derivation: Bose-Einstein occupation

For one bosonic mode with energy ε, any occupation n=0,1,2,… is allowed. In the grand canonical ensemble the weight of n particles is exp[−βn(ε−μ)]. Let x=exp[−β(ε−μ)]. The single-mode grand partition function is Ξε=1+x+x²+…=1/(1−x), for x<1. The mean occupation is n̄=x∂lnΞε/∂x=x/(1−x). Substituting x gives n̄=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))−1]. The result depends on unrestricted bosonic occupancy and requires μ below the lowest allowed energy for the ideal gas.

Derivation: Fermi-Dirac occupation

For a fermionic one-particle state, Pauli exclusion allows n=0 or n=1 only. The single-state grand partition function is Ξε=1+exp[−β(ε−μ)]. The mean occupation is obtained by differentiating lnΞε with respect to βμ, giving n̄=exp[−β(ε−μ)]/[1+exp(−β(ε−μ))]. Multiplying numerator and denominator by exp[β(ε−μ)] gives n̄=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))+1]. The plus sign follows directly from the two allowed occupancy values.

Derivation: density of states for free particles

In a cubic box of side L with periodic boundary conditions, allowed wavevectors are spaced by 2π/L. Each state occupies volume (2π/L)³ in k-space. The number of states with wavevector magnitude below k is the volume of a sphere, 4πk³/3, divided by the cell volume, multiplied by any spin degeneracy. Differentiating with respect to k and using ε=ℏ²k²/(2m) converts the count into g(ε). In three dimensions g(ε) per unit volume is proportional to ε¹/². The exact prefactor depends on whether spin degeneracy is included.

Derivation: Fermi energy of a three-dimensional gas

At zero temperature, fill all states up to kF. For spin degeneracy two, particle density is n=2×[volume of k-space sphere]/[(2π)³]=kF³/(3π²). Therefore kF=(3π²n)¹/³. The highest occupied energy is EF=ℏ²kF²/(2m). This shows EF increases with density as n²/³ and provides the Fermi temperature TF=EF/kB. The result assumes a uniform, non-interacting, non-relativistic spin-½ gas.

Derivation: classical monatomic ideal-gas partition function

The one-particle translational partition function is q=V/h³∫exp[−βp²/(2m)]d³p. Evaluating the three Gaussian momentum integrals gives q=V(2πmkBT)³/²/h³=V/λth³. For N identical non-interacting particles, ZN=qN/N!. The N! removes overcounting of permutations. From F=−kBT lnZN and Stirling's approximation, thermodynamic derivatives recover pV=NkBT and the classical ideal-gas entropy.

Derivation: equipartition theorem

For a canonical distribution, the probability density is proportional to exp(−βH). Consider one quadratic contribution Hx=ax². The average is ⟨ax²⟩=∫ax²e−βax²dx/∫e−βax²dx. Differentiating the Gaussian integral with respect to βa gives ⟨ax²⟩=1/(2β)=kBT/2. Each independent quadratic degree contributes this amount. The theorem assumes classical continuous degrees of freedom and fails when quantum energy levels are not closely spaced compared with kBT.

Derivation: Planck distribution

Electromagnetic radiation in a cavity can be decomposed into independent normal modes. Each mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator with energies nℏω when the zero-point term is omitted from thermal occupation. Photons are bosons and their number is not conserved, so μ=0. The mean occupation is n̄=1/[exp(ℏω/kBT)−1]. Multiplying by photon energy ℏω and the electromagnetic mode density gives the spectral energy density. The result tends to the Rayleigh-Jeans law at low frequency and decays exponentially at high frequency.

Derivation: Debye low-temperature heat capacity

The Debye model approximates acoustic phonon modes with a linear dispersion relation ω=vk up to a cutoff ωD. The density of modes is proportional to ω². Internal energy is an integral of ℏω times the Bose occupation over frequency. At low temperature, the upper cutoff can be extended to infinity after changing variable x=ℏω/kBT. The integral scales as T⁴, so differentiating with respect to temperature gives CV proportional to T³. This is the Debye law for a three-dimensional insulating crystal at sufficiently low temperature.

Derivation: Einstein relation for diffusion

For a random walk with independent zero-mean displacement increments, the mean-square displacement is the sum of the variances of the increments because cross terms average to zero. At long times, the number of increments is proportional to time, so ⟨r²⟩ grows linearly with t. Isotropic diffusion in d dimensions obeys ∂P/∂t=D∇²P; multiplying by r² and integrating over space gives d⟨r²⟩/dt=2dD. Integrating from zero displacement gives ⟨r²⟩=2dDt.

Derivation: entropy and multiplicity for independent systems

If systems A and B are independent, each microstate of A can be paired with each microstate of B. Thus ΩAB=ΩAΩB. Applying Boltzmann's definition gives SAB=kB ln(ΩAΩB)=kB lnΩA+kB lnΩB=SA+SB. The logarithmic form is therefore required for additive entropy under independent composition.

University examination questions and model answers

These original questions cover conceptual reasoning, derivations, calculations and applications. They are independent practice materials, not official examination papers.

Question 1. Define microstate and macrostate.

Model answer: A microstate specifies a complete microscopic configuration. A macrostate specifies bulk constraints such as energy, volume, particle number or magnetisation. Many microstates can correspond to one macrostate.

Question 2. State Boltzmann's entropy formula and explain it.

Model answer: S=kB ln Ω, where Ω is the number of accessible microstates compatible with the macrostate. The logarithm makes entropy additive for independent systems.

Question 3. What is an ensemble?

Model answer: An ensemble is a statistical collection of hypothetical systems prepared under the same macroscopic constraints but occupying different microstates with probabilities determined by the ensemble.

Question 4. Describe the microcanonical ensemble.

Model answer: It models an isolated system with fixed energy, volume and particle number. Accessible states in a narrow energy shell are assigned equal probability.

Question 5. Derive the canonical Boltzmann factor.

Model answer: A system state of energy E leaves the reservoir with energy ER−E. Expanding reservoir entropy gives SR(E−E)≈SR(ER)−E/T; reservoir multiplicity is therefore proportional to exp(−E/kBT).

Question 6. Define the canonical partition function.

Model answer: Z=Σi exp(−βEi), where β=1/(kBT). It normalises canonical probabilities and generates thermodynamic quantities.

Question 7. Show how to obtain mean energy from Z.

Model answer: U=ΣipiEi=−∂lnZ/∂β, at fixed volume and particle number. Differentiation weights states by their energies.

Question 8. Explain the relation between energy fluctuations and heat capacity.

Model answer: In the canonical ensemble, variance of energy equals kBT²CV. Large heat capacity corresponds to larger equilibrium energy fluctuations.

Question 9. What is the grand canonical ensemble?

Model answer: It describes a system that exchanges energy and particles with reservoirs. T, V and μ are fixed; energy and particle number fluctuate.

Question 10. Define chemical potential.

Model answer: Chemical potential is the change in an appropriate thermodynamic potential when particle number changes at fixed natural variables. It controls particle exchange and diffusion.

Question 11. When is Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics valid?

Model answer: When the gas is dilute or hot enough that quantum wavefunctions overlap negligibly and occupation of each quantum state is small; nλth³≪1.

Question 12. Explain thermal de Broglie wavelength.

Model answer: λth=h/√(2πmkBT) estimates the quantum spatial extent of a particle at temperature T. Quantum degeneracy matters when this approaches the mean particle spacing.

Question 13. State the difference between bosons and fermions.

Model answer: Bosons have symmetric many-particle wavefunctions and can share a state. Fermions have antisymmetric wavefunctions and obey Pauli exclusion.

Question 14. Write the Bose-Einstein distribution and explain the minus sign.

Model answer: n̄=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))−1]. The minus sign results from summing all non-negative occupation numbers for a bosonic state.

Question 15. Write the Fermi-Dirac distribution and explain the plus sign.

Model answer: n̄=1/[exp(β(ε−μ))+1]. It follows because a fermionic state can be either empty or occupied once.

Question 16. How does the classical limit emerge from quantum statistics?

Model answer: When exp[β(ε−μ)]≫1, the ±1 in the denominator is negligible, giving n̄≈exp[−β(ε−μ)], the Maxwell-Boltzmann form.

Question 17. Explain density of states.

Model answer: It gives the number of allowed one-particle states per energy interval. It converts sums over levels into energy integrals for particle number and energy.

Question 18. State and explain the equipartition theorem.

Model answer: Each independent quadratic term in classical energy contributes average kBT/2. Quantum level spacing can freeze degrees of freedom and invalidate the classical result.

Question 19. Why does a quantum oscillator have zero-point energy?

Model answer: Its ground-state energy is ℏω/2, required by quantum uncertainty and the oscillator eigenvalue spectrum; it remains even at zero temperature.

Question 20. Explain Planck's radiation law statistically.

Model answer: Electromagnetic modes are bosonic oscillators with zero photon chemical potential. Applying Bose-Einstein occupation yields Planck's spectrum.

Question 21. Compare Einstein and Debye models.

Model answer: Einstein assigns one frequency to all lattice oscillators. Debye uses a continuum of vibrational modes and correctly predicts low-temperature CV∝T³.

Question 22. Define Fermi energy.

Model answer: At zero temperature it is the highest occupied one-particle energy. It sets the characteristic energy and temperature scales of a degenerate Fermi gas.

Question 23. Why do electrons in metals show degeneracy at room temperature?

Model answer: Their Fermi temperature is typically far above room temperature because electron density is high; only states near the Fermi surface are thermally affected.

Question 24. What is Bose-Einstein condensation?

Model answer: At sufficiently low temperature, a macroscopic fraction of bosons occupies the lowest quantum state, producing a coherent quantum phase.

Question 25. Explain the Ising model.

Model answer: It is a lattice model of two-state spins with neighbour coupling and an external field. It illustrates collective magnetic ordering and critical phenomena.

Question 26. What is an order parameter?

Model answer: A macroscopic quantity distinguishing phases, such as spontaneous magnetisation. It may vanish in a symmetric phase and become non-zero in an ordered phase.

Question 27. Explain detailed balance in Monte Carlo simulation.

Model answer: For each pair of configurations, equilibrium probability flow from one to the other balances the reverse flow. This helps ensure the desired stationary distribution.

Question 28. Why is equilibration required before collecting simulation averages?

Model answer: Initial configurations may not represent equilibrium. The system must relax before samples are collected; autocorrelation and metastability must also be considered.

Question 29. What does ergodicity mean?

Model answer: A long trajectory explores the relevant accessible phase space sufficiently that time averages agree with ensemble averages under appropriate conditions.

Question 30. Explain ensemble equivalence.

Model answer: For many large short-range systems, different ensembles give the same bulk thermodynamics in the thermodynamic limit. Finite systems and long-range interactions may differ.

Question 31. What is the thermodynamic limit?

Model answer: N and V tend to infinity at fixed density. Bulk quantities become extensive or intensive as appropriate, and surface effects are often negligible.

Question 32. State the Einstein relation for diffusion.

Model answer: In d dimensions, normal diffusion gives ⟨r²(t)⟩≈2dDt at long times. D is the diffusion coefficient.

Question 33. What are critical exponents?

Model answer: They describe power-law behaviour of quantities near continuous phase transitions, such as heat capacity, susceptibility and correlation length.

Question 34. Explain universality.

Model answer: Different systems can share critical exponents when they have common dimensionality, symmetry and interaction range, despite microscopic differences.

Question 35. What is the Gibbs paradox and how is it resolved?

Model answer: Classical counting of identical particles as distinguishable gives non-extensive entropy. Dividing the partition function by N! corrects overcounting.

Question 36. Distinguish information entropy from thermodynamic entropy.

Model answer: Shannon entropy quantifies uncertainty in a probability distribution. Thermodynamic entropy is a physical state function; they are related when probabilities describe physical microstates.

Question 37. Why do fluctuations grow near critical points?

Model answer: Correlations extend over larger distances and collective modes become important, increasing response functions and producing critical behaviour.

Question 38. Describe how statistical physics explains the second law.

Model answer: High-entropy macrostates correspond to vastly more microstates than low-entropy ones, making evolution toward equilibrium overwhelmingly probable for macroscopic systems.

Question 39. What are Green-Kubo relations used for?

Model answer: They calculate transport coefficients from time integrals of equilibrium current autocorrelation functions, linking microscopic dynamics to macroscopic transport.

Question 40. List checks required for a reliable statistical-physics simulation.

Model answer: Check model assumptions, boundary conditions, equilibration, time-step or sampling convergence, autocorrelation, independent runs, finite-size effects and uncertainty estimates.

Laboratory and computational investigations

1. Calorimetry and heat capacity

Measure a sample mass, temperature response and heat supplied using a calibrated heater or calorimeter. Record the time series and allow thermal equilibrium at each point. Determine heat capacity from the slope of energy against temperature after accounting for the calorimeter constant and heat loss. Repeat at several temperatures to identify departures from classical equipartition. Report sensor calibration, thermal lag, uncertainty in mass and electrical measurements, and the model used to correct environmental exchange.

2. Low-temperature heat capacity

Use a cryostat and calibrated thermometer to measure sample heat capacity over a controlled temperature range. For a metal, plot CV/T against T² in the regime where electronic and Debye phonon terms dominate. The intercept estimates the electronic coefficient and the slope estimates the phonon coefficient. Check addenda from the sample holder, thermal contact resistance, thermometer self-heating and reproducibility across warming and cooling runs.

3. Magnetic susceptibility

Place a paramagnetic sample in a controlled magnetic field and measure magnetisation or an equivalent magnetic response while varying temperature. In the weak-field Curie regime, plot inverse susceptibility against temperature. Departures from linearity may indicate interactions, saturation, background diamagnetism or a phase transition. Calibrate field and sensor response, measure sample geometry and subtract holder contributions.

4. Blackbody radiation

Use a cavity radiator or calibrated thermal source and a spectral detector to measure emitted intensity at known temperatures. Compare the measured spectrum with Planck's law and test the displacement of the spectral maximum with temperature. Account for detector spectral response, emissivity, window transmission, background radiation and alignment. Do not treat a non-ideal surface as a perfect blackbody without an emissivity correction.

5. Brownian motion and diffusion

Track suspended tracer particles with a microscope and video or image-analysis system. Convert pixel displacements to physical distances using a calibration scale. Compute mean-square displacement versus time lag and fit the long-time slope to obtain D. Check for drift, convection, particle interactions, tracking errors and finite field-of-view effects. Compare with the Stokes-Einstein prediction when spherical-particle and continuum-fluid assumptions are justified.

6. Monte Carlo study of the Ising model

Implement a two-dimensional spin lattice with periodic boundaries and nearest-neighbour coupling. Initialise spins, perform random single-spin proposals and apply the Metropolis acceptance rule. Discard equilibration sweeps, then record energy and magnetisation over many decorrelated samples. Repeat for several temperatures and lattice sizes. Examine hysteresis, autocorrelation, finite-size rounding and statistical uncertainty before estimating a transition region.

Formula summary

  • Boltzmann entropy: S=kB ln Ω.
  • Canonical probability: pi=e⁻ᵝᴱⁱ/Z; β=1/(kBT).
  • Canonical partition function: Z=Σi e⁻ᵝᴱⁱ.
  • Helmholtz free energy: F=−kBT ln Z.
  • Mean energy: U=−∂lnZ/∂β.
  • Energy variance: ⟨(ΔE)²⟩=kBT²CV.
  • Grand partition function: Ξ=ΣNΣi e⁻ᵝ⁽ᴱⁱ⁻ᵘᴺ⁾.
  • Bose-Einstein: n̄=1/[eᵝ⁽ᵋ⁻ᵘ⁾−1].
  • Fermi-Dirac: n̄=1/[eᵝ⁽ᵋ⁻ᵘ⁾+1].
  • Thermal wavelength: λth=h/√(2πmkBT).
  • Fermi wavevector: kF=(3π²n)¹/³ for spin-½ particles.
  • Fermi energy: EF=ℏ²kF²/(2m).
  • Equipartition: kBT/2 per independent quadratic term.
  • Diffusion: ⟨r²⟩=2dDt.
  • Debye low-temperature heat capacity: CV∝T³.

Before applying any formula, identify the ensemble and the correct state-counting convention. Distinguish energy per particle from total energy, number density from total particle number, and a one-particle partition function from a many-particle partition function.
