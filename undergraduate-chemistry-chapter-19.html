48. Organometallic reagents in synthesis

Organolithium and Grignard reagents are strongly polar organometallic compounds used to form carbon–carbon bonds by nucleophilic addition and substitution. Their reactivity is influenced by aggregation, solvent coordination, temperature and reagent concentration. They are generally incompatible with water and many protic functional groups, so synthetic planning must include chemoselectivity, controlled quenching and appropriate safety review.

A rigorous assessment defines the metal species and operating conditions, considers competing pathways and identifies measurements that distinguish the proposed mechanism. Report assumptions, selectivity, conversion and uncertainty. Evaluate solvent, waste, metal recovery and hazards as part of the chemistry rather than as afterthoughts.

49. Metal-mediated radical pathways

Some organometallic reactions proceed through single-electron transfer and radical intermediates rather than a purely two-electron cycle. Evidence can include radical-clock rearrangements, EPR signals, trapping experiments and characteristic kinetic behaviour. Radical probes can perturb the reaction, so conclusions should be supported by independent measurements and careful control experiments.

50. Catalyst scale-up and process engineering

Moving a catalytic reaction from laboratory scale to process scale changes heat and mass transfer, gas–liquid contact, mixing, catalyst lifetime and impurity exposure. Process development evaluates space-time yield, productivity, selectivity, separation, recycle streams and process safety. A high small-scale yield does not guarantee an economical or safe industrial process.

1. Foundations and scope

Organometallic chemistry studies compounds containing at least one direct metal–carbon bond, including classical transition-metal complexes, main-group organometallics and clusters. It links inorganic structure with organic reactivity: metals stabilise unusual oxidation states, activate small molecules and organise bond-forming steps. A useful description specifies the metal, formal oxidation state, ligand set, electron count, geometry and likely reactive sites.

2. Classification of metal–carbon bonds

Metal–carbon interactions range from predominantly ionic M–C bonds in highly electropositive organometallics to covalent σ bonds and delocalised π interactions in transition-metal complexes. Carbon may bind as an alkyl, aryl, alkynyl, carbene, carbonyl, alkene, alkyne or aromatic ligand. Hapticity, written ηn, records the number of contiguous atoms of a ligand interacting with the metal.

3. Ionic and covalent bonding descriptions

The ionic method assigns ligand charges and metal oxidation state; the covalent or neutral method counts neutral fragments and shared electrons. Both can give the same total valence-electron count if applied consistently. Formal oxidation states are bookkeeping tools and should not be confused with partial atomic charges, especially in highly covalent metal–carbonyl and metal–alkyl compounds.

4. Electron counting: 18-electron rule

Many low-valent transition-metal complexes are stabilised when the metal valence shell reaches 18 electrons, filling one s, three p and five d orbitals in a simple model. Count metal d electrons and ligand donations consistently. The rule is a guide, not a law: 16-electron square-planar d8 complexes, early-metal complexes and sterically protected species are common exceptions.

5. Hapticity and ligand electron donation

A ligand's electron donation depends on its bonding mode and the counting convention. η1-allyl may behave as a one-point donor in a particular bonding description, while η3-allyl is a delocalised three-carbon donor. Ring slippage changes hapticity and can open a coordination site during a catalytic cycle. Always state the convention used.

7. β-Hydride elimination

A metal alkyl containing a β hydrogen may eliminate it to form a metal hydride and an alkene. The process generally requires a syn-coplanar M–C–C–H arrangement and a vacant or accessible coordination site. It can terminate polymer chains, isomerise alkenes or provide a route to metal hydrides. Alkyl groups without β hydrogens avoid this pathway.

9. Dihydrogen complexes and H–H activation

An H2 molecule can coordinate to a metal through donation from its σ bond and metal-to-H2 back-donation into σ*. Increased back-donation weakens and lengthens the H–H bond, potentially leading toward oxidative addition and two hydride ligands. Structural and spectroscopic evidence, especially H–H distance and NMR relaxation, helps distinguish bound H2 from classical hydrides.

10. Oxidative addition

Oxidative addition formally adds a bond A–B across a metal centre, commonly increasing its oxidation state and coordination number by two. It is favoured by electron-rich, coordinatively unsaturated metals and substrates with accessible bonds, but ligand and substrate effects are decisive. Mechanistic pathways may be concerted, radical or ionic depending on the system.

The ligands usually need to occupy adjacent positions. Steric crowding and favourable product bond formation can promote the step; geometry and electronic configuration can inhibit it.

14. σ-Bond metathesis

σ-Bond metathesis exchanges bonds through a concerted four-centre transition state, often without a formal change in metal oxidation state. It is important for early d0 metals and f-block complexes where conventional oxidative addition may be unfavourable. Reactions commonly exchange M–C or M–H bonds with E–H bonds such as C–H or Si–H.

16. Metal carbenes: Fischer and Schrock types

Schrock carbenes are often high-valent early-metal species with nucleophilic carbon character. These are useful limiting descriptions; real bonding is a continuum and reactivity must be inferred from the full ligand environment.

Greater back-bonding weakens the internal C–O bond and lowers its IR stretching frequency. Compare spectra only with attention to charge, geometry, solvent, bridging carbonyls and vibrational coupling.

31. Cross-coupling catalytic cycles

Worked example 1

Question: Count the electrons in ferrocene using ionic counting.

Solution: Each Cp ligand is Cp− and donates six electrons. Fe is formally Fe(II), d6. Total = 6 + 6 + 6 = 18 electrons.

Worked example 2

Question: Explain the product of β-hydride elimination from M–CH2CH2R.

Solution: A β hydrogen transfers to the metal, giving an M–H ligand and an alkene, commonly CH2=CHR. A suitable syn-coplanar geometry and accessible coordination site are needed.

Worked example 3

Question: Describe the formal changes in oxidative addition of R–X to Pd(0).

Solution: The Pd oxidation state formally increases from 0 to +II and coordination number commonly increases by two as Pd(II)(R)(X) forms. Actual pathway can depend on substrate and ligands.

Worked example 4

Question: Interpret a lower νCO band in a metal carbonyl series.

Solution: A lower CO stretching frequency commonly indicates stronger metal-to-CO π back-donation, which weakens the C–O bond. Charge, geometry and coupling must be compared consistently.

Worked example 5

Question: Calculate enantiomeric excess for 92% major and 8% minor enantiomer.

Solution: ee = 92 − 8 = 84%. Report the major enantiomer identity and analytical method as well.

Worked example 6

Question: Explain why a 16-electron square-planar d8 complex can be stable.

Solution: Square-planar ligand-field splitting strongly stabilises the d8 arrangement; the 18-electron rule is not universal and many Pd(II), Pt(II) and Rh(I) complexes are 16-electron.

Advanced Organometallic Mechanisms, Catalysis and Laboratory Applications

This extension connects organometallic structure to catalytic function. Mechanistic assignments should be supported by electron counting, oxidation-state bookkeeping, ligand coordination changes, rate laws, isotope effects and spectroscopic evidence.

Expanded theoretical treatment

1. Electron counting as a mechanistic consistency check

Use one electron-counting convention consistently. In the ionic method, assign ligand charges, determine the metal oxidation state and d-electron count, then add ligand donations. In the neutral method, count metal group electrons and neutral-ligand donations, accounting explicitly for covalent X-type ligands. The 18-electron rule is a useful heuristic for many low-valent complexes, not a universal law; square-planar d⁸ and coordinatively unsaturated catalytic intermediates are common exceptions.

2. Elementary steps and oxidation-state bookkeeping

Oxidative addition usually increases the metal oxidation state by two and coordination number by two; reductive elimination reverses this pattern. Migratory insertion commonly combines a coordinated ligand with a migrating group without changing the formal metal oxidation state. β-Hydride elimination requires a β-hydrogen, a suitable syn-coplanar geometry and an available coordination site. A proposed cycle must conserve atoms, charge and electron count at every step.

3. Catalytic cycles and kinetic evidence

A catalytic cycle is a sequence of elementary steps that regenerates the active catalyst. The turnover frequency is the number of substrate molecules converted per active catalyst site per unit time. The observed rate may be controlled by substrate binding, oxidative addition, insertion, product release or catalyst activation. A turnover-limiting step must be inferred from kinetic data, not simply from the step that looks most chemically demanding.

4. Ligand effects and selectivity

Strong σ donors can increase electron density at the metal and alter oxidative-addition barriers. π-acceptor ligands can stabilise electron-rich metal centres through back-bonding. Steric bulk can favour low-coordinate species and influence regioselectivity, but electronic and steric effects are coupled. Compare ligand series using measured rates and selectivities under identical conditions.

5. Catalyst deactivation and process chemistry

Common deactivation pathways include aggregation to metal particles, irreversible ligand loss, product inhibition, oxidation, hydrolysis and formation of inactive metal complexes. Scale-up changes heat and mass transfer, mixing, gas–liquid transfer and catalyst lifetime. Report productivity, selectivity, catalyst loading, turnover number, turnover frequency, solvent use and metal residue—not yield alone.

6. Mechanistic experiments and isotopic probes

Deuterium labelling can reveal hydrogen transfer and kinetic isotope effects; ¹³CO can trace carbonyl insertion; crossover experiments can distinguish intra- from intermolecular pathways. Operando IR, NMR, UV–visible or X-ray absorption methods can identify catalyst resting states. A resting state is the most abundant catalyst form under reaction conditions and is not necessarily the active species.

University-level derivation

For a simplified catalytic sequence A + S ⇌ AS → P + A, let binding be rapid and at equilibrium with K = [AS]/([A][S]). If total catalyst concentration is [A]T = [A] + [AS], then [AS] = [A]T K[S]/(1 + K[S]). If product formation from AS is rate-limiting with rate constant k, the rate is v = k[AS] = k[A]T K[S]/(1 + K[S]). At low [S], v ≈ k[A]T K[S] (first order in substrate); at high [S], v ≈ k[A]T (substrate saturation). This derivation explains why catalytic rate can become independent of substrate concentration at high substrate loading. Real cycles may require additional steps, inhibition terms or multiple catalyst states.

Laboratory experiment and data interpretation

Study a model homogeneous hydrogenation or carbonylation reaction only under an approved institutional protocol and with trained supervision. Use a pressure-rated reactor where gas pressure is involved; never improvise a sealed vessel. Run a blank without catalyst, vary catalyst loading and substrate concentration independently, and collect time-course samples. Determine conversion and selectivity by calibrated GC, HPLC or NMR with an internal standard. Compare initial rates, calculate turnover frequency using a clearly defined catalyst-site basis, and examine catalyst stability over repeated cycles. Record temperature, pressure, stirring rate, gas uptake, sampling losses and mass balance. Handle CO, H₂, flammable solvents and metal-containing waste under the relevant safety procedures.

Challenging structured examination questions with model answers

Question 1

For a neutral complex [IrCl(CO)(PPh₃)₂], assign the formal oxidation state and d-electron count of Ir, then estimate the electron count using the ionic method.

Model answer

CO and PPh₃ are neutral L-type ligands; Cl is X-type, −1. For a neutral complex Ir is +1. Ir is group 9, so d⁸. Ionic donations: Cl⁻ donates 2 e, CO 2 e, two PPh₃ donate 4 e; total = 8 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 16 e. This is consistent with a common square-planar d⁸ Ir(I) complex.

Question 2

A catalyst converts 0.250 mol substrate in 30.0 min using 2.00 × 10⁻⁵ mol active metal sites. Calculate the average turnover frequency.

Model answer

TON = 0.250/(2.00 × 10⁻⁵) = 12,500. Average TOF = 12,500/30.0 = 416.7 min⁻¹, or 6.94 s⁻¹. Clarify that this is an average over the stated interval.

Question 3

For the binding-controlled model in the derivation, calculate the fractional catalyst occupancy when K = 40.0 M⁻¹ and [S] = 0.0500 M.

Model answer

Fraction occupied = K[S]/(1 + K[S]) = 2.00/3.00 = 0.667. Approximately 66.7% of total catalyst is in the substrate-bound state.

Question 4

Explain the structural requirements for β-hydride elimination and predict one reason it may be suppressed.

Model answer

A metal–alkyl must contain a β-hydrogen, the M–C–C–H arrangement must reach a suitable syn-coplanar geometry, and a vacant or accessible coordination site is generally required. It can be suppressed by absence of β-H, geometric constraints, strong ligand saturation or competing pathways.

Question 5

A reaction rate increases with catalyst concentration but becomes independent of substrate concentration at high [S]. Explain this behaviour using the derived model.

Model answer

At low [S], catalyst occupancy rises with [S], so the rate is substrate-dependent. At high [S], nearly all catalyst is present as AS; [AS] approaches total catalyst concentration and the rate approaches k[A]T, giving substrate saturation.

Academic standard: In examination and laboratory reports, show assumptions, balanced equations, units, significant figures, uncertainty and the chemical interpretation of each result. Distinguish observations from mechanistic inference.
