Chapter overview and learning outcomes

This chapter develops a structured understanding of foundations of nursing and professional practice. Learners should connect scientific knowledge with person-centred nursing, communicate clearly, use accurate terminology and recognise the limits of simplified models. Clinical examples are educational; patient assessment and care require qualified supervision, institutional policy and current local guidance.

1. Meaning, nature and scope of nursing

Nursing is a health profession concerned with promoting health, preventing illness, supporting recovery, reducing suffering and protecting dignity across the lifespan. Its work includes health education, assessment, planning and evaluation of care, advocacy, coordination, rehabilitation and support during serious illness and end-of-life care. Nursing is both a scientific discipline and a relational practice: it uses biological and behavioural knowledge while responding to each person's values, goals, family context and social circumstances.

The word nurse should not be reduced to a list of tasks. A competent practitioner gathers relevant information, interprets it within an appropriate scope, identifies actual and potential care needs, plans evidence-informed interventions, communicates with the multidisciplinary team and evaluates whether care has achieved its intended outcomes. The nurse also recognises limits of competence and seeks supervision or referral when a need exceeds their role.

2. Historical development of nursing

Modern nursing developed through changes in sanitation, public health, hospital organisation, scientific knowledge, professional education and social reform. Historical accounts commonly discuss the influence of Florence Nightingale on environmental sanitation, systematic observation, nursing education and the use of statistics to improve care. Nursing traditions in different regions also reflect community caregiving, indigenous knowledge, religious organisations, colonial histories and local health-system development.

History should be studied critically rather than as a sequence of heroic biographies. Ask whose work was recorded, which communities were excluded, how gender and social class shaped professional opportunities, and how nursing has responded to changing patterns of disease and technology. Contemporary practice builds on historical lessons while rejecting unsafe traditions and discrimination.

3. Philosophy, values and professional identity

Nursing philosophy considers what health, personhood, environment, caring, dignity and professional responsibility mean. Core values include respect, compassion, integrity, accountability, justice, confidentiality, advocacy and commitment to safe evidence-informed practice. Professional identity develops through education, reflective practice, supervised experience, ethical reasoning and participation in a team.

Values become meaningful through observable conduct: introducing oneself, listening without judgement, explaining care in accessible language, protecting privacy, obtaining appropriate consent, keeping accurate records, acknowledging errors and treating every person fairly. Cultural humility requires curiosity and respect rather than assumptions about a person's beliefs or preferences.

4. Nursing theories and conceptual frameworks

Nursing theories organise knowledge and guide assessment and care. Nightingale's environmental perspective highlights ventilation, cleanliness, light, noise and sanitation. Henderson's work emphasises assisting people with activities that contribute to health, recovery or a peaceful death. Orem's self-care deficit theory examines the relationship between self-care demands and a person's ability to meet them. Roy's adaptation model considers responses to environmental stimuli. Watson's caring science explores human caring relationships.

A framework is a lens, not a rigid formula. Select a model that fits the clinical question, patient context and learning objective. Explain how its concepts influence assessment, goals and evaluation. No single theory replaces clinical judgement, patient preferences, current evidence or local policy.

5. Person-centred and holistic care

Person-centred care recognises the patient as a partner rather than a passive recipient. It considers physical symptoms alongside psychological wellbeing, relationships, culture, spirituality, communication needs, finances, housing, disability, health literacy and personal goals. Holistic assessment helps identify factors that influence a person's ability to follow a care plan or access services.

Shared decision-making involves explaining reasonable options, benefits, harms and uncertainties; eliciting what matters to the person; checking understanding; and agreeing on a plan where possible. Respect autonomy even when a person makes a choice different from the professional's preference, subject to applicable law and immediate safety duties.

6. The nursing process and clinical reasoning

The nursing process is commonly organised as assessment, nursing diagnosis or problem identification, planning, implementation and evaluation. Assessment gathers subjective information (what the person reports) and objective information (what is observed or measured). Analysis identifies patterns, risks and strengths. Planning establishes prioritised, measurable goals and appropriate interventions. Implementation documents what was done and how the person responded. Evaluation determines whether goals were met or the plan needs revision.

Clinical reasoning is iterative. New observations can change priorities. Use structured approaches taught by the institution, distinguish urgent threats from longer-term needs, consider alternative explanations and escalate deterioration promptly. Nursing diagnoses and terminology systems vary by country and institution; use the approved framework in the relevant programme.

7. Communication and therapeutic relationships

Therapeutic communication is purposeful, respectful and focused on the person's needs. Skills include attentive listening, open questions, clarification, reflection, summarising, silence when appropriate and checking understanding. Avoid false reassurance, judgemental language, unnecessary jargon and promises that cannot be kept. Adapt communication for language, hearing, vision, cognition, age and distress.

Professional boundaries protect both patient and practitioner. Avoid exploiting relationships, sharing confidential information casually, accepting inappropriate gifts or communicating through unauthorised channels. When interpreters are needed, use approved services and speak to the patient rather than treating the interpreter as the decision-maker.

8. Consent, capacity, confidentiality and advocacy

Valid consent requires appropriate information, voluntariness and decision-making capacity for the particular decision, as defined by applicable law. Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate. Give information in a form the person can understand, allow questions and respect refusal. Where capacity is uncertain, follow local legal processes and involve authorised representatives only as permitted.

Confidentiality is fundamental but not absolute. Information may need to be shared for direct care, safeguarding or legal reasons under defined conditions. Share only what is necessary with authorised recipients, use secure systems and follow local data-protection law. Advocacy includes helping people express preferences, understand choices and access appropriate services.

9. Ethics, accountability and professional regulation

Ethical reasoning often considers autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. These principles can conflict, so decisions require context, dialogue, evidence and reference to professional codes and law. Accountability means being answerable for one's actions, omissions, decisions and documentation. Delegation must consider the task, the recipient's competence, available supervision and the practitioner's continuing responsibility under local rules.

Nurses must practise within the scope authorised by their regulator, employer and competence. Do not undertake unfamiliar procedures, administer medicines without required checks or conceal mistakes. Raise concerns through appropriate channels and follow incident-reporting policies. Regulatory names and rules differ by jurisdiction; students should consult the current nursing council or board.

10. Evidence-based practice, reflection and lifelong learning

Evidence-based nursing integrates the best available research, clinical expertise, patient preferences and the care context. A useful question identifies the population, intervention or exposure, comparison and outcome where appropriate. Appraise study design, bias, sample size, relevance, uncertainty and whether findings apply to the local population.

Reflective practice examines an experience, the reasoning behind actions, the outcome and what should change next time. Reflection is not a substitute for formal incident reporting or supervision. Maintain learning records, seek feedback, update knowledge and use approved guidelines rather than relying on outdated notes.

Integrated case-based learning

Case: A student nurse is assigned to observe an adult patient during a supervised learning placement. The patient has a change from their usual condition and expresses concern about the proposed care. The student must gather relevant information, listen respectfully, check identity and consent, consider immediate safety, communicate findings to the supervising nurse and document objectively.

Discussion: Identify the information that is subjective and objective. Which findings require prompt escalation? What additional context would help interpretation? How can the student preserve dignity and confidentiality? Which actions are within the student's authorised competence, and which require direct supervision? Explain how the chapter's concepts support safe decisions without attempting to diagnose or prescribe independently.

Learning point: Sound nursing practice combines scientific knowledge, careful observation, communication, ethical conduct, teamwork and timely escalation. A textbook scenario is not a substitute for local protocols or direct clinical supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For longer questions, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and relate the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define nursing and identify four major purposes of nursing practice.

    Show answer guidance

    Nursing is a professional discipline that promotes health, prevents illness, supports recovery, relieves suffering and protects dignity. Examples include health promotion, prevention, care coordination and rehabilitation.

  2. Outline the five stages of the nursing process.

    Show answer guidance

    Assessment; analysis or nursing diagnosis; planning; implementation; evaluation. The process is iterative and should be revised when new information emerges.

  3. Differentiate autonomy and beneficence.

    Show answer guidance

    Autonomy concerns respect for a person's informed choices; beneficence concerns acting to promote wellbeing. Ethical decisions may require balancing these and other principles.

  4. State three features of effective therapeutic communication.

    Show answer guidance

    Active listening, open questions, clarification, empathy, summarising and checking understanding are examples.

  5. Explain why accurate documentation is important.

    Show answer guidance

    It supports continuity, communication, accountability, evaluation, legal record-keeping and patient safety.

  6. Differentiate anatomy from physiology.

    Show answer guidance

    Anatomy studies body structures and their relationships; physiology studies how those structures function.

  7. Name the four primary tissue classes.

    Show answer guidance

    Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

  8. Describe the role of the plasma membrane.

    Show answer guidance

    It forms a selectively permeable boundary, regulates transport, supports signalling and contributes to cell recognition.

  9. State two functions of the integumentary system.

    Show answer guidance

    Barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, limiting water loss and vitamin D synthesis.

  10. Explain the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems.

    Show answer guidance

    The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS.

  11. Trace the normal flow of blood through the heart.

    Show answer guidance

    Venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  12. What is cardiac output?

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute; it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.

  13. State the main function of alveoli.

    Show answer guidance

    Alveoli provide a large, thin surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

  14. Name the three basic processes of urine formation.

    Show answer guidance

    Glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion.

  15. List the links in the chain of infection.

    Show answer guidance

    Infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host.
