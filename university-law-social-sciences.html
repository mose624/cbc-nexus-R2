Learning scope and jurisdictional approach

Law and social sciences study rules, institutions, power, behaviour, culture and social relationships. Legal rules differ by jurisdiction and change over time. Every country-specific legal explanation must name the jurisdiction, identify the relevant legal instruments and state the date or currency of the material. These notes are educational and are not legal advice.

Foundations of law and social inquiry

What is law?

Law comprises recognised rules and institutions through which a political community regulates conduct, resolves disputes and allocates authority. Legal systems may draw on legislation, constitutions, judicial precedent, customary law, religious law and international obligations in different combinations. The hierarchy and status of each source depend on the jurisdiction.

Jurisprudence

Jurisprudence examines the nature, authority and purpose of law. Natural-law approaches connect legal validity or legitimacy with moral principles; legal positivism distinguishes legal validity from moral merit; legal realism studies how institutions and decision-makers operate in practice; critical approaches examine power, inequality and the social effects of legal rules. These traditions contain internal disagreements and should be compared rather than reduced to slogans.

Constitutional systems

Constitutional study considers the source and limits of public power, separation of powers, checks and balances, federal or unitary arrangements, rights protection, judicial review and amendment procedures. The written text must be read with relevant statutes, authoritative judgments, conventions and institutional practice in the named country.

Core legal subject areas

Public and constitutional law

State institutions, legislative authority, executive power, judicial independence, administrative fairness, rights and remedies.

Contract and commercial law

Agreement, offer and acceptance, consideration or equivalent doctrines, capacity, terms, breach, remedies, business entities and commercial regulation—subject to local law.

Criminal law and justice

Elements of offences, mental and physical elements, defences, burden and standard of proof, procedure, sentencing principles and due process.

Property and land law

Ownership, possession, interests, registration, transfer, leases, security rights, land use and customary tenure in jurisdictional context.

International law

Treaties, custom, general principles, state responsibility, jurisdiction, international organisations, human rights and peaceful dispute settlement.

Legal method and advocacy

Case reading, statutory interpretation, ratio and obiter, precedent, legal research, structured argument and accurate citation.

Legal problem-solving method

Identify legal issue → state applicable rule and jurisdiction → interpret authority → apply facts → consider counterarguments → reach qualified conclusion

Use a structured IRAC-style approach: Issue, Rule, Application and Conclusion. Strong analysis identifies uncertainty, distinguishes binding from persuasive authority and explains why a precedent or statutory provision applies to the facts.

Political science and social sciences

Political science

Political science studies power, institutions, political behaviour, public policy, states, parties, elections, governance and international relations. Compare pluralist, elite, institutionalist, behavioural, feminist and political-economy approaches while examining the assumptions and evidence each uses.

Sociology

Sociology investigates social structures, groups, institutions, inequality, identity, socialisation, stratification, urbanisation, work, family, education and social change. Functionalist, conflict, symbolic-interactionist, feminist and postcolonial perspectives ask different questions about order, power, meaning and transformation.

Psychology

Psychology studies behaviour and mental processes through biological, cognitive, developmental, social, clinical and cultural approaches. Research findings depend on operational definitions, sampling, measurement validity, replication and context. Avoid treating group averages as predictions about every individual.

Anthropology

Anthropology examines human diversity across cultural, social, linguistic, biological and archaeological dimensions. Ethnography and participant observation can provide contextual insight, but require reflexivity, consent, confidentiality and attention to power relations between researchers and communities.

Social-science research methods

Quantitative research measures patterns and tests hypotheses using structured data; qualitative research examines meanings, processes and experiences; mixed methods integrates both where the research question justifies it. Sampling should fit the target population and inference goal. Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether an instrument measures the intended construct.

Ethics and evidence

Obtain appropriate ethics approval, informed consent where required, protect confidentiality, minimise harm, disclose conflicts and represent participants fairly. Research with vulnerable groups requires additional safeguards. A correlation is not necessarily causal; causal analysis requires careful design and explicit assumptions.

Comparative and country-specific analysis

When comparing legal or political institutions, define the cases and period, explain selection, identify comparable concepts and avoid assuming that identical labels mean identical practices. For legal research, cite the relevant country's constitution, statutes, regulations and judgments with dates and official references.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Distinguish legal validity from moral legitimacy.
Legal validity concerns whether a rule is recognised as law under the relevant system's criteria; moral legitimacy concerns whether it is justified ethically or politically. Different jurisprudential theories relate the concepts differently.
2. Why must a legal answer identify its jurisdiction?
Sources, hierarchy, procedures, rights and interpretations vary between legal systems and may change through legislation or judgments. A correct answer in one country may be wrong in another.
3. Compare conflict theory and functionalism in sociology.
Functionalism examines how institutions contribute to social order and continuity; conflict approaches focus on power, competition and unequal distribution. Both require evidence and careful attention to variation.
4. Explain reliability and validity in research.
Reliability is consistency of measurement; validity concerns whether the measure captures the construct or inference it claims to represent. A measure can be reliable without being valid.
5. Outline an ethical approach to interviewing a marginalised community.
Engage respectfully, obtain appropriate approvals and informed consent, explain risks and withdrawal rights, protect identity, avoid coercion, compensate fairly where appropriate, share findings responsibly and reflect on researcher power.

Academic use, safety and scope

Follow your university's current syllabus and consult qualified lecturers or supervisors. Frequently asked questions

Concise answers to common university study questions. Use the chapter explanations and recommended institutional materials for deeper study.

1. What subjects are included in law and social sciences?

The learning area includes legal principles, jurisprudence, constitutional systems, international law, political science, sociology, psychology, anthropology and research methods.

2. Are the legal notes valid in every country?

Legal rules differ by jurisdiction. Country-specific material must identify the country, relevant legal instruments and date of currency; these notes are not legal advice.

3. What is jurisprudence?

Jurisprudence is the philosophical and theoretical study of law, including legal authority, interpretation, justice, rights and the relationship between law and society.

4. Why are research methods important in social sciences?

They help researchers frame answerable questions, select appropriate designs, collect and analyse evidence ethically, and explain the limits of conclusions.

Chapter 1: Foundations of Law, Society and Social Inquiry

Learning outcomes. Explain the nature and functions of law; distinguish legal rules from moral and social norms; introduce major jurisprudential approaches; identify the importance of jurisdiction; and compare foundational methods used in social-science research.

1.1 What is law?

Law is a system of recognised rules, institutions, procedures and practices through which a political community regulates conduct, resolves disputes and exercises public authority. Legal systems differ in sources, institutions and interpretive traditions. Law may protect rights, coordinate social and economic activity, allocate powers, provide remedies and express public values. It can also reproduce unequal power or be used oppressively. A rigorous analysis asks not only what a rule says, but who made it, how it is interpreted, whom it affects and what remedies exist.

1.2 Law, morality and social norms

Legal rules are not identical to moral principles or social customs. Some conduct may be socially disapproved but lawful; some legal duties may be contested on moral grounds. Social norms influence behaviour through expectations, reputation and informal sanctions. Law may incorporate moral concepts, but legal validity and moral legitimacy are distinct questions in many jurisprudential debates. Students should identify whether an argument concerns what the law currently is, what the law ought to be or how law operates in practice.

1.3 Sources of law and jurisdiction

Sources may include constitutions, statutes, delegated legislation, judicial decisions, customary law, treaties and other recognised instruments. Their authority and relationship vary by jurisdiction. A constitution may be supreme in one system; another system may organise authority differently. Precedent may bind lower courts under defined conditions, while persuasive decisions may inform reasoning without being binding. Customary law may be recognised subject to constitutional, statutory and human-rights limits. International law interacts with domestic law through each country's constitutional and legislative arrangements.

Jurisdiction identifies the legal authority of a court, legislature or state over persons, territory, subject matter or events. Legal content must therefore name the country and, where relevant, state or province, court level, statute and date. A rule from one jurisdiction must not be presented as universal. Legal materials change through legislation, amendments, judicial interpretation and treaty developments.

1.4 Jurisprudence: major approaches

Natural law traditions explore relationships between law, reason, morality and justice. Legal positivism examines legal validity through social sources and recognised rules rather than requiring moral merit. Historical approaches study law as an institution shaped by social development and tradition. Legal realism examines how institutions and decision-makers operate in practice, including the influence of facts, incentives and discretion. Critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence, critical race approaches and postcolonial perspectives examine how legal structures may reflect or reproduce power and inequality. These approaches are diverse and should not be reduced to slogans.

1.5 Constitutionalism and public power

Constitutionalism concerns the organisation and limitation of public power. Common themes include separation of powers, checks and balances, legality, accountability, rights protection, judicial independence and democratic participation. The precise arrangements differ across states. A constitutional text must be read with relevant legislation, judicial decisions, institutional practice and political context. A written guarantee does not automatically demonstrate effective protection; enforcement, access to justice and institutional capacity matter.

1.6 International law foundations

International law regulates relations among states and other recognised actors. Treaties create obligations for parties under applicable rules; customary international law develops through sufficiently general state practice accepted as law. General principles and judicial decisions may also contribute to legal reasoning under relevant frameworks. International organisations operate under their constituent instruments and powers. International rules do not automatically have identical domestic effect in every country; incorporation, direct effect and hierarchy depend on the jurisdiction's constitutional system.

1.7 Social sciences and levels of explanation

Social sciences study human behaviour, institutions, relationships and cultures using systematic empirical and theoretical approaches. Sociology may examine institutions, stratification and social change; psychology studies behaviour and mental processes; anthropology investigates human diversity and cultural meaning; political science examines power, governance and collective decisions. These fields overlap but use different concepts and methods. Explanations may operate at individual, group, organisational, institutional or structural levels. Strong analysis avoids assuming that one level explains every outcome.

1.8 Research design, evidence and ethics

Qualitative methods include interviews, observation, focus groups, document analysis and ethnography. Quantitative methods include surveys, experiments, administrative data and statistical modelling. Mixed-method designs combine approaches where doing so answers the research question more effectively. Sampling affects representativeness; measurement affects validity; researcher position and context affect interpretation. Correlation does not establish causation, and a statistically significant association may be small or socially unimportant.

Ethical social research requires informed consent where applicable, confidentiality, secure data handling, minimisation of harm, fair treatment and attention to power differences. Research involving vulnerable groups or sensitive information requires heightened safeguards and appropriate review. Legal research also requires accurate citation, careful distinction between binding and persuasive authority, and transparent acknowledgement of uncertainty or conflicting decisions.

Chapter summary and review

  • Law has institutional, normative and practical dimensions.
  • Legal validity, moral legitimacy and social effectiveness are distinct analytical questions.
  • Sources of law and legal hierarchy vary by jurisdiction and must be identified precisely.
  • Jurisprudential approaches offer different ways to understand legal authority, interpretation and power.
  • Social research requires a suitable design, transparent evidence and ethical safeguards.
  1. Distinguish legal validity from moral legitimacy with an example.
  2. Explain why a legal answer must identify its jurisdiction and date.
  3. Compare legal positivism and legal realism without treating either as a complete description of all legal systems.
  4. Differentiate a treaty from customary international law.
  5. Design a mixed-method study of access to justice and identify two ethical risks.

Chapter 2: Constitutional Government, Rights and the Rule of Law

Jurisdiction note: Constitutional rules differ by country. This chapter explains comparative concepts, not the current law of any particular state. A country-specific legal answer must identify the jurisdiction, applicable constitutional text, legislation and relevant judicial decisions, with an appropriate date.

2.1 Constitutionalism

A constitution establishes or recognises the basic framework of public authority. It may allocate powers among institutions, define procedures for law-making, protect rights and provide mechanisms for accountability. Some constitutions are contained primarily in a single written document; others draw on statutes, conventions, judicial decisions and institutional practice. The label “written” or “unwritten” does not alone determine how power is constrained in practice.

2.2 Separation of powers

Separation of powers distributes legislative, executive and judicial functions to reduce excessive concentration of authority. The legislature generally makes laws and scrutinises government; the executive administers policy and public services; courts resolve disputes and interpret law. In practice, systems vary: parliamentary and presidential arrangements create different relationships between branches. Checks and balances may include legislative oversight, judicial review, budget approval, confirmation procedures and independent audit institutions.

2.3 Rule of law

The rule of law is a principle that public power should be exercised under publicly accessible, prospective and sufficiently clear rules, with independent adjudication and meaningful remedies. It includes equality before the law and constraints on arbitrary action. Formal legality alone may be insufficient if people cannot access courts, legal assistance or remedies. A critical assessment therefore considers both legal design and institutional capacity, independence and practical access.

2.4 Rights and limitations

Constitutional and human-rights instruments may protect civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights. Rights can impose duties to respect, protect and fulfil, depending on the legal framework. Some rights are subject to lawful limitations; the tests and standards differ by jurisdiction and instrument. Proportionality analysis in systems that use it commonly asks whether a measure pursues a legitimate aim, is suitable, is necessary or minimally impairing, and strikes a defensible balance. Do not assume that every jurisdiction uses identical language or tests.

2.5 Judicial review and constitutional interpretation

Judicial review enables courts, where authorised, to assess the legality or constitutionality of public action. Interpretive approaches may emphasise text, structure, history, precedent, purpose, consequences or constitutional values. Courts may differ over how these sources should be weighted. A sound case analysis identifies the legal question, governing provisions, binding authorities, material facts, standard of review, reasoning and remedy. Distinguish a court's holding from obiter observations and from commentary by scholars.

2.6 Federalism and devolution

Federal systems divide constitutional authority between central and subnational governments. Devolved systems transfer powers from a central legislature or government to regional institutions under a particular constitutional arrangement. Allocation of powers may be exclusive, concurrent or shared. Fiscal arrangements, intergovernmental relations and dispute-resolution mechanisms influence how these systems work. The same label may conceal substantial differences between countries.

2.7 Comparative constitutional method

Comparative analysis should define the question and select jurisdictions for a reason, rather than collecting superficially similar provisions. Researchers examine text, institutional context, political history, judicial interpretation, enforcement and outcomes. A constitutional clause may operate differently where courts, legislatures, parties, media and civil society have different capacities. Avoid transplanting a legal solution without considering local institutions and social conditions.

2.8 Case analysis and legal writing

A structured case note identifies citation and court, material facts, procedural history, legal issues, arguments, decision, ratio decidendi, reasoning, remedy and significance. A legal essay should state a focused thesis, explain the governing framework, apply authority to evidence and address counterarguments. Citation conventions vary; follow the required jurisdictional or academic style. Never invent case names, statutory sections or quotations. Verify primary legal materials before relying on them.

Review questions

  1. Explain the purpose and limits of separation of powers.
  2. Distinguish formal legality from practical access to justice.
  3. Outline a proportionality analysis and explain why its precise form is jurisdiction-dependent.
  4. Compare federalism and devolution.
  5. Prepare a case-note structure that distinguishes a binding holding from obiter dicta.

Chapter 3: Sociological Theory, Institutions and Social Inequality

3.1 Society and social structure

Society consists of patterned relationships, institutions, norms and practices through which people organise collective life. Social structure refers to durable patterns of relationships and positions, including class, gender, ethnicity, age, occupation and citizenship. Institutions such as family, education, economy, religion, law and government shape opportunities and expectations. Institutions are not simply buildings or organisations; they include rules, roles and shared practices that persist over time.

3.2 Classical theoretical perspectives

Functionalist approaches examine how social institutions contribute to order, coordination and continuity, while also considering dysfunction and change. Conflict approaches focus on unequal control of resources and the ways power shapes institutions and social outcomes. Symbolic interactionist approaches examine how meaning is produced through everyday interaction, symbols and identity. These traditions are broad and internally diverse. They are analytical lenses rather than complete descriptions of every society.

3.3 Socialisation, identity and culture

Socialisation is the process through which people learn norms, values, roles and practical competencies. It occurs through families, peers, schools, workplaces, media and wider institutions. Culture includes meanings, practices, language, knowledge and material forms transmitted and contested within groups. Identity is relational and can change across contexts. Researchers should avoid treating cultures as homogeneous or static; individuals interpret, negotiate and sometimes resist shared norms.

3.4 Stratification and social mobility

Social stratification refers to patterned inequalities in resources, status and power. Class, income, wealth, education, occupation, gender, disability and other dimensions can intersect. Social mobility describes movement between positions across an individual's life or between generations. Absolute mobility and relative mobility are different: a society may experience broad income growth while relative chances remain unequal. Measurement requires clear definitions of class, income, wealth and opportunity.

3.5 Power, authority and social change

Power is the capacity to shape decisions, behaviour and outcomes. Authority is power regarded as legitimate under a particular social or institutional order. Social movements mobilise people around shared claims and can influence policy, identity and public debate. Social change may arise from technological innovation, demographic shifts, economic transformation, conflict, environmental pressures, institutional reform and cultural contestation. Explanations should consider both agency and structural conditions.

3.6 Education, health and inequality

Education can support social mobility, skills and civic participation, but access and outcomes may be shaped by income, geography, language, disability, gender, school resources and discrimination. Health outcomes are influenced by biological factors and social determinants such as housing, employment, nutrition, environment, education and access to care. Correlations between social position and outcomes require careful causal analysis; selection effects, measurement and institutional context can influence observed patterns.

3.7 Researching inequality

Quantitative studies may compare distributions, rates, gaps and trajectories; qualitative studies can reveal lived experience, institutional processes and meanings. Disaggregating data can reveal inequalities hidden by averages, but small samples create confidentiality and reliability challenges. Categories should be theoretically justified and developed with attention to the people being studied. Researchers should avoid presenting group-level patterns as predictions about every individual.

3.8 Ethics, positionality and reflexivity

Researcher positionality concerns how social position, institutional role, assumptions and relationships influence research. Reflexivity makes these influences explicit and examines how they shape questions, access, interpretation and reporting. Ethical research requires voluntary participation, informed consent where appropriate, confidentiality, protection from harm and fair representation. Community engagement can improve relevance, but participation must not become extractive or create unrealistic expectations.

Review questions

  1. Compare functionalist, conflict and symbolic interactionist perspectives on education.
  2. Distinguish social structure from social interaction.
  3. Explain why culture should not be treated as fixed or internally uniform.
  4. Differentiate absolute and relative social mobility.
  5. Discuss how social determinants can influence health outcomes.
  6. Explain positionality and reflexivity in qualitative research.

Chapter 4: Political Science, Governance and Research Methods

4.1 The state, nation and government

The state is commonly analysed through territory, population, institutions and claims to sovereign authority. A nation refers to a community connected through shared identity, history, language or political aspiration; a nation-state is one possible relationship between nation and state, not a universal condition. Government refers to institutions and office-holders exercising public authority at a particular time. Distinguishing these concepts prevents confusion in debates about sovereignty, citizenship, nationalism and state capacity.

4.2 Political authority and legitimacy

Political authority concerns the right or accepted capacity to make binding collective decisions. Legitimacy may be grounded in constitutional rules, electoral consent, performance, tradition, ideology or public acceptance. Different groups may evaluate legitimacy differently. State capacity includes the ability to implement decisions, raise revenue, provide services, maintain security and enforce law. Formal institutions may exist without equivalent practical capacity.

4.3 Democracy and representation

Democracy involves mechanisms for public participation and accountability, but institutional forms vary. Representative systems select officials to make decisions; direct mechanisms allow citizens to decide particular questions. Electoral systems translate votes into seats and can shape party systems, representation and coalition incentives. Free and fair elections require more than voting day: participation, political competition, accurate administration, independent oversight, access to information and protection of rights matter.

4.4 Public policy process

Policy analysis commonly examines problem definition, agenda setting, formulation, adoption, implementation and evaluation. The stages are not always linear: feedback, crises, institutional veto points and political bargaining can reshape a policy at any stage. Evidence informs policy but does not determine values or distributional choices. Stakeholder analysis identifies affected groups, power relations, implementation capacity and potential unintended consequences.

Problem framing → agenda → policy options → decision/adoption → implementation → monitoring/evaluation → feedback and revision

4.5 Governance and accountability

Governance concerns how public, private and civic actors coordinate decisions and resources. Accountability can be vertical through elections, horizontal through oversight institutions and social through media and civic action. Transparency supports scrutiny but does not automatically produce accountability unless information is accessible, institutions can respond and consequences are credible. Corruption analysis should examine incentives, discretion, institutional controls and social context rather than attribute outcomes to simplistic cultural explanations.

4.6 Political economy

Political economy studies interactions between political institutions, power and economic processes. Taxation, public spending, regulation, trade and property rights distribute costs and benefits. Interest groups, firms, workers, voters and bureaucracies may influence policy through different channels. Analysis considers who gains, who loses, which institutions mediate conflict and how decisions affect long-term development. Distributional analysis is essential when evaluating aggregate economic indicators.

4.7 Research questions and methods

A research question should specify a phenomenon, population, place and time where relevant. Concepts must be operationalised into observable indicators. Qualitative case studies can examine processes and meaning; surveys estimate patterns in defined populations; experiments test interventions under controlled conditions; comparative designs investigate similarities and differences across cases; archival research traces institutional change. Method selection should follow the question, evidence and ethical constraints rather than fashion.

4.8 Causal claims and research transparency

Researchers should distinguish descriptive, explanatory and causal questions. Causal inference requires a credible counterfactual: what would have happened to the same unit under a different condition? Randomisation can support this comparison; observational methods require assumptions and careful design. Pre-registration, transparent coding, clear sampling, documentation and robustness checks improve credibility. Negative or inconclusive results are informative and should not be hidden.

Review questions

  1. Distinguish state, nation and government.
  2. Explain three dimensions of democratic accountability.
  3. Use a policy-cycle diagram to analyse a public education reform.
  4. Explain why transparency alone may not ensure accountability.
  5. Compare qualitative case studies and representative surveys.
  6. What is a counterfactual and why is it central to causal inference?

Chapter 5: Psychology, Anthropology and Human Behaviour

5.1 Psychology as an empirical discipline

Psychology studies behaviour and mental processes through biological, cognitive, developmental, social and clinical perspectives. Psychological explanations operate at multiple levels: neural processes, individual learning, relationships, institutions and culture. Research findings are probabilistic and context-dependent; group averages do not determine an individual's behaviour. Psychological concepts should be operationalised clearly before measurement.

5.2 Learning, memory and cognition

Learning involves relatively lasting changes in knowledge or behaviour associated with experience. Classical conditioning links stimuli; operant conditioning examines how consequences influence behaviour; observational learning involves acquiring behaviour through models. Memory includes encoding, storage and retrieval and is influenced by attention, prior knowledge, context and interference. Cognitive research examines perception, language, reasoning, decision-making and problem solving.

5.3 Development across the lifespan

Developmental psychology examines biological, cognitive, emotional and social change across life. Development reflects interactions among genes, environments, relationships, institutions and historical context. Theories may emphasise stages, continuous processes, social learning or ecological systems. No single theory explains all developmental variation. Research with children and vulnerable participants requires careful consent or assent procedures, safeguarding and age-appropriate methods.

5.4 Social psychology and group processes

Social psychology investigates how people perceive, influence and relate to one another. Attribution concerns explanations for behaviour; conformity and obedience examine responses to social influence; group processes include norms, identity, cooperation, conflict and leadership. Experimental findings require attention to sampling, context, demand characteristics, replication and ethical limits. Historical studies should be interpreted in light of modern research standards.

5.5 Anthropology and culture

Anthropology examines human diversity through biological, archaeological, linguistic and sociocultural approaches. Ethnography uses sustained engagement, observation and interviews to understand practices and meanings in context. Participant observation can reveal tacit routines but requires reflexivity about access, power and interpretation. Cultural relativism encourages understanding practices within context; it does not require abandoning critical analysis of harm or human rights.

5.6 Kinship, identity and social organisation

Anthropological study of kinship considers relatedness through descent, marriage, care, adoption and social recognition. Kinship systems vary and cannot be reduced to one universal family form. Rituals, symbols, exchange and institutions organise belonging and obligation. Migration, urbanisation, technology and economic change reshape identities and social networks. Researchers should avoid exoticising communities or presenting one informant as representative of an entire population.

5.7 Methods and ethics

Psychological research may use experiments, longitudinal studies, psychometrics, observation and interviews. Anthropological research often uses ethnography, oral histories, material culture and archival methods. Valid measurement requires reliability and construct validity; translation and cultural adaptation need careful testing. Ethical research addresses consent, privacy, deception where permitted, distress, power imbalance, reciprocity and secure data management.

5.8 Applied questions

  1. Compare classical conditioning and operant conditioning with examples.
  2. Explain why memory is reconstructive rather than a perfect recording.
  3. Discuss how social context can influence individual behaviour.
  4. Define ethnography and identify two strengths and two limitations.
  5. Explain cultural relativism and its relationship to human-rights analysis.
  6. Design an ethical study of student experiences of digital learning, including sampling, consent and confidentiality.
