Learning scope and jurisdictional approach

Law and social sciences study rules, institutions, power, behaviour, culture and social relationships. Legal rules differ by jurisdiction and change over time. Every country-specific legal explanation must name the jurisdiction, identify the relevant legal instruments and state the date or currency of the material. These notes are educational and are not legal advice.

Do not assume that constitutional provisions, court structures, rights, procedures or statutory wording are identical across countries. Verify current legislation and case law using official jurisdictional sources.

Foundations of law and social inquiry

What is law?

Law comprises recognised rules and institutions through which a political community regulates conduct, resolves disputes and allocates authority. Legal systems may draw on legislation, constitutions, judicial precedent, customary law, religious law and international obligations in different combinations. The hierarchy and status of each source depend on the jurisdiction.

Jurisprudence

Jurisprudence examines the nature, authority and purpose of law. Natural-law approaches connect legal validity or legitimacy with moral principles; legal positivism distinguishes legal validity from moral merit; legal realism studies how institutions and decision-makers operate in practice; critical approaches examine power, inequality and the social effects of legal rules. These traditions contain internal disagreements and should be compared rather than reduced to slogans.

Constitutional systems

Constitutional study considers the source and limits of public power, separation of powers, checks and balances, federal or unitary arrangements, rights protection, judicial review and amendment procedures. The written text must be read with relevant statutes, authoritative judgments, conventions and institutional practice in the named country.

Core legal subject areas

Public and constitutional law

State institutions, legislative authority, executive power, judicial independence, administrative fairness, rights and remedies.

Contract and commercial law

Agreement, offer and acceptance, consideration or equivalent doctrines, capacity, terms, breach, remedies, business entities and commercial regulation—subject to local law.

Criminal law and justice

Elements of offences, mental and physical elements, defences, burden and standard of proof, procedure, sentencing principles and due process.

Property and land law

Ownership, possession, interests, registration, transfer, leases, security rights, land use and customary tenure in jurisdictional context.

International law

Treaties, custom, general principles, state responsibility, jurisdiction, international organisations, human rights and peaceful dispute settlement.

Legal method and advocacy

Case reading, statutory interpretation, ratio and obiter, precedent, legal research, structured argument and accurate citation.

Legal problem-solving method

Identify legal issue → state applicable rule and jurisdiction → interpret authority → apply facts → consider counterarguments → reach qualified conclusion

Use a structured IRAC-style approach: Issue, Rule, Application and Conclusion. Strong analysis identifies uncertainty, distinguishes binding from persuasive authority and explains why a precedent or statutory provision applies to the facts.

Political science and social sciences

Political science

Political science studies power, institutions, political behaviour, public policy, states, parties, elections, governance and international relations. Compare pluralist, elite, institutionalist, behavioural, feminist and political-economy approaches while examining the assumptions and evidence each uses.

Sociology

Sociology investigates social structures, groups, institutions, inequality, identity, socialisation, stratification, urbanisation, work, family, education and social change. Functionalist, conflict, symbolic-interactionist, feminist and postcolonial perspectives ask different questions about order, power, meaning and transformation.

Psychology

Psychology studies behaviour and mental processes through biological, cognitive, developmental, social, clinical and cultural approaches. Research findings depend on operational definitions, sampling, measurement validity, replication and context. Avoid treating group averages as predictions about every individual.

Anthropology

Anthropology examines human diversity across cultural, social, linguistic, biological and archaeological dimensions. Ethnography and participant observation can provide contextual insight, but require reflexivity, consent, confidentiality and attention to power relations between researchers and communities.

Social-science research methods

Quantitative research measures patterns and tests hypotheses using structured data; qualitative research examines meanings, processes and experiences; mixed methods integrates both where the research question justifies it. Sampling should fit the target population and inference goal. Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether an instrument measures the intended construct.

Ethics and evidence

Obtain appropriate ethics approval, informed consent where required, protect confidentiality, minimise harm, disclose conflicts and represent participants fairly. Research with vulnerable groups requires additional safeguards. A correlation is not necessarily causal; causal analysis requires careful design and explicit assumptions.

Comparative and country-specific analysis

When comparing legal or political institutions, define the cases and period, explain selection, identify comparable concepts and avoid assuming that identical labels mean identical practices. For legal research, cite the relevant country's constitution, statutes, regulations and judgments with dates and official references.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Distinguish legal validity from moral legitimacy.
Legal validity concerns whether a rule is recognised as law under the relevant system's criteria; moral legitimacy concerns whether it is justified ethically or politically. Different jurisprudential theories relate the concepts differently.
2. Why must a legal answer identify its jurisdiction?
Sources, hierarchy, procedures, rights and interpretations vary between legal systems and may change through legislation or judgments. A correct answer in one country may be wrong in another.
3. Compare conflict theory and functionalism in sociology.
Functionalism examines how institutions contribute to social order and continuity; conflict approaches focus on power, competition and unequal distribution. Both require evidence and careful attention to variation.
4. Explain reliability and validity in research.
Reliability is consistency of measurement; validity concerns whether the measure captures the construct or inference it claims to represent. A measure can be reliable without being valid.
5. Outline an ethical approach to interviewing a marginalised community.
Engage respectfully, obtain appropriate approvals and informed consent, explain risks and withdrawal rights, protect identity, avoid coercion, compensate fairly where appropriate, share findings responsibly and reflect on researcher power.

Academic use, safety and scope

These are independent learning materials, not an accredited programme, professional qualification or substitute for institutional teaching. Module order, prerequisites and assessment standards vary. Follow your university's current syllabus and consult qualified lecturers or supervisors. For laboratory, clinical, engineering and legal activities, follow the applicable institutional procedures, professional standards and country-specific rules.
