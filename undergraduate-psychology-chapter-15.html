CBE NEXUS · UNDERGRADUATE PSYCHOLOGY · CHAPTER 15

Educational, Health and Community Psychology

Undergraduate textbook notes · Learning · Health behaviour · Inclusion · Community wellbeing

Learning outcomes: Explain major educational, health and community psychology concepts; evaluate learning and health behaviour models; plan inclusive and preventive interventions; analyse social determinants; and apply ethical, evidence-informed approaches across settings.

Introduction

Educational, health and community psychology apply psychological science to important human settings. They examine how learning environments, health systems and communities influence development, behaviour and wellbeing. The fields share a commitment to understanding people in context, strengthening participation and reducing avoidable barriers. This chapter connects theories and research to practical questions in schools, healthcare and community programmes.

1. Scope and foundations of educational, health and community psychology

Educational psychology studies how people learn and develop in educational settings and how teaching, assessment, relationships and institutions influence learning. It examines cognition, motivation, development, individual differences, classroom interaction, curriculum, inclusion and educational policy. Health psychology studies psychological, behavioural, social and biological influences on health, illness and healthcare. It addresses health promotion, prevention, adjustment to illness, treatment adherence, communication and healthcare systems. Community psychology focuses on people in their social and ecological contexts, including neighbourhoods, institutions, social networks, power, participation and collective action. Although these areas have distinct histories and professional pathways, they share an interest in improving wellbeing through evidence-informed work with individuals, groups and systems.

All three fields reject explanations that locate every difficulty inside an individual. A learner's progress may be shaped by teaching quality, language, nutrition, disability access, family circumstances and school resources. Health behaviour may depend on income, housing, food availability, discrimination and access to services as well as knowledge and motivation. Community wellbeing is influenced by social cohesion, safety, infrastructure, political participation and distribution of resources. Psychological interventions are most effective when they recognise these interacting influences. Practitioners should define the problem with affected people, consider structural conditions, select proportionate actions and evaluate outcomes at more than one level.

2. Theories of learning and educational development

Behaviourist approaches explain learning through relationships between environmental events and observable behaviour. Classical conditioning concerns learned associations between stimuli; operant conditioning concerns how consequences influence the future probability of behaviour. Reinforcement can increase a behaviour, while punishment may suppress it but can have unwanted emotional and relational effects. Cognitive approaches examine attention, memory, prior knowledge, mental representations and problem-solving. Constructivist perspectives emphasise that learners actively interpret new information in relation to existing knowledge. Social learning theories highlight modelling, observation, self-efficacy and the social context of learning.

Developmental theories help educators understand changes in reasoning, language, executive function, social understanding and identity. They should not be used as rigid age-based labels: development varies between individuals and is shaped by opportunity, culture, disability, health and experience. Effective teaching activates prior knowledge, makes learning goals explicit, provides examples and guided practice, checks understanding and gradually transfers responsibility to the learner. Retrieval practice, spaced learning, elaboration and feedback can support durable learning. A psychological theory is a guide to testable teaching decisions, not a substitute for observing whether students actually learn.

3. Motivation, engagement and self-regulated learning

Motivation concerns the reasons, direction, intensity and persistence of learning activity. Expectancy-value approaches propose that engagement is influenced by whether learners expect to succeed and whether they value the task. Self-determination theory highlights autonomy, competence and relatedness. Goal orientations distinguish a focus on developing competence from a focus on demonstrating ability or avoiding failure. These frameworks explain why identical rewards or teaching methods may affect learners differently. Motivation is influenced by task design, belonging, perceived relevance, feedback, prior experiences and material conditions such as time, safety and access to learning resources.

Self-regulated learning involves setting goals, planning, selecting strategies, monitoring understanding and reflecting on outcomes. Learners can be taught to break complex tasks into manageable steps, use retrieval questions, monitor confidence and change strategies when progress stalls. Metacognition is not simply thinking about thinking; it requires accurate monitoring and purposeful action. Teachers can model planning aloud, provide checklists and gradually reduce scaffolding. Persistent disengagement should prompt inquiry into curriculum relevance, language, bullying, unmet learning needs, fatigue, accessibility and family or economic pressures rather than an immediate assumption that the learner lacks effort.

4. Assessment, feedback and educational measurement

Educational assessment gathers evidence about knowledge, skills, understanding and progress. Formative assessment informs the next teaching and learning steps; summative assessment judges achievement at a defined point. Diagnostic assessment identifies strengths and specific learning needs, while screening identifies learners who may benefit from closer assessment. Assessment can use examinations, projects, practical tasks, portfolios, observation, oral explanation and performance demonstrations. A sound assessment aligns learning outcomes, teaching activities and the evidence collected. A test that measures reading speed may not fairly measure scientific reasoning when reading speed is not the intended construct.

Reliability concerns consistency of measurement, while validity concerns whether evidence supports the intended interpretation and use of scores. Validity is not a permanent property of a test: it depends on the population, purpose, administration and consequences. Fair assessment considers language demands, disability accommodations, cultural assumptions, access to materials and opportunity to learn. Feedback should identify what has been achieved, what needs improvement and a practical next step. Grades without guidance may communicate ranking but provide little information for learning. Assessment systems should also be examined for unintended effects such as narrowing the curriculum, excessive anxiety or teaching only to test formats.

5. Inclusive education, disability and neurodiversity

Inclusive education aims to ensure that learners can access, participate in and benefit from education alongside peers, with appropriate support. Disability may arise from interactions between a person's characteristics and environmental barriers. A learner's participation can be restricted by inaccessible buildings, inflexible teaching, stigma, communication barriers or assessment arrangements. Neurodiversity recognises variation in cognition and information processing, including autism, ADHD, dyslexia and other profiles. It does not imply that every individual has identical needs or that support should be withheld. Person-centred planning starts with the learner's strengths, preferences, goals and barriers.

Universal Design for Learning encourages multiple means of engagement, representation and expression so that teaching is accessible to a broad range of learners. Reasonable accommodations may include assistive technology, alternative formats, additional processing time, structured instructions, quiet spaces or communication support. Adjustments should preserve the essential learning outcome while removing irrelevant barriers. Educators should collaborate with learners, families and qualified specialists, respect privacy and avoid reducing a learner to a diagnostic label. Inclusion is evaluated through meaningful participation, belonging, progress and learner voice, not merely physical placement in a classroom.

6. Language, culture and educational opportunity

Language influences access to instruction, classroom participation, reading, assessment and relationships with teachers. Learners may use a home language, a community language and a language of formal instruction. A difference between these languages is not evidence of low intelligence or weak subject understanding. Teachers can support learning through explicit vocabulary teaching, visual explanations, discussion, examples and opportunities to connect new concepts with existing knowledge. Assessment should distinguish subject knowledge from language proficiency where these are separate objectives.

Cultural responsiveness requires curiosity about learners' experiences rather than assumptions that every member of a cultural group shares the same beliefs or practices. Examples, classroom interaction and definitions of participation may carry different meanings across contexts. Practitioners should examine whose knowledge is represented, whose voices are heard and whether institutional routines disadvantage particular learners. Socioeconomic conditions, migration, displacement, gender expectations and discrimination may influence opportunity. Culturally responsive practice combines high expectations with flexible support, partnership with families and critical reflection on institutional barriers.

7. Classroom relationships, behaviour and school climate

Classroom behaviour is influenced by developmental needs, task difficulty, relationships, reinforcement patterns, school rules, peer norms and environmental conditions. A functional assessment asks what happens before a behaviour, what the behaviour looks like and what consequences may maintain it. This approach helps educators identify whether a learner is seeking attention, escaping an overwhelming task, communicating distress or responding to an unmet need. It is important to avoid assuming function from appearance alone. Observation across settings and consultation with the learner can improve the accuracy of an explanation.

Positive classroom management combines predictable routines, clear expectations, engaging instruction, respectful relationships and proportionate responses. Behaviour support should teach useful alternatives and preserve dignity. Exclusionary discipline can interrupt learning and may be applied inequitably; schools should examine patterns across learner groups and consider restorative and supportive approaches where appropriate. School climate includes perceived safety, belonging, fairness, relationships and opportunities to participate. Bullying prevention requires consistent reporting routes, adult action, support for affected learners and attention to the peer and institutional conditions that allow harm to continue.

8. Learning difficulties and evidence-informed support

Learning difficulties can involve reading, writing, mathematics, language, attention, memory, executive functioning or broader developmental needs. A low score is a signal for inquiry rather than a diagnosis. Assessment should consider instructional history, attendance, language exposure, sensory access, health, emotional wellbeing, task demands and the quality of previous teaching. Specific learning disorders require qualified assessment using appropriate evidence and applicable diagnostic standards. Schools should not delay practical support while waiting for a label when a learner's needs are already evident.

Effective support is targeted, explicit, systematic and monitored. Reading support may include phonological awareness, decoding, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension, selected according to the learner's profile. Mathematics support can use concrete materials, visual representations, explicit strategy instruction and cumulative practice. Attention and executive-function supports may include predictable routines, chunked instructions, external reminders and reduced distraction. Progress monitoring asks whether the intervention is improving the intended skill and whether the learner can generalise it. If progress is limited, practitioners should review implementation, intensity, fit and possible alternative explanations.

9. Guidance, counselling and learner wellbeing in education

School guidance and counselling support learners' academic, social and personal development. Concerns may include transitions, relationships, grief, anxiety, bullying, identity, family stress, attendance and future pathways. Support should be developmentally appropriate, culturally responsive and within the practitioner's competence. A school counsellor or psychologist must explain confidentiality and its limits, especially where safeguarding or serious risk concerns arise. Staff should not promise absolute secrecy when information may need to be shared to protect a learner.

Brief supportive conversations can help clarify concerns, identify strengths, agree practical next steps and connect learners with appropriate services. Persistent or severe difficulties may require referral to qualified mental-health or healthcare professionals. Schools should maintain clear safeguarding procedures, record information securely and share only what is necessary with authorised people. Wellbeing programmes are most credible when they are supported by safe environments, manageable academic demands, anti-bullying action, trusted adults and accessible referral pathways. Assemblies or awareness posters alone cannot substitute for a responsive support system.

10. Health psychology: concepts, models and determinants

Health psychology examines how psychological processes, behaviour, biology and social environments influence health and illness. The biopsychosocial model considers biological factors such as physiology and genetics, psychological factors such as beliefs and coping, and social factors such as relationships, income and healthcare access. The model does not mean every illness is psychologically caused. Rather, it provides a framework for understanding how multiple influences can shape risk, experience, treatment and recovery. Practitioners must avoid blaming people for conditions that arise through complex causes.

Health is influenced by social determinants including education, employment, housing, food security, transport, clean water, environmental exposure and discrimination. Health inequalities are systematic differences in health opportunities and outcomes between groups. Individual behaviour matters, but choices are constrained by available resources and conditions. A person cannot reliably follow dietary advice if nutritious food is unaffordable or unavailable. Effective health promotion therefore combines accessible information with supportive environments, equitable services and policy-level action. Assessment should ask what makes a healthy behaviour feasible in the person's actual circumstances.

11. Health behaviour theories and behaviour change

The Health Belief Model proposes that health action may be influenced by perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy. The Theory of Planned Behaviour considers attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control as influences on intentions and behaviour. Social cognitive theory emphasises reciprocal influences among personal factors, behaviour and environment, including observational learning and self-efficacy. The COM-B framework proposes that behaviour requires capability, opportunity and motivation. These models can organise assessment, but none should be applied mechanically or assumed to explain every behaviour.

Behaviour-change work begins by specifying the target behaviour, context and desired outcome. Practitioners explore capability, opportunity, motivation, competing demands, social support and practical barriers. Goals should be collaborative, specific and realistic. Self-monitoring, prompts, action planning, feedback, problem-solving and environmental restructuring may help when matched to the mechanism of difficulty. Advice alone is often insufficient. A person may understand the benefits of physical activity but lack safe spaces, time, transport or accessible facilities. Behaviour-change plans should be revised in response to experience rather than treating setbacks as moral failure.

12. Stress, coping and adjustment to illness

Stress involves processes through which demands are appraised in relation to available resources. The transactional model distinguishes primary appraisal of what is at stake and secondary appraisal of coping options. Coping may be problem-focused, emotion-focused or meaning-focused, and people often use several strategies. The usefulness of a strategy depends on context: direct problem-solving may help when a situation is controllable, while emotional support or acceptance may be more appropriate when it is not. Coping categories should not be used to rank people as strong or weak.

Illness can affect identity, relationships, work, finances, independence and future expectations. Adjustment is not a simple sequence of fixed stages. People may experience uncertainty, fear, frustration, grief, hope and adaptation in different combinations. Psychological support can help with communication, coping, treatment decisions, symptom management and maintaining valued activities. Practitioners should recognise the medical realities of the condition and collaborate with healthcare teams. Psychological care complements, rather than replaces, appropriate medical assessment and treatment.

13. Pain, symptom experience and self-management

Pain is a complex sensory and emotional experience influenced by biological, psychological and social processes. Acute pain may serve a protective function, while persistent pain can continue beyond the expected period of tissue healing and may involve changes in nervous-system processing. Psychological factors can influence attention, distress, sleep, activity and coping, but this does not mean pain is imaginary. Pain reports should be taken seriously, and new, severe or changing symptoms require appropriate clinical assessment to investigate medical causes.

Psychological approaches to persistent pain may include education, pacing, graded activity within clinical guidance, relaxation, cognitive behavioural strategies, acceptance-based approaches and support for sleep and valued functioning. The goal is not to persuade a person that pain is unreal or promise elimination of symptoms. It may be to reduce distress, improve function and support quality of life alongside medical care. Plans should be individualised and coordinated with qualified clinicians, particularly where medication, physical rehabilitation or complex conditions are involved.

14. Adherence, health communication and shared decisions

Treatment adherence describes the extent to which a person's actions correspond with an agreed healthcare plan. The term should be used carefully because non-adherence may reflect cost, side effects, confusing instructions, access barriers, beliefs, competing responsibilities or disagreement with the plan. Labelling a patient as difficult can obscure the reasons a treatment is not being followed. Clinicians should ask open questions, listen without judgement and explore what is workable for the person.

Health communication should be clear, accurate, accessible and respectful. Teach-back asks a patient to explain information in their own words so the clinician can check how well the explanation was communicated. Shared decision-making combines the best available evidence with clinical expertise and the patient's preferences, values and circumstances. It is especially important when reasonable treatment options have different benefits, burdens or uncertainties. Informed consent requires understandable information, voluntariness and decision-making capacity as applicable. Communication should be adapted for language, literacy, disability and cultural context.

15. Chronic illness, rehabilitation and family support

Chronic conditions may require long-term treatment, lifestyle adaptation, symptom monitoring and changes in daily roles. Psychological support can help people manage uncertainty, maintain routines, communicate with clinicians and preserve valued relationships and activities. Rehabilitation often involves a multidisciplinary team, with goals related to function, participation and quality of life. Progress may be uneven, and a person's priorities can change over time. Practitioners should avoid defining success only through clinical measurements when participation and personal meaning also matter.

Family members and caregivers may provide practical, emotional and financial support, but they can also experience strain, role changes and reduced time for their own needs. With the patient's consent and appropriate privacy safeguards, family involvement can improve understanding and coordination. Support should not assume that every family relationship is safe or helpful. Caregiver assessment may identify the need for respite, information, peer support or referral. A strengths-based approach recognises the patient's agency while acknowledging that support systems and resources affect the feasibility of self-management.

16. Community psychology: ecological and systems perspectives

Community psychology examines the relationship between people and the social settings in which they live. Ecological models consider interacting levels such as individuals, relationships, organisations, communities and public policy. A concern such as youth unemployment may involve skills, transport, local economic conditions, employer practices, education access and national policy. An intervention focused only on changing young people's attitudes would be incomplete if suitable jobs and training opportunities are absent. Community psychologists therefore combine individual support with environmental and institutional analysis.

Prevention and promotion are central. Rather than waiting for crises, community approaches strengthen protective conditions such as social connection, accessible services, safe public spaces, education, participation and economic opportunity. Community capacity refers to the skills, relationships, leadership and resources that enable collective action. Practitioners should understand local history, informal networks and power relationships before proposing a programme. Communities are not homogeneous: different groups may have conflicting interests, unequal influence and different experiences of risk. Good practice makes these differences visible and creates fair opportunities to participate.

17. Participation, empowerment and community development

Participation means that people affected by decisions have meaningful influence over how problems are defined, priorities are set, programmes are delivered and results are interpreted. Consultation can range from simply informing residents to shared decision-making and community control. A meeting does not automatically demonstrate participation if the agenda, language, timing and decisions are controlled entirely by external organisations. Practitioners should ask who is absent, who speaks, whose knowledge counts and whether participants can see how their contributions changed the programme.

Empowerment is a process through which individuals and groups gain greater control over decisions and resources that affect their lives. It includes personal confidence, collective organisation and access to institutional power. Empowerment is not achieved by transferring responsibility to communities without providing resources or authority. Community development may involve local leadership, skills building, mutual aid, advocacy and partnership with public services. Ethical facilitation is transparent about constraints, funding and decision rights. It avoids promising outcomes that the practitioner cannot deliver.

18. Prevention, promotion and levels of intervention

Prevention is often described as primary, secondary and tertiary. Primary prevention aims to reduce the occurrence of a problem before it develops; secondary prevention seeks early identification and timely response; tertiary prevention aims to reduce complications and improve functioning after a condition is established. These categories are useful but can overlap. A school-based anti-bullying programme may combine universal prevention with targeted support for learners already affected. A community health programme may combine education, screening and ongoing care.

Universal interventions are offered to a whole population, selective interventions target groups with elevated exposure or risk, and indicated interventions support people showing early signs of difficulty. Each approach has benefits and limitations. Universal programmes can reduce stigma but may be too broad for specific needs. Targeted programmes can be more intensive but risk labelling or excluding people. Programme design should consider reach, accessibility, cultural fit, costs, possible harms and the strength of evidence. Evaluation should assess whether intended groups actually receive the intervention.

19. Community mental health and psychosocial support

Community mental-health approaches aim to make support accessible in everyday settings and reduce unnecessary barriers to care. Services may include early identification, psychoeducation, peer support, outreach, crisis pathways, rehabilitation and links to primary healthcare. A stepped-care model offers the least intensive appropriate support first, with review and escalation when needs require more specialised intervention. Stepped care must not become a reason to delay urgent assessment or deny specialist care to people with complex needs.

Psychosocial support after emergencies, displacement, violence or loss should prioritise safety, practical assistance, connection with trusted people and access to services. Psychological first aid is a humane, supportive approach that does not require people to recount traumatic experiences. It is not psychotherapy and should be delivered within training and safeguarding boundaries. Community workers need supervision, clear referral pathways and attention to their own wellbeing. Programmes should avoid imposing a single cultural model of distress or assuming that all affected people want the same form of support.

20. Health promotion and community-based public health

Health promotion enables people to increase control over factors affecting health. It may involve health education, supportive environments, community action, healthy public policy and reorientation of services towards prevention. Effective programmes connect information with opportunities to act. For example, nutrition education has limited impact if households cannot access affordable nutritious food. Physical-activity campaigns need safe, accessible places to move. Public health communication should be accurate, transparent about uncertainty and adapted to language and accessibility needs.

Community-based programmes benefit from local partnership and contextual knowledge. Planning should identify the health issue, affected populations, determinants, available resources and existing community initiatives. Programme objectives should be measurable but meaningful. Process indicators may include reach and participation; outcome indicators may include behaviour, service access or health-related quality of life. Equity analysis asks whether benefits reach people facing the greatest barriers. Ethical communication avoids fear, stigma and blame, especially when addressing infectious disease, substance use, sexual health or weight.

21. Social determinants, inequality and advocacy

Social determinants of health and education include the conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work and age, together with the wider distribution of power, money and resources. Poverty can influence nutrition, housing, stress, school attendance, healthcare access and exposure to environmental hazards. Discrimination can affect safety, trust, employment and service quality. These influences are not merely background variables; they shape the options available to individuals and communities. Psychological practice should therefore consider structural causes as well as personal experience.

Advocacy involves using evidence and partnerships to support changes in policies, institutions or resource allocation. Practitioners should distinguish empirical findings from value judgements and disclose uncertainty. Advocacy must respect the autonomy and priorities of the people represented rather than speaking over them. Data should be disaggregated where appropriate to identify unequal outcomes, with safeguards against re-identification and stigma. Ethical advocacy seeks fair access and participation and evaluates whether proposed policies actually improve conditions for affected groups.

22. Programme planning, implementation and evaluation

Programme planning begins with a needs assessment and a clear account of the problem, its determinants and existing strengths. A theory of change explains how activities are expected to produce outputs and outcomes. A logic model can map inputs, activities, outputs, short-term outcomes and longer-term impact. Objectives should specify who is expected to benefit, what change is intended and the time frame. Planning should include community participation, realistic resources, safeguarding, accessibility and risks of unintended consequences.

Implementation evaluation examines whether the programme was delivered as intended, who it reached, how participants experienced it and what adaptations occurred. Outcome evaluation asks whether the intended changes happened; impact evaluation considers whether the programme caused those changes compared with what might otherwise have occurred. Mixed methods can combine numerical indicators with accounts of experience. A programme may be effective in a small pilot but difficult to scale because of staffing, cost or cultural differences. Evaluation should inform adaptation, continuation or discontinuation rather than merely demonstrate success.

23. Research methods and evidence across settings

Educational, health and community psychologists use experiments, randomised controlled trials, quasi-experiments, cohort studies, surveys, interviews, observation, case studies and participatory research. Each method has strengths and limits. Randomisation can reduce confounding in a trial, but a controlled setting may differ from routine practice. Observational research can examine real-world patterns but is vulnerable to confounding. Qualitative methods illuminate meaning, context and mechanisms but require transparent sampling, reflexivity and analytic rigour.

Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research with professional expertise and the values, needs and circumstances of the people involved. Evidence hierarchies can help compare designs, but a research design is not automatically high quality simply because it has a prestigious label. Practitioners should examine sample characteristics, measurement, attrition, effect size, uncertainty, bias, feasibility and relevance to the local setting. Community knowledge and lived experience are important evidence about implementation and acceptability. Conclusions should be proportionate to the data.

24. Ethics, safeguarding and professional boundaries

Ethical practice across education, health and community settings includes respect for dignity, informed consent, competence, confidentiality, fairness, integrity and prevention of harm. Consent processes should use language and formats that participants can understand. Where children or people with impaired decision-making capacity are involved, practitioners must follow applicable law, professional standards and safeguarding procedures, including appropriate assent and authorised consent arrangements. Participation should be voluntary wherever possible and refusal should not lead to unfair treatment.

Confidentiality has limits where there is a serious safety concern, a legal duty or a safeguarding obligation. These limits should be explained before information is collected and revisited when circumstances change. Practitioners should share only necessary information with authorised people, document decisions and consult supervisors or safeguarding leads. Dual relationships, conflicts of interest, gifts, online contact and use of personal data require careful boundaries. Professional titles and scopes of practice vary by jurisdiction; students and unlicensed workers must not present themselves as qualified psychologists or clinicians.

25. Digital practice, telepsychology and accessibility

Digital interventions include online learning platforms, telepsychology, health applications, remote monitoring, digital self-help and community information systems. They can improve reach and flexibility for people who face distance, mobility or scheduling barriers. However, access depends on devices, connectivity, electricity, data costs, digital literacy, language, privacy and disability accessibility. Digital delivery can exclude precisely those groups who face the greatest barriers. A service should offer alternatives when digital access is unreliable or inappropriate.

Telepsychology and digital health require informed consent, identity and location procedures appropriate to the service, secure communication, data minimisation and clear plans for emergencies or technical failure. Practitioners must work within competence and applicable professional rules. Apps should not be assumed effective or safe because they are popular or commercially available. Organisations should examine evidence, privacy policies, accessibility, clinical governance and how user data may be shared. Digital tools should support human care rather than obscure accountability.

26. Applied case study: improving school participation

A secondary school reports rising absenteeism among learners in one year group. A narrow response might increase sanctions. A broader assessment examines attendance patterns, transport, health, bullying, caregiving, school climate, curriculum relevance, disability access, household economic pressure and relationships with teachers. Confidential interviews and learner participation reveal that some students miss school because of unsafe travel routes and fear of peer harassment, while others struggle to catch up after illness. Attendance data alone could not explain these mechanisms.

A coordinated response might include safe reporting routes, anti-bullying action, catch-up support, family partnership, referral to relevant services and collaboration with local transport or community stakeholders. Evaluation should track attendance, reported safety, learner belonging and academic participation, disaggregated carefully to identify unequal effects. The school should review whether the intervention reduces barriers rather than merely changing the recording of absence. The case illustrates ecological assessment, participation and the need to match intervention to causes.

27. Applied case study: community health behaviour

A community clinic observes low attendance at a preventive screening service. Staff initially believe that residents do not understand the benefits. Interviews and community discussions identify several barriers: clinic hours conflict with work, transport is expensive, instructions are unclear and some residents fear disrespectful treatment. The behaviour is therefore not explained by knowledge alone. A COM-B analysis would examine capability, opportunity and motivation, while a social-determinants analysis would examine the material and institutional conditions shaping access.

Possible responses include extended or mobile service hours, clearer multilingual information, respectful staff communication, transport partnerships and confidential opportunities to ask questions. Evaluation should assess service uptake, waiting times, user experience, equity and cost. It should also monitor unintended effects, such as longer queues or reduced access for other patients. The clinic should involve residents in reviewing the changes and communicate what has been improved. The case shows why service design and trust are central to health behaviour.

28. Integrating educational, health and community psychology

These fields share a commitment to understanding people in context, preventing avoidable harm, strengthening capability and improving access to opportunity. Educational psychology contributes knowledge about learning, development and inclusive teaching. Health psychology contributes models of health behaviour, illness adjustment and communication. Community psychology contributes ecological analysis, participation, empowerment and social change. Collaboration can connect schools, families, health services, community organisations and policy institutions while respecting professional boundaries and the different responsibilities of each service.

An integrated practitioner begins by defining the concern with the people affected, examining evidence at individual and system levels, identifying strengths and barriers, and agreeing measurable and ethical goals. Interventions should be proportionate, culturally responsive and accessible. Outcomes should include quality of experience, equity and unintended effects as well as target indicators. Professional humility is essential: no single theory or discipline explains every problem. The strongest practice combines scientific reasoning, lived experience, partnership, supervision and a willingness to revise decisions when evidence changes.

Structured university examination questions and model answers

For strong answers, define the relevant concept, explain the theory, apply it to a realistic example and evaluate limitations or ethical considerations.

1. Define educational psychology.

Model answer: The scientific study of learning, development and teaching processes and the application of psychological knowledge to improve educational experiences and outcomes.

2. Distinguish educational, health and community psychology.

Model answer: Educational psychology focuses on learning and educational systems; health psychology examines psychological and social influences on health and illness; community psychology studies people in ecological and collective contexts.

3. Explain self-regulated learning.

Model answer: A process in which learners set goals, plan strategies, monitor understanding and evaluate outcomes, adjusting their approach to improve learning.

4. Differentiate formative and summative assessment.

Model answer: Formative assessment informs ongoing teaching and learning; summative assessment judges achievement at a defined point.

5. What is assessment validity?

Model answer: The extent to which evidence supports the intended interpretation and use of assessment results for a particular purpose and population.

6. Explain Universal Design for Learning.

Model answer: A framework that provides multiple means of engagement, representation and expression to improve access and participation for diverse learners.

7. Why should language difference not be confused with low ability?

Model answer: Language proficiency and subject understanding are distinct; a learner may understand a concept but have difficulty expressing it in the assessment language.

8. What is functional behaviour assessment?

Model answer: A systematic examination of antecedents, observable behaviour and consequences to develop hypotheses about the factors maintaining behaviour.

9. Identify two elements of a supportive school climate.

Model answer: Safety, belonging, fair treatment, positive relationships, learner voice and accessible support are examples.

10. Explain the biopsychosocial model.

Model answer: Health and illness are understood through interacting biological, psychological and social influences rather than a single explanatory level.

11. What are social determinants of health?

Model answer: The conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work and age, shaped by the distribution of resources and power.

12. Summarise the Health Belief Model.

Model answer: Health action may be influenced by perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy.

13. What does COM-B stand for?

Model answer: Capability, Opportunity and Motivation are proposed as interacting conditions required for Behaviour.

14. Distinguish problem-focused and emotion-focused coping.

Model answer: Problem-focused coping attempts to change the stressor or its practical consequences; emotion-focused coping manages the emotional response.

15. Why is persistent pain not necessarily evidence of ongoing tissue damage?

Model answer: Persistent pain can involve altered nervous-system processing and complex biopsychosocial influences, although new or changing symptoms still require appropriate medical assessment.

16. Define shared decision-making in healthcare.

Model answer: A collaborative process combining evidence and clinical expertise with the patient's values, preferences and circumstances when choosing care.

17. What is an ecological perspective in community psychology?

Model answer: It examines interacting individual, relationship, organisational, community and policy influences on behaviour and wellbeing.

18. Explain empowerment.

Model answer: A process through which individuals and groups gain greater control over decisions, resources and conditions affecting their lives.

19. Differentiate primary, secondary and tertiary prevention.

Model answer: Primary prevention reduces new cases; secondary prevention identifies and responds early; tertiary prevention reduces complications and supports functioning after a condition is established.

20. What is psychological first aid?

Model answer: A humane, practical and supportive response that prioritises safety, basic needs, connection and access to services after distressing events; it is not psychotherapy.

21. Why is health education alone sometimes ineffective?

Model answer: People may lack the material opportunities, service access, time, safety or resources needed to act on information.

22. What is a logic model?

Model answer: A structured representation linking programme inputs and activities to outputs, short-term outcomes and longer-term impact.

23. Differentiate process and outcome evaluation.

Model answer: Process evaluation examines delivery, reach and experience; outcome evaluation assesses whether intended changes occurred.

24. State two ethical considerations when working with children.

Model answer: Developmentally appropriate assent and authorised consent, safeguarding, confidentiality limits, privacy and the child's best interests are key considerations.

25. Identify two challenges in telepsychology.

Model answer: Digital exclusion and privacy/security risks; other issues include emergency planning, accessibility and professional competence.

26. How should a school investigate rising absenteeism?

Model answer: Combine attendance data with learner and family consultation and assess health, safety, transport, bullying, access, teaching and socioeconomic barriers.

27. Give two ways a clinic could improve screening attendance.

Model answer: Adapt service hours or location and improve accessible communication and respectful engagement; involve community members in designing changes.

28. What is evidence-based practice?

Model answer: Integration of best available research, professional expertise and the values, needs and circumstances of the people receiving the service.

29. Why must community participation be meaningful?

Model answer: It gives affected people influence over priorities and decisions, improves contextual fit and reduces the risk of externally imposed or inequitable programmes.

30. Explain why psychological practice should consider structural conditions.

Model answer: Resources, institutions, discrimination and policy shape the choices and opportunities available to individuals; individual-level interventions alone may not address root causes.

Chapter synthesis and revision framework

Educational psychology links learning, development, motivation, assessment and inclusion. Health psychology connects biological processes, beliefs, behaviour, relationships and social determinants. Community psychology examines ecological systems, participation, empowerment, prevention and collective action. Across all three fields, effective practice begins with contextual assessment and collaboration with the people affected.

When designing an intervention, define the problem and intended outcomes, examine individual and structural influences, review the evidence, assess feasibility and accessibility, agree ethical safeguards, implement with appropriate supervision and evaluate both benefits and unintended consequences. A programme should be adapted when local evidence shows that it is not reaching the intended population or improving the outcome.
