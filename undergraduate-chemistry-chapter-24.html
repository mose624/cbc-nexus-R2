Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Surface free energy arises because atoms or molecules at an interface have an environment different from the bulk. Creating new surface area requires work, so finely divided solids and droplets often have high surface energy. Surface-to-volume ratio increases as particle size decreases, helping explain the high reactivity of many nanomaterials.

Adsorption is accumulation at an interface, whereas absorption is uptake into the bulk phase. Physisorption is dominated by dispersion and other weak intermolecular forces; chemisorption involves stronger chemical bonding and often greater specificity. Adsorption may be exothermic, so equilibrium coverage commonly decreases as temperature rises.

Adsorption rates can be limited by transport through a fluid boundary layer, pore diffusion, surface diffusion, attachment to sites or desorption. An observed rate law can therefore reflect transport rather than intrinsic surface chemistry. Vary particle size, stirring rate and temperature to test for transport limitations.

For A(g)+*⇌A*, the Langmuir model assumes identical sites and monolayer coverage. Equating adsorption and desorption rates gives θ=KP/(1+KP). At low pressure θ≈KP; at high pressure θ approaches one. The model ignores lateral interactions and site heterogeneity.

Freundlich adsorption is an empirical relation qe=KFCe^(1/n), useful over a limited concentration range for heterogeneous surfaces. BET theory describes multilayer gas adsorption and estimates specific surface area. BET fitting requires an appropriate relative-pressure region; microporous materials may need dedicated pore-filling models.

Wetting is determined by interfacial tensions. Young's equation is γSV=γSL+γLVcosθ. Small contact angle means good wetting and spreading; large angle means poor wetting. Roughness, chemical heterogeneity and contact-angle hysteresis complicate measurements on real surfaces.

A simplified catalytic sequence is bulk transport, adsorption, surface reaction, desorption and product transport away. The active site must bind reactants sufficiently strongly for activation but not so strongly that products cannot leave. A catalyst's activity and selectivity depend on surface structure and reaction conditions.

In a Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism both reactants adsorb before reacting. Surface coverages depend on partial pressures and adsorption constants; the rate may be proportional to θAθB. Competitive adsorption can inhibit one reactant when another occupies the same sites.

In an Eley–Rideal mechanism, one reactant from the gas or liquid phase reacts directly with an adsorbed species. Distinguishing it from Langmuir–Hinshelwood requires kinetic dependencies, isotope effects or operando spectroscopy; a fitted rate expression alone may not be unique.

Active sites include terraces, edges, steps, vacancies and metal-support boundaries. Particle size and morphology change their relative abundance. Supports disperse active particles, provide mechanical stability and can modify electronic structure; characterisation under operating conditions is especially valuable.

Micropores are below 2 nm, mesopores are 2–50 nm and macropores exceed 50 nm. Zeolites offer shape-selective micropores; mesoporous materials provide accessible channels; activated carbon has high internal area. Diffusion through narrow pores can limit turnover.

Colloids contain dispersed particles whose behaviour reflects Brownian motion, surface charge and interparticle forces. The electrical double layer and zeta potential influence stability. Electrolytes can screen charge and promote aggregation; steric stabilisers can provide an alternative barrier.

Surfactants adsorb at interfaces and lower interfacial tension. Above the critical micelle concentration, micelles form in suitable systems. Emulsions and foams are kinetically stabilised dispersions; droplet size, interfacial films, viscosity and electrostatic or steric repulsion influence lifetime.

Nanomaterials have high surface atom fractions and size-dependent optical, magnetic and catalytic behaviour. Characterise size distribution, morphology, surface ligands, aggregation and chemical state. A single mean diameter cannot describe polydispersity or distinguish primary particles from aggregates.

Catalyst deactivation can result from poisoning, fouling, sintering, phase transformation, leaching or mechanical attrition. Regeneration may remove deposits or restore oxidation state but can also change the active structure. Evaluate activity, selectivity and stability over repeated cycles.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Surface chemistry: adsorption models and catalytic cycles

1. Derivation of the Langmuir isotherm

Let θ be the fraction of occupied sites. For gas adsorption A(g)+* ⇌ A*, the adsorption rate is ra=kaP(1−θ), while desorption rate is rd=kdθ. At equilibrium kaP(1−θ)=kdθ. Rearranging gives θ=KP/(1+KP), where K=ka/kd. The model assumes identical independent sites, one adsorbate per site, monolayer coverage and negligible lateral interactions.

2. Langmuir–Hinshelwood rate expression

If A and B adsorb competitively, θA=KAPA/(1+KAPA+KBPB) and θB=KBPB/(1+KAPA+KBPB). If the surface reaction between adsorbed A and B is rate-limiting, r=krθAθB. Substitution gives a denominator squared. This predicts saturation and competitive inhibition at high coverage; real systems may require dissociative adsorption or multiple site types.

3. BET multilayer adsorption

BET theory considers multilayer adsorption. A common linear form is P/[V(P0−P)] = 1/(VmC) + [(C−1)/(VmC)](P/P0), where Vm is monolayer capacity and C relates to first-layer adsorption energy. The BET surface area is calculated from Vm and the molecular cross-sectional area of the adsorbate. The selected relative-pressure interval and assumptions must be reported.

4. Wetting and contact angle

At a three-phase contact line, horizontal interfacial tensions balance: γSV=γSL+γLVcosθ. A low contact angle indicates favourable spreading. Real surfaces may be rough, porous or chemically heterogeneous; advancing and receding angles can differ because of contact-line pinning and hysteresis.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Langmuir coverage

Problem: For K=0.80 kPa⁻¹ and P=2.0 kPa, calculate θ.

Solution: θ=KP/(1+KP)=1.6/2.6=0.615. Approximately 61.5% of ideal sites are occupied.

Worked numerical problem 2: Pressure for 90% coverage

Problem: For K=0.50 bar⁻¹, determine pressure required for θ=0.90.

Solution: θ/(1−θ)=KP. P=0.90/(0.10×0.50)=18 bar. High pressure is needed because the adsorption affinity is modest.

Worked numerical problem 3: BET monolayer capacity from slope and intercept

Problem: A BET plot has slope 0.0400 and intercept 0.0100 in consistent units. Find Vm.

Solution: Vm=1/(slope+intercept)=1/0.0500=20.0 volume units per sample mass. Surface area then requires gas molecular cross-sectional area and consistent STP volume conversion.

Worked numerical problem 4: Competitive adsorption

Problem: At a surface, KAPA=2 and KBPB=1. Calculate θA and θB.

Solution: Denominator=1+2+1=4. θA=2/4=0.50; θB=1/4=0.25; vacant fraction=0.25. The coverages sum to one.

Worked numerical problem 5: Surface reaction rate

Problem: For r=krθAθB, kr=0.020 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, θA=0.50 and θB=0.25.

Solution: r=0.020×0.50×0.25=0.00250 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The rate is per catalytic surface area under the assumed model.

Worked numerical problem 6: Contact angle balance

Problem: For γSV=72 mN m⁻¹, γSL=25 mN m⁻¹ and γLV=50 mN m⁻¹, calculate θ.

Solution: cosθ=(72−25)/50=0.94; θ=arccos(0.94)=19.9°. This predicts strong wetting for an ideal smooth equilibrium surface.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: List the main assumptions of the Langmuir model.

Answer: The surface has equivalent sites; adsorption is monolayer; each site holds one adsorbate; adsorbed molecules do not interact laterally; and adsorption/desorption reach equilibrium.

Model answer 2

Question: How can chemisorption differ from physisorption?

Answer: Chemisorption involves specific chemical bonding and often higher adsorption enthalpy and activation barriers. Physisorption is dominated by weaker intermolecular forces and is often more readily reversible.

Model answer 3

Question: Explain why a catalyst can be poisoned.

Answer: A strongly bound impurity occupies or modifies active sites, preventing reactant adsorption or changing surface electronic structure. Even trace concentrations can strongly reduce activity.

Model answer 4

Question: What information does BET analysis provide?

Answer: It estimates specific surface area from gas multilayer adsorption, subject to BET model assumptions, adsorbate choice and fitting range.

Model answer 5

Question: Why can a catalyst with high intrinsic activity perform poorly in a reactor?

Answer: Pore diffusion, external mass transfer, heat transfer, pressure drop, deactivation and inaccessible active sites can limit observed reactor performance.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: Why is a high surface area useful in heterogeneous catalysis?

Answer: It can expose more accessible active sites per unit mass, increasing potential reaction rate. Pore diffusion, site quality and sintering mean surface area alone does not guarantee high activity.

Model answer 7

Question: How do pore diffusion limitations affect observed kinetics?

Answer: Reactant concentration inside catalyst pores may be lower than in the bulk fluid. The measured rate then reflects transport as well as intrinsic surface kinetics and may depend on particle size.

Model answer 8

Question: What is catalyst selectivity?

Answer: Selectivity describes the fraction of converted reactant forming a desired product or pathway. A catalyst can accelerate conversion while favouring undesired products, so activity and selectivity must be measured separately.

Model answer 9

Question: Explain the significance of zeta potential in colloid stability.

Answer: Zeta potential approximates electrical potential near the slipping plane. Large-magnitude values often indicate electrostatic repulsion that resists aggregation, though steric stabilisation and solution conditions also matter.

Model answer 10

Question: Why can BET surface area be misleading for microporous materials?

Answer: Pore filling and restricted diffusion can violate the assumptions of multilayer adsorption on an open surface. Appropriate micropore models and complementary gas probes may be needed.
