Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Surface free energy arises because atoms or molecules at an interface have an environment different from the bulk. Creating new surface area requires work, so finely divided solids and droplets often have high surface energy. Surface-to-volume ratio increases as particle size decreases, helping explain the high reactivity of many nanomaterials.

Adsorption is accumulation at an interface, whereas absorption is uptake into the bulk phase. Physisorption is dominated by dispersion and other weak intermolecular forces; chemisorption involves stronger chemical bonding and often greater specificity. Adsorption may be exothermic, so equilibrium coverage commonly decreases as temperature rises.

Adsorption rates can be limited by transport through a fluid boundary layer, pore diffusion, surface diffusion, attachment to sites or desorption. An observed rate law can therefore reflect transport rather than intrinsic surface chemistry. Vary particle size, stirring rate and temperature to test for transport limitations.

For A(g)+*⇌A*, the Langmuir model assumes identical sites and monolayer coverage. Equating adsorption and desorption rates gives θ=KP/(1+KP). At low pressure θ≈KP; at high pressure θ approaches one. The model ignores lateral interactions and site heterogeneity.

Freundlich adsorption is an empirical relation qe=KFCe^(1/n), useful over a limited concentration range for heterogeneous surfaces. BET theory describes multilayer gas adsorption and estimates specific surface area. BET fitting requires an appropriate relative-pressure region; microporous materials may need dedicated pore-filling models.

Wetting is determined by interfacial tensions. Young's equation is γSV=γSL+γLVcosθ. Small contact angle means good wetting and spreading; large angle means poor wetting. Roughness, chemical heterogeneity and contact-angle hysteresis complicate measurements on real surfaces.

A simplified catalytic sequence is bulk transport, adsorption, surface reaction, desorption and product transport away. The active site must bind reactants sufficiently strongly for activation but not so strongly that products cannot leave. A catalyst's activity and selectivity depend on surface structure and reaction conditions.

In a Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism both reactants adsorb before reacting. Surface coverages depend on partial pressures and adsorption constants; the rate may be proportional to θAθB. Competitive adsorption can inhibit one reactant when another occupies the same sites.

In an Eley–Rideal mechanism, one reactant from the gas or liquid phase reacts directly with an adsorbed species. Distinguishing it from Langmuir–Hinshelwood requires kinetic dependencies, isotope effects or operando spectroscopy; a fitted rate expression alone may not be unique.

Active sites include terraces, edges, steps, vacancies and metal-support boundaries. Particle size and morphology change their relative abundance. Supports disperse active particles, provide mechanical stability and can modify electronic structure; characterisation under operating conditions is especially valuable.

Micropores are below 2 nm, mesopores are 2–50 nm and macropores exceed 50 nm. Zeolites offer shape-selective micropores; mesoporous materials provide accessible channels; activated carbon has high internal area. Diffusion through narrow pores can limit turnover.

Colloids contain dispersed particles whose behaviour reflects Brownian motion, surface charge and interparticle forces. The electrical double layer and zeta potential influence stability. Electrolytes can screen charge and promote aggregation; steric stabilisers can provide an alternative barrier.

Surfactants adsorb at interfaces and lower interfacial tension. Above the critical micelle concentration, micelles form in suitable systems. Emulsions and foams are kinetically stabilised dispersions; droplet size, interfacial films, viscosity and electrostatic or steric repulsion influence lifetime.

Nanomaterials have high surface atom fractions and size-dependent optical, magnetic and catalytic behaviour. Characterise size distribution, morphology, surface ligands, aggregation and chemical state. A single mean diameter cannot describe polydispersity or distinguish primary particles from aggregates.

Catalyst deactivation can result from poisoning, fouling, sintering, phase transformation, leaching or mechanical attrition. Regeneration may remove deposits or restore oxidation state but can also change the active structure. Evaluate activity, selectivity and stability over repeated cycles.
