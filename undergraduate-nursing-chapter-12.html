Foundations of mental health nursing

Mental health nursing promotes psychological wellbeing, supports people experiencing mental distress and helps prevent avoidable harm. Practice is grounded in dignity, human rights, cultural humility, therapeutic relationships, evidence-informed care and recovery-oriented principles. Mental illness is shaped by biological, psychological, social, cultural and environmental factors.

Avoid stigma, blame and assumptions. Ask how the person understands their experience and what support they find helpful. Care planning should be collaborative, strengths-based and proportionate, with the least restrictive safe approach. Legal frameworks for consent, capacity, involuntary care and confidentiality vary by jurisdiction.

Therapeutic communication and relationships

Therapeutic communication uses attentive listening, empathy, open questions, reflection, clarification, silence and summarising to understand the person's experience. The nurse should communicate honestly, consistently and respectfully, maintain appropriate boundaries and avoid judgement, false reassurance, arguing about beliefs or making promises that cannot be kept.

A therapeutic relationship has a professional purpose and clear limits. Consider power differences, trauma history, language, culture, neurodiversity and the person's preferred communication style. Document relevant statements and observed behaviour objectively, distinguishing the person's words from the nurse's interpretation.

Mental status examination

A mental status examination is a structured description of current psychological functioning. Common domains include appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Findings are interpreted with history, physical health, medicines, substance exposure and context.

Describe what is observed rather than using labels alone. For example, document speech rate, volume and coherence, or the person's own description of mood. An MSE is a time-specific assessment and does not independently establish a psychiatric diagnosis. Sudden mental-status change may reflect delirium, infection, metabolic disturbance or other medical causes.

Assessment, formulation and risk

Assessment explores presenting concerns, history, strengths, supports, trauma, physical health, substance use, functioning and the person's goals. Formulation organises biological, psychological and social factors that may contribute to distress and maintain difficulties. It remains provisional and should be revised as new information emerges.

Risk assessment is an ongoing process, not a prediction tool or checklist alone. Explore immediate safety, suicidal thoughts, self-harm, violence, exploitation, vulnerability, access to means and protective factors with sensitivity. Follow local policy, consult senior staff and escalate imminent concerns. Do not promise absolute confidentiality when safety or law requires disclosure.

Anxiety, stress-related and trauma-related disorders

Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear or worry and related behavioural or physical symptoms that cause distress or impairment. Trauma-related conditions may follow exposure to traumatic events and include intrusive experiences, avoidance, changes in mood or cognition and heightened arousal. Symptoms overlap with physical illness and medicine or substance effects.

Nursing support includes validation, calm communication, assessment of triggers and function, grounding strategies where appropriate, sleep and coping support, and referral for evidence-based psychological care. Avoid forcing disclosure of trauma. Severe symptoms, inability to maintain safety or new physical red flags require clinical assessment.

Depressive and bipolar disorders

Depressive disorders involve persistent changes in mood, interest, cognition, energy, sleep, appetite or functioning. Bipolar disorders involve episodes of depression and mania or hypomania, with changes in mood, activity, energy, sleep and judgement. Diagnosis requires qualified assessment of duration, severity, impairment and differential causes.

Ask directly and compassionately about thoughts of death, self-harm and suicide when indicated. Observe changes in sleep, activity, speech, impulsivity, functioning and treatment response. Support continuity, psychoeducation and collaborative safety planning. Urgent assessment is required for suicidal intent, severe mania, psychosis, inability to care for basic needs or rapid deterioration.

Psychotic disorders and altered perception

Psychosis describes experiences such as delusions, hallucinations or disorganised thinking that affect interpretation of reality. Psychotic symptoms can occur in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, mood disorders, substance-related states, neurological illness and other medical conditions. A careful assessment of onset, context, physical health and substance exposure is essential.

Do not ridicule, reinforce or aggressively challenge a person's beliefs. Acknowledge distress, focus on safety and practical needs, use clear simple communication and reduce excessive stimulation where helpful. Sudden onset, fluctuating attention, fever, intoxication or neurological signs require urgent medical assessment.
