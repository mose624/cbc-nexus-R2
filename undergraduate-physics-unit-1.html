Unit guide and mathematical standard

These notes are intended for undergraduate study. Work through the derivations before memorising results. Unless stated otherwise, use SI units, state assumptions, retain units through calculations and round final values to a precision justified by the data. Check limiting cases and dimensions as part of every solution.

Method for numerical problems: identify known and unknown quantities; draw a labelled diagram where useful; select a physical model; state assumptions; derive or rearrange the governing relation; substitute values with units; calculate; then interpret and check the answer.

1. Physical quantities, dimensions and units

A physical equation must be dimensionally homogeneous: every term added or equated must have the same dimensions. The SI base dimensions are mass [M], length [L], time [T], electric current [I], temperature [Θ], amount [N] and luminous intensity [J]. Velocity has dimensions [LT⁻¹], acceleration [LT⁻²], force [MLT⁻²], energy [ML²T⁻²] and pressure [ML⁻¹T⁻²]. Dimensional analysis detects impossible equations and helps construct scaling laws, but cannot determine dimensionless constants such as 1/2 or 2π.

2. Vectors and components

A vector A in Cartesian coordinates is A = Aₓi + Aᵧj + A_zk. Its magnitude is |A| = √(Aₓ²+Aᵧ²+A_z²). For a vector of magnitude A making angles θ and φ in a plane, components are Aₓ=A cosθ and Aᵧ=A sinθ. The dot product A·B=AB cosθ gives a scalar and is useful for work; the cross product A×B has magnitude AB sinθ and gives an oriented vector, as in torque.

3. Differential calculus in physics

The derivative dx/dt is the instantaneous rate of change of x. If position is x(t), velocity v=dx/dt and acceleration a=d²x/dt². For a scalar field f(x,y,z), the gradient ∇f points in the direction of greatest increase. Differentiation rules include the product rule d(uv)/dx=u'v+uv' and chain rule df/dx=(df/du)(du/dx).

4. Integral calculus and physical accumulation

A definite integral ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx accumulates a quantity over an interval. If force varies with position, work is W=∫ F(x)dx. If power varies with time, energy transferred is E=∫P(t)dt. Integration constants are fixed by initial or boundary conditions.

5. First-order differential equations

A separable equation dy/dx=g(x)h(y) can be rearranged as dy/h(y)=g(x)dx and integrated. Exponential relaxation obeys dN/dt=−kN, giving N=N₀e^(−kt). The constant k has units of inverse time, and the time constant is τ=1/k.

6. Complex numbers and oscillatory physics

A complex number is z=x+iy, with modulus r=√(x²+y²) and argument θ=tan⁻¹(y/x), with quadrant chosen correctly. Euler's identity e^(iθ)=cosθ+i sinθ allows sinusoidal signals to be represented compactly. The real physical signal is obtained by taking the real part of a complex representation.

7. Worked calculation: vector resultant

Let A=(3i+4j) m and B=(−i+2j) m. Then R=A+B=(2i+6j) m. Its magnitude is |R|=√(2²+6²)=√40=6.32 m. Its direction relative to +x is θ=tan⁻¹(6/2)=71.6°. The components are added algebraically before calculating magnitude.

8. Worked calculation: derivative and acceleration

A particle moves as x(t)=4t³−3t²+2 m, where x is in metres and t in seconds. Differentiate: v=dx/dt=12t²−6t m s⁻¹; a=dv/dt=24t−6 m s⁻². At t=2 s, v=48−12=36 m s⁻¹ and a=48−6=42 m s⁻².

9. Worked calculation: exponential decay

A sensor response follows N(t)=N₀e^(−0.20t), with t in seconds. At t=5.0 s, N/N₀=e^(−1)=0.3679. Thus 36.8% of the initial response remains. The time constant is τ=1/0.20=5.0 s; after one time constant, the response is 1/e of its initial value.

10. Laboratory and computational application

For a pendulum experiment, measure length L and period T for several trials. The model T=2π√(L/g) gives T²=(4π²/g)L. Plot T² against L; the gradient m=4π²/g, hence g=4π²/m. Use repeated timings, uncertainty in length and period, a best-fit line and residual inspection. The small-angle approximation sinθ≈θ (θ in radians) is essential.

Expanded concept-by-concept explanations and worked examples

Every concept below is followed immediately by a worked numerical example. Calculations use SI units and state the model assumptions.

1. Dimensions and dimensional homogeneity

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For T=2π√(L/g), [L/g]=L/(LT⁻²)=T²; the square root has dimension T. The period expression is dimensionally consistent. Dimensional consistency is necessary, but does not prove an equation correct.

2. Cartesian vectors and components

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A 50 N force acts 30° above +x. Fₓ=50cos30°=43.3 N; Fᵧ=50sin30°=25.0 N. Recombining gives √(43.3²+25.0²)=50.0 N.

3. Dot product and work

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A 12 N force moves a body 5.0 m at 60° to displacement. W=Fs cosθ=12×5×0.5=30 J. The perpendicular force component does no work.

4. Cross product and torque

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A 20 N force acts perpendicular to a 0.30 m spanner. τ=rF=6.0 N m. At 30° to the spanner, τ=0.30×20×sin30°=3.0 N m.

5. Differentiation: velocity and acceleration

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For x=2t³−5t²+4t m, v=6t²−10t+4 and a=12t−10. At t=2 s, v=8 m s⁻¹ and a=14 m s⁻².

6. Integration: displacement and work

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

If v=3t² m s⁻¹, displacement from 0 to 2 s is ∫₀²3t²dt=[t³]₀²=8.0 m.

7. Partial derivatives and gradients

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For T=4x²+3y, ∇T=8xi+3j. At x=2, ∇T=16i+3j; this vector points toward the steepest temperature increase.

8. First-order differential equations

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

dN/dt=−0.15N gives N=N₀e^(−0.15t). At 4 s, N/N₀=e^(−0.6)=0.549. Half-life is ln2/0.15=4.62 s.

9. Complex numbers and Euler representation

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

(3+4i)(2−i)=10+5i. Its modulus is √125=11.18 and argument tan⁻¹(0.5)=26.6°.

10. Uncertainty propagation

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For A=lw, l=2.00±0.01 m and w=1.00±0.02 m, A=2.00 m². Relative uncertainty=√[(0.01/2)²+(0.02/1)²]=0.0206, so A=2.00±0.04 m².

Textbook expansion: mathematical foundations for physical reasoning

How to use this chapter. Mathematical methods are not separate from Physics: they are the language used to express measurable relationships, predict motion, quantify fields and compare theory with experiment. For every model, identify the physical system, define coordinates and sign conventions, state assumptions, choose a mathematical representation, solve it, interpret the result and test whether the result is physically plausible. Keep units attached to numerical quantities throughout the calculation.

11. Coordinate systems, position vectors and changes of reference frame

A coordinate system assigns numbers to the location of an event or object. In a Cartesian frame, a point in three-dimensional space is represented by r = xi + yj + zk, where the unit vectors identify mutually perpendicular axes. The coordinate values depend on the chosen origin and orientation, but the physical separation between two points does not. If A and B have position vectors rA and rB, the displacement from A to B is Δr = rB − rA. Its magnitude is the straight-line distance, while its direction describes the orientation of the change.

Changing the origin translates all position vectors by the same constant vector. Rotating the axes changes their components but not scalar quantities such as distance, speed or the dot product of two geometrical vectors. This distinction is essential: components are coordinate descriptions; vectors represent geometrical or physical quantities. In a rotating or accelerating frame, additional inertial terms may be required in the equations of motion, so a frame should be stated explicitly.

Worked example 11. A laboratory sensor is at A = (1, 2, 0) m and a source is at B = (4, 6, 3) m. The displacement A→B is (3, 4, 3) m. Its magnitude is √(3²+4²+3²) = √34 = 5.83 m. The unit vector from A to B is (3i+4j+3k)/√34. The answer is a direction as well as a distance; reporting 5.83 m alone loses directional information.

12. Vector algebra, projections and physical components

Vector addition follows the parallelogram rule and is independent of the order of addition. Scalar multiplication changes magnitude and may reverse direction if the scalar is negative. The component of vector A along a unit direction n is A·n. The corresponding projection vector is (A·n)n. This operation is useful when resolving a force along an inclined plane, extracting the component of velocity along a track, or determining the part of a field measured by a directional sensor.

For A = Axi + Ayj + Azk and B defined similarly, A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz. Geometrically it is AB cosθ. A positive dot product indicates an angle below 90°, a zero dot product indicates perpendicular vectors, and a negative dot product indicates an obtuse angle. Do not confuse the dot product with multiplication of magnitudes: direction matters.

Worked example 12. A force F = (6i + 8j) N acts through a displacement s = (3i − 2j) m. The work is W = F·s = 6(3)+8(−2) = 2 J. Although the force magnitude is 10 N and the displacement magnitude is √13 m, their product is not the work because the vectors are not parallel. The dot product correctly accounts for the angle.

13. Cross products, orientation and torque

The cross product A×B is a vector perpendicular to the plane containing A and B. Its magnitude is AB sinθ and its direction follows the right-hand rule. In Cartesian components, A×B = (AyBz−AzBy)i + (AzBx−AxBz)j + (AxBy−AyBx)k. The operation is anti-commutative: A×B = −B×A. It appears in torque τ = r×F, angular momentum L = r×p and magnetic force F = qv×B.

The lever arm is the perpendicular distance between the pivot and the line of action of the force. Therefore τ = rF sinθ = Fd⊥. A force directed through the pivot produces no torque, even if its magnitude is large. A force perpendicular to the radius vector produces maximum torque for a fixed r and F.

Worked example 13. A 25 N force is applied to a spanner 0.20 m from the pivot at 60° to the handle. The torque magnitude is 0.20×25×sin60° = 4.33 N m. If the force were parallel to the handle, the torque would be zero. The direction of rotation is determined by the right-hand rule and the chosen sign convention.

14. Differentiation from first principles and local linear behaviour

The derivative is the limiting rate of change of a function. For y=f(x), f′(x)=limh→0[f(x+h)−f(x)]/h, provided the limit exists. The derivative is the slope of the tangent to the graph and the best local linear approximation: f(x+Δx)≈f(x)+f′(x)Δx for sufficiently small Δx. In mechanics, differentiating position with respect to time gives velocity; differentiating velocity gives acceleration. In thermodynamics and electromagnetism, derivatives describe response coefficients and spatial or temporal variation.

For a power law y=Cxⁿ, differentiation gives dy/dx=nCxⁿ⁻¹. The product rule, quotient rule and chain rule are not merely algebraic shortcuts; they encode how combined dependencies change. If y=f(g(x)), then dy/dx=(df/dg)(dg/dx). Always identify the independent variable. A derivative with respect to time has different dimensions from a derivative with respect to distance.

Worked example 14. Let x(t)=4t³−3t²+2 m, with t in seconds. Then v=dx/dt=12t²−6t m s⁻¹ and a=dv/dt=24t−6 m s⁻². At t=2 s, v=36 m s⁻¹ and a=42 m s⁻². The constant position offset 2 m disappears under differentiation because it does not change with time.

15. Second derivatives, curvature and extrema

The second derivative measures the rate of change of the first derivative. For a position coordinate x(t), d²x/dt² is acceleration. For a graph y(x), the sign of y″ indicates local concavity: positive curvature corresponds to a locally convex graph and negative curvature to a locally concave graph. A stationary point satisfies y′=0. If y″ is positive there, the point is a local minimum; if y″ is negative, it is a local maximum. If y″=0, the test is inconclusive and higher derivatives or a sign analysis are needed.

In physical optimisation, extrema must be interpreted within constraints. A stationary value may be unstable, inaccessible or outside the domain of the model. For example, a calculated minimum energy is meaningful only if the system can occupy the corresponding configuration and the assumed potential remains valid there.

Worked example 15. A particle has potential energy U(x)=2x²−8x+11 J, where x is in metres. dU/dx=4x−8; setting this to zero gives x=2 m. Since d²U/dx²=4 J m⁻² is positive, the point is a local minimum. U(2)=3 J. The equilibrium is stable for small one-dimensional displacements because the potential curves upward around the minimum.

16. Definite integrals, signed area and accumulation

A definite integral ∫abf(x)dx is the limit of a sum of contributions f(xi)Δx. It represents signed area for a plotted function and accumulated physical quantity when f is a density or rate. If v(t) is velocity, integrating it over time gives displacement, not necessarily total distance. If power P(t) is integrated over time, the result is energy transferred. If force F(x) is integrated along a path, the result is work for a one-dimensional conservative force component.

The fundamental theorem of calculus links differentiation and integration: if F′(x)=f(x), then ∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a). The limits and variable carry units. A common error is to integrate a rate but omit the differential, or to confuse signed area with the sum of absolute areas.

Worked example 16. A particle moves with v(t)=3t²−2t m s⁻¹ from t=0 to 2 s. Its displacement is ∫₀²(3t²−2t)dt=[t³−t²]₀²=4 m. Since velocity changes sign at t=2/3 s, total distance is larger: integrate |v| over the two intervals rather than using the signed displacement alone.

17. Multivariable functions and partial derivatives

Many physical quantities depend on more than one independent variable. Temperature may be T(x,y,z,t), pressure may depend on volume and temperature, and electric potential may vary throughout space. A partial derivative measures the rate of change with respect to one variable while holding the others fixed. For example, (∂U/∂V)T describes how internal energy changes with volume at fixed temperature. The subscript is important because different experimental constraints can produce different derivatives.

The total differential of f(x,y) is df=(∂f/∂x)dx+(∂f/∂y)dy for small changes. This is the basis of first-order uncertainty propagation and local sensitivity analysis. The gradient ∇f is a vector of partial derivatives; it points in the direction of greatest increase of a scalar field, and its magnitude gives the maximum directional rate of change.

Worked example 17. Let T(x,y)=300+2x−3y K, where x and y are in metres. Then ∂T/∂x=2 K m⁻¹ and ∂T/∂y=−3 K m⁻¹. A small displacement (dx,dy)=(0.10,0.20) m gives dT≈2(0.10)−3(0.20)=−0.40 K. The negative sign means the temperature decreases for this displacement.

18. Ordinary differential equations: modelling a physical process

A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives. It is a compact statement of a physical law. The equation must be accompanied by initial or boundary conditions to select a particular solution. For a first-order linear equation dy/dt+ay=b, where a and b are constants, the integrating-factor method uses μ(t)=eat. Multiplication gives d(μy)/dt=bμ, which can be integrated directly. If b=0, the solution is exponential decay or growth depending on the sign of a.

For a cooling body in an environment at fixed temperature Te, Newton's law of cooling gives dT/dt=−k(T−Te). Separating variables and integrating yields T−Te=(T₀−Te)e−kt. The model assumes a constant environment, a uniform body temperature and a heat-transfer rate proportional to the temperature difference. Real experiments may depart from these assumptions.

Worked example 18. A sensor initially 80°C is placed in a 20°C room. Its temperature difference follows ΔT=60e−0.10t, with t in minutes. After 10 min, T=20+60e⁻¹=42.1°C. The time constant is 1/k=10 min; after one time constant, the temperature difference has fallen to about 36.8% of its initial value.

19. Complex numbers, phasors and harmonic signals

A complex number z=a+ib has real part a and imaginary part b, where i²=−1. Its modulus is |z|=√(a²+b²), and its argument is the angle it makes with the positive real axis. Euler's identity eiθ=cosθ+i sinθ gives a compact representation of sinusoidal behaviour. A harmonic signal can be represented as the real part of a complex exponential, for example x(t)=Re[X₀eiωt]. This notation simplifies differentiation because d(eiωt)/dt=iωeiωt.

Phasors encode amplitude and phase. They are particularly useful for sinusoidal steady-state circuits and wave superposition. The physical measured signal is real; the complex representation is a mathematical tool. When taking a final observable, select the real part or magnitude as required by the model. Do not interpret the imaginary component as a second physical displacement.

Worked example 19. Write x(t)=5cos(20t+π/6) mm as a complex signal. One representation is x(t)=Re[5eiπ/6ei20t] mm. Differentiating gives v(t)=Re[i20·5eiπ/6ei20t] mm s⁻¹, equivalent to a sinusoid of amplitude 100 mm s⁻¹ leading the displacement by π/2.

20. Fourier ideas and why physical signals are decomposed

Fourier analysis represents a suitable periodic signal as a sum of sinusoidal components. A complicated waveform can therefore be studied in terms of its frequencies, amplitudes and phases. The fundamental frequency sets the repetition rate; harmonics occur at integer multiples of that frequency. The method underpins sound analysis, vibration diagnostics, optical spectroscopy, signal processing and the solution of linear differential equations with periodic forcing.

For a periodic function with period T, the angular fundamental frequency is ω₀=2π/T. The Fourier series contains a constant term and sine and cosine terms at nω₀. The coefficients quantify the contribution of each harmonic. A sharp discontinuity requires many harmonics and can produce Gibbs overshoot near the discontinuity. Experimental sampling must also be fast enough to avoid aliasing; the sampling frequency should exceed twice the highest frequency of interest in the ideal Nyquist condition, with practical systems allowing a safety margin and anti-alias filtering.

Worked example 20. A vibration repeats every 0.040 s. Its fundamental frequency is f=1/T=25 Hz and angular frequency is ω=2πf=157.1 rad s⁻¹. If a measured spectrum also contains a strong component at 50 Hz, it may be the second harmonic. Confirm this interpretation by checking whether the 50 Hz component remains phase-locked to the 25 Hz fundamental.

21. Dimensional analysis, scaling and model testing

Dimensional analysis tests whether an equation is physically homogeneous and can reveal how variables combine. Suppose a pendulum period T depends only on length L and gravitational acceleration g. Assume T∝Lᵃgᵇ. Since [T]=T, [L]=L and [g]=LT⁻², dimensional balance gives T=La+bT−2b. Thus −2b=1 and a+b=0, giving b=−1/2 and a=1/2. Therefore T∝√(L/g). Dimensional analysis cannot determine the dimensionless constant 2π; that requires solving the equation of motion under the small-angle approximation.

Scaling arguments help identify dominant effects. A dimensionless ratio such as Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous effects; a small or large value indicates which contribution is likely to dominate. Such reasoning is useful before attempting a detailed calculation, but it does not replace the assumptions and boundary conditions of a complete model.

Worked example 21. For a pendulum of length 0.90 m in a region where g=9.81 m s⁻², the dimensional estimate √(L/g)=0.303 s. The small-angle period is T≈2π√(L/g)=1.90 s. The factor 2π is dimensionless and cannot be obtained from dimensional analysis alone.

22. Numerical methods: roots, integration and differential equations

Many physical equations cannot be solved exactly. Numerical methods approximate a solution and must be accompanied by an error estimate or convergence check. The bisection method brackets a root between values of opposite sign and repeatedly halves the interval. Newton–Raphson iteration uses xn+1=xn−f(xn)/f′(xn), which can converge rapidly near a suitable root but may fail if the derivative is small or the initial guess is poor.

Numerical integration approximates an area using rectangles, trapezia or higher-order quadrature. For a measured data set, the trapezoidal rule is often appropriate: ∫f(x)dx≈Σ[(fᵢ+fᵢ₊₁)/2]Δx. The Euler method advances a differential equation y′=F(t,y) by yn+1=yn+hF(tn,yn). Reducing step size should improve accuracy for a well-behaved problem, but rounding error and model error remain.

Worked example 22. Estimate the area under f(x)=x² from 0 to 2 using one trapezium. The estimate is (2−0)[f(0)+f(2)]/2=4. The exact integral is 8/3=2.667, so the absolute error is 1.333. Dividing the interval into more subintervals reduces the trapezoidal error for this smooth convex function.

23. Measurement uncertainty and significant figures

A measured value is incomplete without an uncertainty and unit. Random uncertainty describes scatter among repeated measurements; systematic uncertainty shifts measurements in a consistent direction. Repetition reduces the standard error of the mean but does not automatically remove calibration error, zero offset, parallax or environmental bias. Record instrument resolution, calibration status, repeat count and the method used to estimate uncertainty.

For independent variables x and y and a derived quantity f(x,y), first-order propagation gives σf²≈(∂f/∂x)²σx²+(∂f/∂y)²σy². Correlated variables require covariance terms. For multiplication and powers, fractional uncertainties are often convenient: if q=xᵃyᵇ, then (σq/q)²≈a²(σx/x)²+b²(σy/y)² for independent inputs.

Worked example 23. A rectangle has measured sides 2.00±0.02 m and 3.00±0.03 m. Its area is 6.00 m². The fractional uncertainty is √[(0.02/2.00)²+(0.03/3.00)²]=0.0141, so σA≈0.085 m². A sensible report is A=(6.00±0.09) m², with the uncertainty rounded to one or two significant figures and the value rounded to the same decimal place.

24. Graphs, linearisation and extraction of physical constants

A graph is an analytical tool, not decoration. Plot the dependent variable vertically against the independent variable horizontally unless there is a reason to choose otherwise. Include variable names, units, uncertainty bars where available and a scale that uses the plotting area well. A fitted gradient and intercept should be interpreted through the theoretical model. Linearisation can reveal a parameter: if y=Ae−kt, plotting ln y against t gives gradient −k, provided the measured quantity is positive and the model assumptions hold.

Do not force a straight line through data that show curvature. Inspect residuals, compare fit quality, and distinguish a statistically good fit from a physically valid model. The intercept may represent a background signal, initial condition or instrument offset. A non-zero intercept should not be discarded without justification.

Worked example 24. A plot of ln(I/I₀) against time gives gradient −0.045 s⁻¹. For exponential attenuation I=I₀e−μt, the attenuation coefficient is μ=0.045 s⁻¹. If the slope uncertainty is ±0.003 s⁻¹, report μ=(0.045±0.003) s⁻¹, subject to the validity of the exponential model and the time calibration.

25. Common mathematical modelling errors and how to prevent them

  • Undefined symbols: define each symbol, its unit and sign convention before substitution.
  • Inconsistent units: convert to SI units before combining quantities, unless a clearly stated alternative system is used.
  • Missing initial conditions: a differential equation's general solution is not a unique physical prediction until conditions are applied.
  • Sign errors: draw axes and indicate positive directions before writing vector equations.
  • Over-precision: calculator digits do not imply measurement accuracy.
  • Unstated assumptions: identify idealisations such as negligible friction, constant acceleration, small angle or uniform field.
  • Uninterpreted answers: state what the sign, magnitude and units mean for the physical system.

26. Extended laboratory: determining gravitational acceleration with a pendulum

Objective: estimate local gravitational acceleration by measuring the period of a simple pendulum at several lengths. Apparatus: light inextensible string, dense bob, clamp stand, metre rule, stopwatch or photogate, balance and protractor. Model: for small angular displacement θ, sinθ≈θ and the restoring torque leads to d²θ/dt²+(g/L)θ=0. The angular frequency is ω=√(g/L), so T=2π√(L/g) and T²=(4π²/g)L.

Method: measure the distance from pivot to the bob's centre; use a small initial angle, preferably below about 10°; release without pushing; time 20 complete oscillations; repeat at least three times for each length; and repeat for several lengths. Calculate the mean period T from total time divided by the number of oscillations. Plot T² against L. The gradient m should be 4π²/g, so g=4π²/m.

Data analysis: include uncertainty in length and timing, use the spread of repeated trials to estimate random uncertainty, and determine the uncertainty in the fitted gradient. Propagate the gradient uncertainty using σg/g≈σm/m. Discuss reaction time, pivot friction, finite bob size, string stretch, air resistance and departures from the small-angle approximation. A photogate reduces timing uncertainty but does not correct a length measurement from the wrong reference point.

Safety: secure the clamp stand, keep the swing path clear, use a compact bob and prevent the bob from striking people or equipment. Do not stand beneath a suspended mass while adjusting the clamp.

27. Extended laboratory: testing Hooke's law and uncertainty in a gradient

Objective: determine the spring constant k from the relation F=kx within the elastic limit. Suspend known masses, measure extension from the unloaded reference length, add loads gradually and repeat readings while avoiding parallax. Plot force F against extension x. The gradient is k in N m⁻¹. If the graph curves, the spring may have left its linear elastic range, the zero may have shifted or the measurement may be faulty.

Use a suitable range of loads and do not exceed the spring's safe working limit. Allow oscillations to settle before reading. Repeat each measurement and consider unloading data to check for hysteresis. The intercept should be consistent with the chosen zero within uncertainty. A spring with a non-zero intercept may indicate pre-load or a systematic length offset.

Worked example 27. A best-fit line through force-extension data has gradient 18.4 N m⁻¹ with standard uncertainty 0.6 N m⁻¹. The spring constant is k=(18.4±0.6) N m⁻¹. A 0.250 kg mass produces an ideal extension x=mg/k=(0.250×9.81)/18.4=0.133 m. The uncertainty in k alone contributes a relative extension uncertainty of approximately 0.6/18.4=3.3%; mass and g uncertainties may also be included if significant.

28. Further worked synthesis: choosing the right mathematical tool

Consider a particle whose position is r(t)=(2t²)i+(3t)j+(t³)k m. Differentiate each component to obtain v(t)=4t i+3j+3t²k m s⁻¹ and a(t)=4i+6tk m s⁻². At t=1 s, v=(4,3,3) m s⁻¹, with speed √34=5.83 m s⁻¹. The acceleration is (4,0,6) m s⁻². The scalar speed is the magnitude of velocity; it is not generally equal to the rate of change of a single coordinate.

At t=1 s, the instantaneous rate of change of speed is (v·a)/|v|=(4×4+3×0+3×6)/√34=34/√34=5.83 m s⁻². The component of acceleration perpendicular to velocity changes the direction of motion, while the parallel component changes the speed. This decomposition is a useful bridge to curvilinear motion and classical mechanics.

29. Concept checks for self-assessment

  1. Why is displacement a vector while distance is a scalar?

    Displacement describes the directed change in position; distance is the non-negative path length.

  2. Can a particle have zero velocity but non-zero acceleration?

    Yes. At a turning point in one-dimensional motion, instantaneous velocity can be zero while acceleration remains non-zero.

  3. What does a negative gradient on a position-time graph mean?

    The velocity component along the chosen positive axis is negative.

  4. Why does dimensional agreement not prove an equation is correct?

    Many incorrect equations are dimensionally homogeneous; dimensional analysis cannot usually determine dimensionless constants or the full functional form.

  5. When is a complex representation useful?

    For linear sinusoidal systems, because differentiation, phase shifts and superposition become algebraically simpler.

  6. Why must a numerical solution be checked for convergence?

    A computed value can depend on step size or iteration tolerance; stability under refinement provides evidence that discretisation error is controlled.

30. Connections to the rest of the undergraduate Physics curriculum

Vector calculus is used in classical mechanics to express force, momentum and angular momentum; in electromagnetism it defines divergence, curl and field flux; in fluid mechanics it describes velocity and pressure fields. Differential equations describe oscillators, heat flow, electrical circuits, radioactive decay and wave propagation. Complex numbers simplify AC circuit analysis and quantum amplitudes. Integration connects local quantities to global observables such as work, charge, mass, probability and energy. Numerical methods support systems where exact analytic solutions are unavailable. The methods in this unit should therefore be revisited as each later unit introduces its own physical variables and boundary conditions.
