Core framework and conceptual foundations

Biochemistry links molecular structure to cellular function and clinical phenotype. Interpret metabolic pathways as regulated networks rather than isolated reaction lists. For each pathway identify its cellular location, substrate and product, irreversible or rate-limiting steps, energy requirements, cofactors, hormonal regulation and tissue distribution. Enzyme activity depends on substrate availability, enzyme concentration, allosteric effectors, covalent modification, pH, temperature and compartmentalisation. A measured enzyme concentration in plasma may reflect tissue injury or altered synthesis and clearance; it is not automatically a direct measure of pathway flux.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and converts glucose to pyruvate with net production of ATP and NADH; under anaerobic conditions, lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD+ to sustain glycolysis. Pyruvate can enter mitochondria and be converted to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, linking glycolysis to the citric acid cycle. The electron transport chain establishes a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, which drives ATP synthase. Fatty acid beta-oxidation generates acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH2; hepatic ketogenesis becomes important when carbohydrate availability and insulin signalling are low relative to counter-regulatory hormones. Amino acid nitrogen is transferred and ultimately disposed largely through the urea cycle in the liver. DNA replication is semiconservative; DNA polymerases synthesise in the 5' to 3' direction, requiring primers. Transcription produces RNA, and translation reads mRNA codons at ribosomes. Variants may alter protein sequence, splicing, expression or dosage; genotype-phenotype relationships are modified by penetrance, expressivity, environment and epigenetic regulation.

Integrated clinical case

A patient with fasting hypoglycaemia and hepatomegaly is being evaluated for a possible inherited glycogen disorder. Explain the roles of hepatic glycogen synthesis and breakdown, the distinction between fasting and fed-state hormonal signals, and why a pathway defect can produce both low blood glucose and enlarged liver.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.

Case discussion

A medical student is preparing a case-based learning presentation involving a person whose symptoms and investigation findings may relate to concepts in biochemistry, genetics and molecular medicine.
