Core framework and conceptual foundations

Biochemistry links molecular structure to cellular function and clinical phenotype. Interpret metabolic pathways as regulated networks rather than isolated reaction lists. For each pathway identify its cellular location, substrate and product, irreversible or rate-limiting steps, energy requirements, cofactors, hormonal regulation and tissue distribution. Enzyme activity depends on substrate availability, enzyme concentration, allosteric effectors, covalent modification, pH, temperature and compartmentalisation. A measured enzyme concentration in plasma may reflect tissue injury or altered synthesis and clearance; it is not automatically a direct measure of pathway flux.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and converts glucose to pyruvate with net production of ATP and NADH; under anaerobic conditions, lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD+ to sustain glycolysis. Pyruvate can enter mitochondria and be converted to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, linking glycolysis to the citric acid cycle. The electron transport chain establishes a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, which drives ATP synthase. Fatty acid beta-oxidation generates acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH2; hepatic ketogenesis becomes important when carbohydrate availability and insulin signalling are low relative to counter-regulatory hormones. Amino acid nitrogen is transferred and ultimately disposed largely through the urea cycle in the liver. DNA replication is semiconservative; DNA polymerases synthesise in the 5' to 3' direction, requiring primers. Transcription produces RNA, and translation reads mRNA codons at ribosomes. Variants may alter protein sequence, splicing, expression or dosage; genotype-phenotype relationships are modified by penetrance, expressivity, environment and epigenetic regulation.

Integrated clinical case

A patient with fasting hypoglycaemia and hepatomegaly is being evaluated for a possible inherited glycogen disorder. Explain the roles of hepatic glycogen synthesis and breakdown, the distinction between fasting and fed-state hormonal signals, and why a pathway defect can produce both low blood glucose and enlarged liver.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.

Medical Biochemistry and Molecular Biology: Integrated Clinical Masterclass

Learning purpose. This masterclass connects molecular structure to cellular function, metabolic regulation, laboratory measurements and disease. The central question in clinical biochemistry is not simply whether a value is high or low, but what biological process could produce the pattern, how the result fits the patient, and what should be checked next. Interpret all laboratory results using the reporting laboratory's reference intervals, units, analytical method and clinical context.

1. From molecular structure to biological function

Biological molecules are built from atoms joined by covalent bonds and organised by non-covalent interactions. Covalent bonds provide molecular frameworks; hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces help determine folding, binding and assembly. Water is a polar solvent and participates directly in hydrolysis, condensation and acid-base reactions. The hydrophobic effect helps drive non-polar groups away from water and is important in protein folding and membrane formation.

Carbohydrates contain carbonyl and hydroxyl groups and commonly serve as fuels, storage molecules and recognition structures. Monosaccharides can form glycosidic bonds; the position and stereochemistry of these bonds determine whether enzymes can digest a polymer. Starch and glycogen contain alpha-linked glucose, whereas human digestive enzymes cannot hydrolyse the beta-1,4 linkages of cellulose. Glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces contribute to cell recognition, immune interactions and tissue organisation.

Lipids are chemically diverse. Triacylglycerols store concentrated metabolic energy; phospholipids and cholesterol contribute to membrane structure; steroid molecules act as hormones; and eicosanoids function as local signalling mediators. The amphipathic character of phospholipids—hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails—allows bilayers to form spontaneously in aqueous environments. Membrane fluidity depends on fatty-acid chain length, degree of unsaturation, temperature and cholesterol content.

Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Primary structure is the amino-acid sequence; secondary structure includes alpha helices and beta sheets; tertiary structure describes the three-dimensional fold of one polypeptide; and quaternary structure describes assembly of multiple subunits. Folding is stabilised by hydrophobic packing, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions and disulphide bonds. A substitution can alter charge, size or polarity and may change folding, stability, binding or catalytic activity. Not every sequence variant causes disease: its effect depends on location, molecular mechanism, inheritance and biological context.

2. Enzymes, kinetics and regulation

Enzymes lower activation energy without changing the overall free-energy difference or equilibrium position of a reaction. The active site binds substrate and stabilises the transition state. Enzyme activity can be influenced by substrate concentration, enzyme abundance, pH, temperature, cofactors, inhibitors, covalent modification and cellular compartmentalisation. Many enzymes require metal ions or organic coenzymes; deficiencies of a vitamin-derived cofactor can therefore impair several pathways at once.

For a simple Michaelis–Menten enzyme, the initial velocity is described by v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]). Vmax is the limiting velocity at saturating substrate under the specified assay conditions. Km is the substrate concentration at which velocity is half Vmax in this model; it is not universally identical to a binding dissociation constant. Competitive inhibition increases the apparent Km while leaving Vmax unchanged in the ideal model. Pure non-competitive inhibition decreases Vmax without changing Km; mixed inhibition can alter both. Real enzymes may show allostery, cooperativity or multi-substrate behaviour that does not fit this simple model.

Clinical reasoning: an enzyme result in plasma may indicate tissue injury, altered synthesis, impaired clearance or induction. Interpret the specific enzyme, pattern, timing and clinical presentation. A raised enzyme concentration does not automatically identify the organ or prove a diagnosis.

3. Bioenergetics and mitochondrial ATP production

Cells couple energetically unfavourable reactions to favourable processes, commonly ATP hydrolysis. ATP is continuously regenerated rather than stored in large quantities. In glycolysis, glucose is converted in the cytosol to pyruvate, producing a net two ATP and two NADH per glucose. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate can enter mitochondria and be converted by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex to acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle, where oxidation generates NADH, FADH2 and GTP/ATP equivalents. Reduced coenzymes donate electrons to the respiratory chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electron transfer supports proton pumping, creating an electrochemical gradient used by ATP synthase to phosphorylate ADP.

Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the respiratory chain and is reduced to water. When oxygen delivery or mitochondrial oxidation is inadequate, oxidative phosphorylation becomes limited and cells rely more heavily on glycolytic ATP production, with lactate formation helping regenerate NAD+ for continued glycolysis. Lactate may also rise with adrenergic stimulation, impaired hepatic clearance, seizures, certain drugs or other metabolic stresses; it should be interpreted clinically rather than treated as a specific marker of tissue hypoxia in isolation.

4. Carbohydrate metabolism and glucose homeostasis

Glycolysis supplies ATP and intermediates for biosynthesis. Its major regulatory steps include hexokinase or glucokinase, phosphofructokinase-1 and pyruvate kinase. Phosphofructokinase-1 responds to cellular energy status and allosteric signals, including ATP, AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. Glucokinase in liver and pancreatic beta cells has a role in glucose sensing and handling. Insulin generally promotes glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues, glycogen synthesis, glycolysis and lipogenesis; glucagon supports hepatic glucose output during fasting.

Glycogenesis stores glucose as glycogen, particularly in liver and skeletal muscle. Hepatic glycogen helps maintain blood glucose between meals; muscle glycogen is mainly used locally during contraction. Glycogenolysis releases glucose-1-phosphate, which is converted to glucose-6-phosphate. Liver has glucose-6-phosphatase and can release free glucose; skeletal muscle lacks this enzyme and uses glucose-6-phosphate in its own metabolism. Gluconeogenesis synthesises glucose mainly in liver and, during prolonged fasting, also in kidney. It uses lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids as substrates.

Diabetes mellitus is a group of disorders characterised by chronic hyperglycaemia due to inadequate insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or both. Diagnostic interpretation uses accepted criteria and appropriate repeat confirmation when indicated. HbA1c reflects glycation over the lifespan of circulating erythrocytes and can be misleading when red-cell turnover, haemoglobin variants, pregnancy, renal disease or recent blood loss alters that lifespan. A glucose value and HbA1c should be interpreted alongside symptoms, medication, fasting status and the reason for testing.

5. Lipid metabolism, transport and ketone bodies

Dietary lipids are emulsified by bile salts and hydrolysed by pancreatic enzymes. Enterocytes re-esterify absorbed fatty acids and package triacylglycerol into chylomicrons, which enter lymph and then blood. Lipoprotein lipase releases fatty acids for tissue uptake. Chylomicron remnants are taken up by the liver. The liver exports triacylglycerol in very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL); progressive lipolysis contributes to intermediate-density and low-density lipoprotein particles. LDL delivers cholesterol to tissues through LDL receptors. High-density lipoproteins participate in cholesterol transport and exchange; HDL concentration alone does not describe the entire function of reverse cholesterol transport.

During fasting, adipose tissue lipolysis releases non-esterified fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids enter mitochondria and undergo beta-oxidation, producing acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH2. In the liver, excess acetyl-CoA can be converted into ketone bodies—acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone—which provide an alternative fuel for extrahepatic tissues. In diabetic ketoacidosis, insulin deficiency and counter-regulatory hormone excess promote lipolysis and ketogenesis, producing ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis. The clinical assessment includes hydration, electrolytes, glucose, ketones, acid-base status and precipitating illness; management requires urgent professional protocols.

6. Amino acids, nitrogen balance and the urea cycle

Amino acids are used to synthesise proteins, neurotransmitters, hormones, nucleotides and other molecules. When amino acids are catabolised, their nitrogen must be handled safely. Transamination transfers amino groups, commonly using pyridoxal phosphate derived from vitamin B6. Oxidative deamination releases ammonia, which is toxic at elevated concentrations, particularly to the central nervous system. The liver converts nitrogen into urea through the urea cycle; urea is transported in blood and excreted by the kidneys.

Essential amino acids cannot be synthesised in sufficient amounts by humans and must be obtained from the diet. Positive nitrogen balance occurs when nitrogen intake exceeds loss, as during growth or tissue repair; negative balance may occur in severe illness, trauma, inadequate nutrition or catabolic states. Plasma ammonia is sensitive to collection and handling conditions; unexpected results should be checked against sampling quality and clinical findings. Hyperammonaemia warrants urgent assessment.

7. DNA, RNA, gene expression and molecular control

DNA stores hereditary information in a double helix with antiparallel strands. Complementary base pairing—A with T and G with C—supports accurate replication and transcription. DNA replication is semi-conservative: each daughter molecule contains one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. DNA polymerases extend a primer in the 5′ to 3′ direction, while helicases, primases, ligases and repair systems coordinate copying and genome maintenance. Replication errors that escape proofreading and repair may become permanent mutations.

Transcription uses RNA polymerase to synthesise RNA from a DNA template. In eukaryotes, pre-mRNA is processed through 5′ capping, splicing and polyadenylation before export. Alternative splicing allows one gene to generate different transcript isoforms. Translation occurs on ribosomes, where codons in mRNA specify amino acids delivered by transfer RNAs. Initiation, elongation and termination are regulated processes; errors can produce absent, shortened, misfolded or dysfunctional proteins.

Gene expression is controlled at multiple levels: chromatin accessibility, transcription factors, RNA processing and stability, translation, protein modification and degradation. DNA methylation and histone modifications can alter transcriptional accessibility without changing the underlying DNA sequence. Cell signalling pathways connect extracellular information to transcriptional responses. Molecular diagnostics may identify variants, gene fusions, copy-number changes or pathogen nucleic acids, but a detected variant must be interpreted using validated classification frameworks, phenotype, inheritance and test limitations.

8. Vitamins, minerals and biochemical cofactors

Many vitamins function as coenzyme precursors. Thiamine (B1) supports oxidative decarboxylation and transketolase reactions; riboflavin (B2) contributes to FAD and FMN; niacin (B3) contributes to NAD and NADP; pantothenate (B5) is part of coenzyme A; pyridoxine (B6) supports amino-acid metabolism; biotin supports carboxylases; folate and vitamin B12 are central to one-carbon transfer and DNA synthesis. Vitamin C supports collagen hydroxylation and acts as an antioxidant; vitamin D regulates calcium and phosphate homeostasis; vitamins A, E and K have roles in vision/gene regulation, antioxidant protection and coagulation-protein modification respectively.

Iron is essential for haemoglobin and many enzymes; iodine is required for thyroid hormone synthesis; zinc supports numerous enzymes and transcription factors; selenium is incorporated into selenoproteins; calcium functions in bone, muscle contraction, secretion and intracellular signalling; magnesium is required for many ATP-dependent reactions. Deficiency and toxicity both matter. Nutritional assessment should consider diet, absorption, losses, physiological demand, medication and relevant laboratory tests rather than relying on symptoms alone.

Drugs and supplements may interfere with assays or alter physiology. A result should be checked for units, reference interval, analytical method, previous values and whether the specimen is suitable. Case A: Hyperglycaemia with ketonaemia

A young adult presents with vomiting, abdominal pain, polyuria and deep rapid breathing. Investigations show marked hyperglycaemia, raised blood ketones, low bicarbonate and acidemia. The educational point is to link hormonal regulation, lipid mobilisation, hepatic metabolism and acid-base physiology. Suspected diabetic ketoacidosis is an emergency and requires urgent guideline-based clinical care.

Case B: Jaundice and abnormal liver tests

A patient develops jaundice and dark urine. Bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase are elevated, with a smaller rise in aminotransferases. Explain how impaired bile flow can produce a cholestatic pattern, why conjugated bilirubin may appear in urine, and why the pattern alone cannot identify the anatomical cause. Case C: Raised lactate during acute illness

A patient with severe infection has tachycardia, hypotension and an elevated lactate. Explain the relationship between glycolytic flux, NAD+ regeneration and lactate production. Discuss why elevated lactate can reflect several processes—including altered perfusion, adrenergic drive and reduced clearance—and why trends and clinical reassessment are more informative than a single isolated measurement.

Case D: Hyperammonaemia and altered mental status

A patient with known liver disease becomes confused. Explain the role of hepatic urea-cycle function in ammonia disposal and why ammonia accumulation can affect cerebral function. Identify pre-analytical factors that may distort ammonia measurement and explain why the result must be interpreted alongside the neurological examination, liver function, medications and other causes of encephalopathy.

11. Worked quantitative examples

Example 1: Enzyme kinetics. An enzyme has Vmax of 120 μmol/min and Km of 3 mmol/L. At a substrate concentration of 3 mmol/L, the Michaelis–Menten model predicts v = 120 × 3/(3 + 3) = 60 μmol/min, which is half Vmax. At 9 mmol/L, v = 120 × 9/(3 + 9) = 90 μmol/min. This illustrates the diminishing increase in velocity as substrate concentration approaches saturation.

Example 2: Anion gap. If sodium is 140 mmol/L, chloride 104 mmol/L and bicarbonate 18 mmol/L, the anion gap using the formula Na − (Cl + HCO₃) is 140 − (104 + 18) = 18 mmol/L. Compare this with the local laboratory interval and consider albumin correction. This calculation is not a diagnosis by itself.

Example 3: Body mass and metabolic context. Body mass index is calculated as mass in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It is a population screening measure, not a direct measurement of body fat distribution or metabolic health. Interpret it alongside waist measurement, clinical history, body composition and relevant risk factors.

12. Laboratory quality, research literacy and responsible genomics

Analytical quality includes accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, calibration, internal quality control and external quality assessment. A highly sensitive test detects most people with the target condition but may produce false positives; a highly specific test produces fewer false positives but may miss some affected people. Positive and negative predictive values depend on prevalence and pre-test probability. Reference intervals describe a defined reference population and are not identical to clinical decision limits.

When reading a molecular or biochemical research paper, identify the study design, sample size, population, assay method, controls, effect size, uncertainty interval and limitations. Association does not establish causation. A statistically significant difference may not be clinically important. Genomic findings can have implications for relatives and future health; testing should use appropriate consent, privacy safeguards, counselling and locally applicable legal and professional standards.

15. Key equations and concepts to memorise

  • Michaelis–Menten: v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]).
  • ATP hydrolysis is energetically favourable under cellular conditions and can drive coupled reactions.
  • Glycolysis occurs in cytosol; the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation are mitochondrial processes in human cells.
  • Anion gap (common formula): Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), with local laboratory conventions and albumin considered.
  • DNA replication is semi-conservative; DNA polymerases synthesise in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
  • Transcription produces RNA; translation uses ribosomes to synthesise polypeptides from mRNA codons.
