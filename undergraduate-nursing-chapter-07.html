Introduction to pathophysiology

Pathophysiology explains functional changes associated with disease or injury. It connects normal anatomy and physiology with cellular, tissue and organ responses. A disease process may involve a cause or risk factor, a mechanism of injury, structural or functional changes, clinical manifestations, complications and outcomes.

Aetiology refers to the cause or origin of a condition; pathogenesis describes how it develops; signs are objectively observed; symptoms are experienced and reported. A diagnosis is made by an appropriately qualified practitioner using the whole clinical picture, not by matching one isolated symptom to a textbook description.

Cellular adaptation, injury and death

Cells adapt to stress through changes such as hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy and metaplasia. Adaptation may preserve function but can become harmful when the stimulus persists. Reversible cell injury may involve swelling and impaired function; severe or sustained injury can lead to cell death.

Necrosis is cell death associated with membrane disruption and inflammation. Apoptosis is regulated cell death that generally limits inflammation. Hypoxia, toxins, infection, immune injury, nutritional deficits and physical trauma can damage cells. The type and extent of injury influence tissue function and clinical manifestations.

Inflammation, healing and repair

Inflammation is a protective response to infection or tissue injury. Vascular changes and chemical mediators increase blood flow and permeability and recruit immune cells. Acute inflammation develops rapidly; chronic inflammation may persist and contribute to tissue damage and fibrosis.

Healing may occur through regeneration, where damaged cells are replaced by cells of the same type, or repair with connective tissue and scar formation. Outcomes depend on tissue regenerative capacity, blood supply, infection, nutrition, diabetes, smoking, medicines and mechanical stress. Nurses monitor wounds and systemic signs and follow approved wound-care guidance.

Haemodynamic disorders and shock

Oedema is excess fluid in interstitial spaces and can arise from increased hydrostatic pressure, reduced plasma oncotic pressure, increased capillary permeability or impaired lymphatic drainage. Thrombosis is formation of a clot within a vessel; embolism occurs when material travels and obstructs a vessel. Infarction is tissue necrosis due to interrupted blood supply.

Shock is a life-threatening state of inadequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery. Major categories include hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, distributive and obstructive shock. Early recognition relies on the whole clinical picture and trends, not a single measurement. Suspected shock requires immediate emergency escalation under local protocol.

Fluid, electrolyte and acid-base balance

Water distribution is influenced by intracellular and extracellular compartments, solute concentration, membrane permeability and regulatory hormones. Sodium is a major extracellular cation; potassium is a major intracellular cation. Calcium, magnesium, chloride, phosphate and bicarbonate contribute to neuromuscular, metabolic and acid-base function.

Fluid or electrolyte disorders may affect consciousness, cardiac rhythm, muscle function, renal function and circulation. Acid-base disturbances are commonly classified as metabolic or respiratory acidosis and alkalosis. Interpretation uses clinical context and laboratory results; replacement or restriction decisions require authorised orders and monitoring.

Endocrine and metabolic pathophysiology

Endocrine disorders arise from abnormal hormone production, secretion, transport, receptor response or feedback regulation. Diabetes mellitus involves chronic dysregulation of glucose metabolism; thyroid disorders alter metabolic regulation; adrenal and pituitary disorders can affect multiple organ systems.

Metabolic changes may influence hydration, energy, weight, temperature, cardiovascular function and wound healing. Nursing learning should connect symptoms with mechanisms and understand why monitoring plans are individualised. Do not independently alter insulin or other high-risk medicines outside authorised protocols.

Pharmacology foundations and medicine terminology

Pharmacology studies medicines and their effects. A medicine may be described by chemical, generic and brand names. Indications describe intended uses; contraindications identify circumstances where use may be unsafe; precautions identify situations requiring additional consideration; adverse drug reactions are harmful unintended responses at normal doses.

Medicines may be prescribed, supplied or administered under different legal frameworks depending on jurisdiction. Nurses must understand local scope, prescription requirements, controlled-medicine rules and institutional policies. A drug reference should be current, authoritative and appropriate to the country and patient population.

Pharmacokinetics: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a medicine. Absorption is movement from the administration site into systemic circulation. Distribution is movement between blood and tissues. Metabolism transforms medicines, often in the liver but also in other tissues. Excretion removes medicines or metabolites, commonly through the kidneys.

Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged. Half-life is the time required for the concentration to decrease by half under defined conditions. Age, organ function, blood flow, interactions, route and formulation can alter medicine exposure. These concepts explain why doses and intervals must be prescribed for the individual.

Pharmacodynamics and medicine response

Pharmacodynamics describes what a medicine does to the body. Medicines may act as receptor agonists, partial agonists, antagonists, enzyme inhibitors, channel modulators or through other mechanisms. Potency concerns the amount needed for an effect; efficacy concerns the maximum effect achievable in a defined system.

Dose-response relationships are influenced by receptor number, sensitivity, tolerance, disease state and interactions. Therapeutic effect must be balanced against adverse effects. A narrow therapeutic index means the margin between beneficial and harmful concentrations is relatively small and may require close monitoring.

Routes, formulations and medication safety

Routes include oral, buccal, sublingual, topical, inhaled, rectal, enteral-tube, subcutaneous, intramuscular and intravenous administration. The route affects absorption, onset, duration and risk. Formulations may be immediate-release, modified-release, liquid, suspension, solution or other preparations; they are not automatically interchangeable.

Safe administration includes confirming the patient, medicine, dose, route, time, indication, documentation and response, alongside allergies, relevant observations, interactions, expiry, formulation and required monitoring. Clarify unclear or unusual orders. Never crush modified-release or enteric-coated products unless an authoritative source confirms it is safe.

Medication calculations and dose checking

Medication calculations may involve unit conversion, dose-volume calculations, infusion rates and weight-based calculations. Keep units visible throughout the calculation, convert consistently and check whether the result is clinically plausible. Use a leading zero for doses below one unit and avoid trailing zeros, following accepted medication-safety conventions.

An educational formula may be expressed as volume to administer = prescribed dose ÷ available dose × available volume, provided the units are compatible. This formula does not validate the prescription or product. Students must use approved references, required independent checks and direct supervision, especially for paediatric, intravenous and high-alert medicines.

Adverse reactions, interactions and pharmacovigilance

Adverse reactions range from predictable dose-related effects to unusual immune-mediated responses. Interactions may occur between medicines, foods, supplements, alcohol or disease states. Allergic reactions can vary in severity; anaphylaxis is a medical emergency requiring immediate action under local protocol.

Monitor the intended therapeutic response and relevant adverse effects. Document and report suspected reactions through the institution's pharmacovigilance pathway and national reporting system where applicable. Do not rechallenge a patient or discontinue essential therapy independently unless emergency protocol or authorised direction requires it.

Integrated clinical-learning case

Scenario: During a supervised placement, a student encounters a patient whose condition or care needs require closer assessment. The patient asks questions and expresses preferences that may affect the proposed plan. The student must listen, gather relevant information, check identity and consent, assess immediate safety, communicate findings and seek supervision.

Discussion prompts: Which information is subjective and which is objective? What additional assessment would be appropriate within the student's competence? Which features require urgent escalation? How can dignity, privacy and shared decision-making be protected? What information should be included in a structured handover?

Learning point: Safe nursing integrates knowledge, observation, communication, ethical conduct and teamwork. Clinical decisions and interventions must follow approved local protocols and qualified supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For extended responses, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and connect the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define pathophysiology.

    Show answer guidance

    The study of functional changes associated with disease or injury and how they affect cells, tissues and organ systems.

  2. Differentiate necrosis from apoptosis.

    Show answer guidance

    Necrosis is cell death associated with membrane damage and inflammation; apoptosis is regulated cell death that generally limits inflammation.

  3. List the four broad stages of pharmacokinetics.

    Show answer guidance

    Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion.

  4. What is pharmacodynamics?

    Show answer guidance

    The study of the biochemical and physiological effects of medicines and their mechanisms of action.

  5. Define half-life.

    Show answer guidance

    The time required for a medicine concentration or amount in the body to decrease by half under defined conditions.

  6. Why is renal function relevant to medicine dosing?

    Show answer guidance

    The kidneys excrete many medicines or metabolites; impaired function can increase exposure and toxicity.

  7. What is a contraindication?

    Show answer guidance

    A circumstance in which a medicine or intervention should not be used because the risk is unacceptable under the relevant guidance.

  8. State the educational formula for a dose-volume calculation.

    Show answer guidance

    Volume = prescribed dose divided by available dose, multiplied by the available volume, with compatible units; the order and result still require verification.

  9. Why should modified-release tablets not be crushed automatically?

    Show answer guidance

    Crushing may alter release characteristics, cause dose dumping or reduce safety; verify with an authoritative product reference.

  10. What is an adverse drug reaction?

    Show answer guidance

    A harmful, unintended response to a medicine used at normal doses.

  11. Define therapeutic index.

    Show answer guidance

    A measure of the margin between doses or concentrations producing beneficial and harmful effects.

  12. Name two factors that influence drug distribution.

    Show answer guidance

    Protein binding, tissue perfusion, body composition, membrane permeability and age can influence distribution.

  13. What is antimicrobial or medication stewardship in the context of safe use?

    Show answer guidance

    It is the systematic promotion of appropriate medicine selection, dose, route and duration, review and monitoring.

  14. Why are high-alert medicines subject to additional safeguards?

    Show answer guidance

    Errors involving them have a higher risk of causing serious patient harm.

  15. What should a student do when a medication order appears unclear or unsafe?

    Show answer guidance

    Do not guess or proceed; clarify with the authorised prescriber or supervisor and follow institutional policy.
