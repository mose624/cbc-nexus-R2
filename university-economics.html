Diploma Economics: core principles and applications

Module 1 — Scarcity, choice and economic systems

Economics studies choices under scarcity. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone. A production possibility frontier (PPF) shows maximum attainable combinations of two goods with available resources and technology. Points inside indicate underutilisation; points on the frontier are productively efficient; points outside are currently unattainable.

PPF: bowed-out frontier — increasing opportunity cost as resources specialised for one good are transferred to producing another

Module 2 — Demand, supply and equilibrium

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices, other things equal. The law of demand predicts an inverse relationship. Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell; the law of supply predicts a positive relationship, other things equal. Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A change in the good's own price causes movement along a curve; a non-price determinant shifts the curve.

Worked equilibrium: Suppose Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 20 + 2P. Set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = 20 + 2P; 80 = 4P; P* = 20. Substitute: Q* = 60 units.

Module 3 — Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand (PED) = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. Using the midpoint method: PED = [(Q₂−Q₁)/((Q₂+Q₁)/2)] ÷ [(P₂−P₁)/((P₂+P₁)/2)]. Absolute PED greater than 1 is elastic; below 1 is inelastic; equal to 1 is unit elastic. Elasticity affects how price changes influence total revenue.

Module 4 — Production, costs and markets

Short run means at least one input is fixed; long run means all inputs can vary. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Average cost = total cost/output; marginal cost = change in total cost/change in output. Perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly differ in number of firms, product differentiation, entry barriers and strategic interdependence.

Module 5 — Macroeconomic indicators

GDP measures the market value of final goods and services produced within a territory in a period. Real GDP adjusts for price changes; nominal GDP uses current prices. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Unemployment measures people without work who are available and actively seeking it under the relevant statistical definition. Fiscal policy concerns government revenue and expenditure; monetary policy concerns money, credit and interest-rate conditions.

Undergraduate Economics: formal models and established theories

Consumer theory

Consumers choose bundles subject to a budget constraint. Under standard assumptions, an interior optimum occurs where the marginal rate of substitution equals the relative price ratio: MRSₓᵧ = Pₓ/Pᵧ. Indifference curves represent bundles yielding equal utility; they are generally downward sloping and convex under diminishing marginal rate of substitution. Corner solutions may occur, so the tangency condition is not universal.

Firm theory and market structure

Profit maximisation for a price-taking firm occurs where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC), provided the second-order and shutdown conditions are satisfied. In perfect competition, P = MR for an individual firm. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve and chooses output where MR = MC, then charges the price on the demand curve. Oligopoly models include Cournot quantity competition, Bertrand price competition and Stackelberg leadership; outcomes depend on assumptions about timing, products and strategic information.

Macroeconomic frameworks

The Keynesian cross links planned expenditure to output in the short run. The IS-LM framework analyses goods-market and money-market equilibrium under its assumptions. The AD-AS model connects aggregate demand and supply to output and the price level. The Solow growth model explains long-run output per worker through capital accumulation, labour growth, depreciation and exogenous technological progress; diminishing returns to capital imply that sustained per-capita growth ultimately depends on technological progress in the basic model.

AD shifts right → short-run output and price level tend to rise (magnitude depends on SRAS slope and context); long-run adjustment depends on expectations, capacity and policy

International trade

Comparative advantage arises from lower opportunity cost, not necessarily absolute productivity. The Heckscher–Ohlin model links trade patterns to relative factor endowments under restrictive assumptions. Specific-factors and new trade theories explain distributional effects, scale economies and product differentiation. Tariffs can protect selected producers but raise domestic prices and create efficiency losses; distributional effects matter.

Public finance and development

Public economics studies market failure, taxation, public goods, externalities and redistribution. Pigouvian taxes aim to align private and social marginal costs; subsidies may address positive externalities. Development economics examines structural transformation, institutions, human capital, poverty traps, credit constraints, coordination failures and inequality. No single development theory explains every country or period.

Econometrics foundations

The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator minimises the sum of squared residuals. Under the classical linear model assumptions, OLS has desirable properties; omitted variables, simultaneity, measurement error, selection and non-random missingness can bias estimates. Correlation alone does not establish causation. Interpret coefficients with units, uncertainty intervals, sample definition and model assumptions.

Postgraduate Economics: advanced theory, identification and policy research

Microeconomic foundations

Graduate microeconomics formalises preferences, choice under uncertainty, producer optimisation, general equilibrium and welfare. The First Welfare Theorem requires assumptions such as competitive markets, complete markets and absence of relevant externalities; it does not imply that actual markets are efficient or that outcomes are equitable. The Second Welfare Theorem concerns decentralisation of Pareto-efficient allocations under convexity assumptions and suitable redistribution.

Game theory and information economics

Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile in which no player can gain by unilateral deviation. Subgame perfection refines dynamic-game equilibria by requiring optimal play in every subgame. Adverse selection arises from hidden characteristics before a transaction; moral hazard arises from hidden actions after an agreement. Signalling and screening models examine how parties respond to asymmetric information.

Advanced macroeconomics

Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models describe optimising agents and market-clearing or other equilibrium conditions under shocks and frictions. New Keynesian models incorporate nominal rigidities and monetary policy rules. The expectations-augmented Phillips curve links inflation to expected inflation, economic slack and supply shocks under model-specific assumptions. Evaluate identification, calibration, estimation, policy invariance and external validity.

Causal inference and econometric identification

Potential-outcomes notation defines an individual treatment effect as Yᵢ(1) − Yᵢ(0), but only one potential outcome is observed for each unit. Randomisation identifies average treatment effects under design integrity and appropriate handling of attrition and interference. Difference-in-differences requires a credible parallel-trends assumption; event studies help inspect dynamics but do not prove the assumption. Instrumental variables require relevance and an exclusion restriction, with additional assumptions for particular estimands. Regression discontinuity relies on continuity around a threshold and no precise manipulation. State assumptions explicitly and test what can be tested.

Time series, panel data and policy evaluation

Time-series analysis considers stationarity, unit roots, cointegration, autocorrelation and structural breaks. Panel methods exploit repeated observations across units; fixed effects control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity but do not solve time-varying confounding. Policy evaluation should define the estimand, counterfactual, treatment timing, spillovers, distributional effects, costs and implementation context.

Research standards

Pre-specify hypotheses where appropriate, document data provenance and transformations, report uncertainty, disclose robustness checks, avoid selective specification searching and provide replication materials where ethically and legally possible. Distinguish statistical significance, economic magnitude and policy relevance.

Methods, diagrams and worked applications

Demand and supply diagram

Vertical axis: price (P); horizontal axis: quantity (Q). Demand slopes downward; supply slopes upward; their intersection is equilibrium (P*, Q*). A rise in input costs shifts supply left, raising equilibrium price and reducing equilibrium quantity, ceteris paribus.

Elasticity worked calculation

Price rises from 10 to 12; quantity falls from 100 to 80. Midpoint percentage change in quantity = −20/90 = −22.22%; midpoint percentage change in price = 2/11 = 18.18%. PED = −1.22 (absolute value 1.22), so demand is elastic over this interval. A 10% price rise would not automatically imply the same response outside this interval.

National income identity

For a simplified open economy, GDP = C + I + G + (X − M), where C is consumption, I investment, G government purchases, X exports and M imports. Transfers are not directly counted as government purchases; they affect disposable income and may influence consumption.

OLS interpretation

In Yᵢ = β₀ + β₁Xᵢ + uᵢ, β₁ is the expected change in Y associated with a one-unit increase in X under the model. A causal interpretation requires identification assumptions beyond fitting a regression. Report coefficient units, standard errors or confidence intervals, sample size and specification.

Chapter 1 — The economic problem, choice and economic methodology

1.1 Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

Economics studies how individuals, households, firms, governments and societies make choices when resources are limited and alternative uses compete. Scarcity does not mean that every person is poor; it means that time, labour, land, capital, knowledge and natural resources are finite relative to the wants and objectives people seek to satisfy. Because choices have consequences, economic analysis asks what is gained, what is sacrificed, who bears costs and how outcomes are distributed.

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone when a choice is made. If a government allocates a fixed budget to a new road, the opportunity cost may include the best alternative health, education or water project that cannot be funded. It is not necessarily the total of all rejected options, nor is it always a cash payment. A student who attends university gives up time that could have been spent in paid work, family care or another course of study. The relevant opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative, including non-monetary consequences where they matter.

Economic models simplify reality to clarify relationships. A model is not a miniature copy of the world; it is a structured representation built around assumptions. Ceteris paribus means “other things being equal” and allows analysts to isolate one relationship while holding other influences constant. In real economies, many influences change simultaneously, so model predictions should be tested against evidence and revised when assumptions are implausible.

1.2 Production possibility frontier

A production possibility frontier (PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two goods or categories of output that an economy can produce with given resources, technology and institutions. Points on the frontier are productively efficient under the model; points inside indicate underused resources or inefficiency; points outside are unattainable with current productive capacity. The frontier may shift outward when the quantity or quality of resources, technology or productive institutions improve. It may shift inward after conflict, disaster or destruction of productive capacity.

The slope of the PPF represents the opportunity cost of producing more of one good in terms of the other. A bowed-out frontier illustrates increasing opportunity cost: as resources specialised for one activity are transferred to another, progressively less suitable resources are used. The PPF is useful for discussing trade-offs, but it does not identify which combination society should choose. That is a normative question involving preferences, distribution, institutions and ethical priorities.

1.3 Positive and normative economics

Positive economics develops testable statements about what is, what was or what may happen under specified conditions. Normative economics evaluates what ought to be, drawing on ethical principles and social objectives. “A tax increase reduces disposable income, other things equal” is a positive proposition that can be examined. “The tax system should be more progressive” is normative because it depends on judgments about fairness and social priorities. Policy analysis often combines both: evidence estimates likely consequences, while public debate determines which objectives deserve priority.

1.4 Microeconomics and macroeconomics

Microeconomics studies choices and interactions of consumers, workers, firms and markets. It examines prices, incentives, costs, competition, information and market failure. Macroeconomics studies aggregate outcomes such as national output, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, public debt, exchange rates and economic growth. The two levels are connected: household consumption and firm investment contribute to aggregate demand, while inflation and interest-rate changes affect individual decisions. Aggregating individual behaviour is not always straightforward because composition, expectations and feedback can change outcomes.

Chapter 2 — Demand, supply and market equilibrium

2.1 Demand and the consumer

Demand is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy over a specified period, holding other determinants constant. The law of demand states that, other things equal, quantity demanded usually falls when price rises. This inverse relationship can be explained by substitution and income effects. When a good becomes relatively more expensive, consumers may switch to alternatives; the price rise can also reduce real purchasing power.

A movement along a demand curve results from a change in the good's own price. A shift of the entire curve results from a change in another determinant, such as income, preferences, population, expectations or the price of related goods. For a normal good, higher income generally raises demand; for an inferior good, higher income may reduce demand over a relevant income range. Substitutes are goods that can replace one another, while complements are used together. These relationships are empirical and can vary across consumers and contexts.

2.2 Supply and the firm

Supply describes the relationship between price and the quantity producers are willing and able to sell during a period. Higher prices may encourage firms to supply more because additional production becomes profitable, although capacity constraints and rising marginal costs matter. Supply shifts when production costs, technology, taxes, subsidies, expectations, the number of firms or natural conditions change. A rise in input prices usually shifts supply left; a productivity improvement may shift it right.

2.3 Equilibrium, shortages and surpluses

Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At a price below equilibrium, a shortage may emerge as buyers seek more than sellers offer. Competition among buyers or adjustments by sellers can put upward pressure on price. At a price above equilibrium, a surplus may emerge, creating pressure for price reductions or lower output. Actual markets may not adjust instantly because of contracts, regulation, menu costs, market power, information problems or rationing.

Worked example: Suppose demand is Qd = 100 − 2P and supply is Qs = 20 + 2P. At equilibrium, set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = 20 + 2P. Therefore 80 = 4P, so P = 20. Substitute into either equation: Q = 100 − 40 = 60. The equilibrium price is 20 currency units and quantity is 60 units. Check by substituting into supply: 20 + 40 = 60.

2.4 Price controls, taxes and subsidies

A binding price ceiling below equilibrium can make a good more affordable for consumers who obtain it, but it may also create shortages, queues, lower quality or informal allocation. A price floor above equilibrium can support producer incomes but may create surpluses or require government purchases. Outcomes depend on enforcement, market structure, storage, import access and complementary policies. A tax creates a wedge between the price paid by buyers and the net price received by sellers. The legal remitter is not necessarily the party bearing the economic burden; incidence depends on relative elasticities.

A subsidy lowers the effective cost of production or consumption. It may expand access or encourage socially valuable activity, but can also impose fiscal costs, distort incentives or benefit groups that do not need support. Good policy analysis identifies the objective, target group, financing method, distributional effects, administrative capacity and likely unintended consequences.

Chapter 3 — Elasticity and consumer responsiveness

3.1 Price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP. Because price and quantity demanded usually move in opposite directions, PED is often negative; many textbooks report its absolute value for classifying responsiveness. Demand is elastic when the absolute value exceeds one, inelastic when it is below one, and unit elastic when it equals one.

Elasticity depends on the availability of substitutes, the proportion of income spent on the good, whether the good is a necessity or luxury, the time available to adjust and how narrowly the market is defined. A product may have inelastic demand in the short run but more elastic demand in the long run as consumers find alternatives. Elasticity is not the same as the slope of a curve because it is a percentage measure and depends on the point on the curve.

Worked example: Price rises from 10 to 12 while quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80. Using the simple percentage method based on initial values, the percentage change in quantity is −20% and the percentage change in price is +20%, so PED = −1. Using the midpoint method, percentage changes are calculated relative to the average of initial and final values; quantity changes by −20/90 = −22.22%, and price by 2/11 = 18.18%, giving PED approximately −1.22. State which method is used because results can differ.

3.2 Income and cross-price elasticity

Income elasticity of demand (YED) is percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in income. A positive value is consistent with a normal good; a negative value is consistent with an inferior good over the observed range. Cross-price elasticity (XED) measures the percentage change in demand for good X divided by the percentage change in the price of good Y. A positive XED is commonly associated with substitutes; a negative value with complements. These classifications should be interpreted with care because product relationships can change by consumer group and market conditions.

3.3 Elasticity and total revenue

Total revenue equals price multiplied by quantity sold. When demand is elastic, a price reduction tends to increase total revenue because the percentage rise in quantity exceeds the percentage fall in price. When demand is inelastic, a price increase tends to raise total revenue because quantity falls proportionately less. At unit elasticity, total revenue is locally unchanged for a small price change under the standard model. Revenue effects do not by themselves establish whether a price change improves welfare or equity.

Chapter 4 — Production, costs and market structures

4.1 Production and productivity

Production transforms inputs into goods and services. Inputs include land and natural resources, labour, physical capital, human capital, entrepreneurship and intermediate goods. A production function represents the maximum output obtainable from a given combination of inputs and technology. In the short run, at least one factor is fixed; in the long run, all factors can be varied. Marginal product is the additional output generated by one more unit of an input, holding other inputs constant. Average product is output per unit of input.

The law of diminishing marginal returns states that, with at least one factor fixed, adding more units of a variable factor will eventually yield smaller additions to output under the conventional production setting. It does not mean total output must immediately fall. Congestion, coordination and fixed capacity can explain why marginal product declines. Productivity can rise through skills, technology, management, infrastructure, health and better organisation.

4.2 Costs and firm decisions

Total cost (TC) is the sum of fixed cost (FC) and variable cost (VC). Average total cost is TC divided by output; average variable cost is VC divided by output; marginal cost is the additional cost of producing one more unit. Fixed costs do not vary with output in the short run, although they may change in the long run. Sunk costs have already been incurred and cannot be recovered; rational forward-looking decisions should focus on relevant future costs and benefits rather than allowing sunk costs to distort choices.

In standard competitive models, a firm chooses output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, provided the output decision satisfies relevant shutdown and feasibility conditions. Profit is total revenue minus total economic cost, including opportunity costs. Accounting profit and economic profit differ because economic cost includes implicit costs such as the owner's alternative use of time and capital.

4.3 Market structures

Perfect competition is an idealised structure with many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, good information and free entry and exit. Individual firms are price takers. Monopoly involves a single seller or a firm with substantial market power protected by barriers to entry. Monopolistic competition features many firms selling differentiated products, while oligopoly involves a small number of strategically interdependent firms. Real markets may combine features of more than one structure.

Market power can allow prices above marginal cost and may reduce output relative to a competitive benchmark, but its consequences depend on innovation, scale economies, network effects, regulation and potential competition. Oligopoly analysis often uses game theory because each firm's best action depends on expected actions by rivals. Collusion may raise prices and reduce consumer welfare; competition authorities examine evidence and legal standards before making findings.

4.4 Labour markets and inequality

Labour demand is derived from demand for the output workers help produce. Wages reflect productivity, scarcity, bargaining institutions, discrimination, regulation, monopsony power and broader economic conditions. Human capital theory emphasises education, training and experience, but wage differences cannot be explained by skills alone. Segmented labour-market approaches examine differences between secure and insecure employment, formal and informal work, and unequal access to career ladders. Analyse labour markets with attention to gender, disability, migration status, care responsibilities and regional opportunity.

Chapter 5 — Macroeconomic measurement, output and employment

5.1 Gross domestic product and national income

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the market value of final goods and services produced within an economy during a specified period. The expenditure identity is GDP = C + I + G + (X − M), where C is household consumption, I is investment, G is government purchases, X is exports and M is imports. Imports are subtracted because they may already be included in consumption, investment or government expenditure but are produced abroad. Transfer payments are not direct purchases of current output and are not counted as government purchases in this identity.

Nominal GDP values output at current prices; real GDP adjusts for price changes using a chosen price basis or chain-volume method. GDP per capita divides GDP by population but does not show how income is distributed. GDP also omits or imperfectly measures unpaid care, informal activity, environmental depletion, leisure, safety and many aspects of wellbeing. It is useful but incomplete as a measure of social progress.

5.2 Inflation and price indices

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level, reducing the purchasing power of money. Consumer price indices track price changes for a representative basket, with methods varying across statistical agencies. Headline inflation includes the full basket; core measures exclude or adjust volatile components to help identify underlying trends. Inflation can arise from demand pressures, supply shocks, exchange-rate depreciation, expectations and cost increases. A single cause rarely explains every episode.

The inflation rate is not the same as the price level. If inflation falls from 10% to 4%, prices are still rising, but more slowly. Deflation is a sustained decline in the general price level and can create difficulties when nominal debts remain fixed. Disinflation refers to a decline in the rate of inflation. Policy responses should consider the source of inflation, expectations, output gaps and distributional effects.

5.3 Unemployment and labour-market indicators

The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, multiplied by 100. The labour force includes employed and unemployed people who meet the relevant activity and availability criteria. People outside the labour force are not counted as unemployed under the standard definition, even if they would like work but are not currently searching or available. Underemployment, discouraged workers, informality and job quality provide additional information about labour-market conditions.

Frictional unemployment arises as people move between jobs; structural unemployment reflects mismatches in skills, location or institutions; cyclical unemployment is associated with downturns in aggregate demand. These categories can overlap. Policies may include job-search support, training, mobility assistance, macroeconomic stabilisation, childcare, transport and demand for labour. Training alone cannot solve unemployment if suitable jobs are absent.

Chapter 6 — Aggregate demand, aggregate supply and stabilisation policy

6.1 Aggregate demand

Aggregate demand represents planned expenditure on domestically produced output at different overall price levels, under the model's assumptions. Its components include consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports. Consumption is influenced by disposable income, wealth, expectations, credit conditions and household balance sheets. Investment depends on expected returns, interest rates, uncertainty, capacity utilisation and financing conditions. Net exports respond to domestic and foreign incomes, relative prices and exchange rates.

6.2 Aggregate supply and shocks

Short-run aggregate supply may slope upward when some wages and prices adjust slowly or when firms face changing marginal costs. Long-run aggregate supply is linked to productive capacity, including labour, capital, technology, institutions and resources. A negative supply shock, such as a sudden increase in energy costs, can raise prices while reducing output. A positive productivity shock can increase output and ease cost pressures. The distinction between demand and supply shocks matters for choosing policy responses.

6.3 Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy uses government spending, taxation and transfers to influence aggregate demand, distribution and public services. Expansionary fiscal policy may support demand during a downturn through higher spending or lower taxes; contractionary measures may restrain demand or improve fiscal balances. The multiplier describes how an initial spending change can produce a larger total change in income under particular assumptions. Its size depends on leakages into saving, taxes and imports, spare capacity, monetary conditions and expectations.

Fiscal sustainability is not simply the absence of public debt. Analysts examine debt service, currency denomination, maturity, interest-growth differentials, revenue capacity, contingent liabilities, investment returns and social needs. Austerity can improve some fiscal indicators but may also weaken output, public services and long-term productive capacity. Evaluation requires a counterfactual and attention to who bears adjustment costs.

6.4 Monetary policy

Monetary policy is generally conducted by a central bank and influences financial conditions through policy rates, liquidity operations, communication and, in some jurisdictions, asset purchases or other instruments. Higher policy rates tend to raise borrowing costs, restrain interest-sensitive spending and influence exchange rates and expectations. Transmission is not immediate or uniform. The effectiveness of policy depends on financial structure, credibility, household debt, bank lending and the nature of the shock.

Central-bank mandates vary. Some prioritise price stability, while others also consider employment, output or financial stability. Independence can protect policy decisions from short-term political pressure, but accountability, transparency and democratic oversight remain important. Monetary policy cannot directly create missing food, fuel or infrastructure; supply-side constraints require complementary measures.

Chapter 7 — Economic growth, development and international trade

7.1 Sources and measurement of growth

Economic growth is commonly measured as an increase in real output over time. Long-run growth depends on labour, physical capital, human capital, technological progress, institutions, infrastructure, entrepreneurship and the use of natural resources. The Solow growth model explains how capital accumulation, labour growth and exogenous technological progress shape output per worker. With diminishing returns to capital, sustained growth in output per worker requires continuing improvements in productivity or other drivers beyond simple capital deepening.

Growth accounting decomposes output growth into contributions from factor inputs and a residual often interpreted as total factor productivity, subject to measurement limitations. Productivity is affected by innovation, management, competition, education, infrastructure, health, institutional quality and resource allocation. Rapid output growth does not guarantee broad-based development if gains are concentrated, environmental costs are high or essential services remain inaccessible.

7.2 Development theories and structural transformation

Structural transformation describes changes in the composition of output and employment as economies develop, often involving shifts from agriculture toward manufacturing and services. Lewis's dual-sector model examines movement of labour from a traditional sector with low measured marginal productivity to a modern sector. Its assumptions and relevance vary across economies with large informal sectors, land constraints and weak job creation. Rostow's stages of growth present a historical sequence of development stages but are criticised for linearity and limited attention to colonial histories, unequal global relations and diverse pathways.

Dependency approaches emphasise unequal economic relationships between core and peripheral economies; world-systems analysis situates production and power in a global hierarchy. Institutional approaches examine how rules, state capacity, property rights, accountability and political settlements influence investment and public goods. Human-development approaches focus on capabilities and substantive freedoms. Each framework highlights different mechanisms; strong analysis compares them against historical evidence rather than treating any as a universal formula.

7.3 International trade and comparative advantage

Comparative advantage occurs when a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country. Trade can permit specialisation and consumption beyond domestic production possibilities. Absolute advantage concerns producing more with a given resource input, whereas comparative advantage concerns relative opportunity cost. The gains from trade are potential gains; their distribution depends on wages, ownership, adjustment costs, market power, public policy and the ability of workers and firms to move between activities.

Trade policy includes tariffs, quotas, subsidies, standards, trade facilitation and regional agreements. Tariffs can protect selected producers and raise public revenue, but often increase prices for consumers and input users and may invite retaliation. Non-tariff measures can pursue legitimate health, safety or environmental goals, but may also restrict market access. Policy evaluation should distinguish protection for learning and capability-building from indefinite support that entrenches inefficiency or political rents.

7.4 Exchange rates and balance of payments

The balance of payments records transactions between residents and non-residents. The current account includes trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income transfers. The capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and financial flows under the relevant statistical framework. A current-account deficit is not automatically a crisis; its sustainability depends on financing, investment quality, currency risk, external liabilities and future earning capacity.

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Depreciation may improve export competitiveness over time but can raise the domestic price of imported fuel, food, machinery and debt service. The pass-through depends on invoicing, competition, contracts and monetary conditions. A country's exchange-rate regime, reserves, capital mobility and policy credibility shape its adjustment options.

Chapter 8 — Public finance, market failure and economic policy

8.1 Public goods and externalities

A pure public good is non-rival and non-excludable: one person's consumption does not reduce another's, and it is difficult to prevent non-payers from benefiting. National defence and some forms of basic scientific knowledge approximate this definition. Common-pool resources are rival but difficult to exclude users from, creating potential overuse. Club goods are excludable but largely non-rival until congestion occurs. These categories help explain why private markets may underprovide some goods or overuse shared resources.

An externality occurs when an action affects third parties outside the market transaction. Negative externalities include pollution and congestion; positive externalities include some benefits of vaccination, education and research. A Pigouvian tax can align private incentives with social costs when the external damage can be estimated and monitored. Subsidies, regulation, tradable permits, liability rules and public provision are alternative instruments. The best instrument depends on information, enforcement, administrative capacity and distribution.

8.2 Information asymmetry and behavioural considerations

Information asymmetry occurs when one party to a transaction has relevant information that another lacks. Adverse selection can occur before a transaction when hidden characteristics affect participation; moral hazard can occur after a transaction when hidden actions change incentives. Signalling, screening, warranties, disclosure, regulation and reputation can reduce some problems. Behavioural economics studies how attention, framing, habits, social norms and bounded rationality influence decisions. Behavioural interventions should be transparent, proportionate and respectful of autonomy.

8.3 Taxation, redistribution and public expenditure

Taxes may be direct or indirect, progressive, proportional or regressive relative to income. A progressive tax takes a larger share of income as income rises; a regressive tax takes a larger share from lower-income households. Statutory design does not alone determine economic incidence. Public expenditure can provide public goods, correct market failures, redistribute resources and invest in human and physical capital. Assess expenditure by effectiveness, equity, opportunity cost, transparency and long-term fiscal implications.

Chapter 9 — Econometrics, evidence and causal inference

9.1 From economic question to empirical strategy

Econometrics applies statistical methods to economic data to estimate relationships, test theories and evaluate policies. A research design begins with a clear estimand: the quantity the researcher seeks to estimate, such as the average effect of a training programme on participants' earnings. Define the population, treatment or exposure, outcome, time horizon and comparison condition. A correlation may be useful for description but does not by itself establish causation.

9.2 Ordinary least squares and interpretation

A simple linear regression can be written Yᵢ = β₀ + β₁Xᵢ + uᵢ. The coefficient β₁ describes the expected change in Y associated with a one-unit change in X under the model. In multiple regression, coefficients are conditional on included covariates. OLS estimates have causal interpretations only under appropriate assumptions, including a defensible exogeneity condition. Omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error, sample selection and functional-form misspecification can undermine interpretation.

Statistical significance is not the same as practical importance. Confidence intervals communicate uncertainty around estimates; standard errors depend on sampling and model assumptions. Researchers should consider heteroskedasticity, clustering, serial correlation and spatial dependence where relevant. Report units, sample size, missing-data treatment, model specification and robustness checks. Avoid selecting specifications only because they produce a preferred result.

9.3 Natural experiments and quasi-experimental designs

Difference-in-differences compares changes over time between a treatment group and a comparison group. Its central identifying assumption is that, absent treatment, the groups would have followed parallel trends. Researchers should examine pre-treatment trends, anticipate spillovers, account for group-specific shocks and consider treatment timing. Instrumental variables use a source of variation that changes treatment exposure but affects the outcome only through the treatment, subject to relevance and exclusion restrictions. These assumptions are substantive and cannot be proven by a single statistical test.

Regression discontinuity designs exploit a treatment rule based on a threshold. Near the cutoff, units may be comparable if they cannot precisely manipulate assignment and other conditions hold. The estimated effect is local to the threshold and may not generalise to all units. Randomised controlled trials can provide strong internal validity when implemented well, but attrition, non-compliance, spillovers, ethical constraints and external validity remain important.

Extended applied case study — Evaluating a youth employment programme

Suppose a public agency offers vocational training to young adults in selected districts. The policy question is whether participation improves employment, earnings, job stability or job quality. A weak evaluation might compare average earnings of participants and non-participants after training and attribute the difference to the programme. This comparison may be biased because participants could be more motivated, better connected or initially more employable. Alternatively, the programme may target people facing greater barriers, making participants' outcomes appear worse even if the programme helps.

A stronger design would establish baseline characteristics and outcomes before implementation, define eligibility and treatment clearly, and identify a credible comparison group. If programme rollout is phased, difference-in-differences may be considered after assessing parallel trends and spillovers. If a transparent score threshold determines eligibility, a regression-discontinuity design may be appropriate near the cutoff. Where random allocation is ethical and feasible, a randomised design may be considered. The choice should be justified by institutional rules and the research question rather than statistical convenience.

Outcome measures should include more than a single employment indicator. Researchers may examine employment status, hours, earnings, contract security, occupational match, job-search duration and self-employment survival. Disaggregate results where sample size and privacy allow, while avoiding unsupported subgroup claims. Cost-effectiveness analysis should include programme delivery costs, participant time, travel, childcare and the opportunity cost of public funds. Qualitative interviews can explain why a programme works for some groups and not others.

Case study tasks: (1) Write a precise causal research question. (2) Identify two possible sources of selection bias. (3) Propose a feasible evaluation design and state its identifying assumption. (4) Select three outcome indicators and explain their limitations. (5) Discuss privacy, informed consent and the risk of excluding people with disabilities or care responsibilities.
