Diploma Economics: core principles and applications

Module 1 — Scarcity, choice and economic systems

Economics studies choices under scarcity. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone. A production possibility frontier (PPF) shows maximum attainable combinations of two goods with available resources and technology. Points inside indicate underutilisation; points on the frontier are productively efficient; points outside are currently unattainable.

PPF: bowed-out frontier — increasing opportunity cost as resources specialised for one good are transferred to producing another

Module 2 — Demand, supply and equilibrium

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices, other things equal. The law of demand predicts an inverse relationship. Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell; the law of supply predicts a positive relationship, other things equal. Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A change in the good's own price causes movement along a curve; a non-price determinant shifts the curve.

Worked equilibrium: Suppose Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 20 + 2P. Set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = 20 + 2P; 80 = 4P; P* = 20. Substitute: Q* = 60 units.

Module 3 — Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand (PED) = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. Using the midpoint method: PED = [(Q₂−Q₁)/((Q₂+Q₁)/2)] ÷ [(P₂−P₁)/((P₂+P₁)/2)]. Absolute PED greater than 1 is elastic; below 1 is inelastic; equal to 1 is unit elastic. Elasticity affects how price changes influence total revenue.

Module 4 — Production, costs and markets

Short run means at least one input is fixed; long run means all inputs can vary. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Average cost = total cost/output; marginal cost = change in total cost/change in output. Perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly differ in number of firms, product differentiation, entry barriers and strategic interdependence.

Module 5 — Macroeconomic indicators

GDP measures the market value of final goods and services produced within a territory in a period. Real GDP adjusts for price changes; nominal GDP uses current prices. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Unemployment measures people without work who are available and actively seeking it under the relevant statistical definition. Fiscal policy concerns government revenue and expenditure; monetary policy concerns money, credit and interest-rate conditions.

Undergraduate Economics: formal models and established theories

Consumer theory

Consumers choose bundles subject to a budget constraint. Under standard assumptions, an interior optimum occurs where the marginal rate of substitution equals the relative price ratio: MRSₓᵧ = Pₓ/Pᵧ. Indifference curves represent bundles yielding equal utility; they are generally downward sloping and convex under diminishing marginal rate of substitution. Corner solutions may occur, so the tangency condition is not universal.

Firm theory and market structure

Profit maximisation for a price-taking firm occurs where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC), provided the second-order and shutdown conditions are satisfied. In perfect competition, P = MR for an individual firm. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve and chooses output where MR = MC, then charges the price on the demand curve. Oligopoly models include Cournot quantity competition, Bertrand price competition and Stackelberg leadership; outcomes depend on assumptions about timing, products and strategic information.

Macroeconomic frameworks

The Keynesian cross links planned expenditure to output in the short run. The IS-LM framework analyses goods-market and money-market equilibrium under its assumptions. The AD-AS model connects aggregate demand and supply to output and the price level. The Solow growth model explains long-run output per worker through capital accumulation, labour growth, depreciation and exogenous technological progress; diminishing returns to capital imply that sustained per-capita growth ultimately depends on technological progress in the basic model.

AD shifts right → short-run output and price level tend to rise (magnitude depends on SRAS slope and context); long-run adjustment depends on expectations, capacity and policy

International trade

Comparative advantage arises from lower opportunity cost, not necessarily absolute productivity. The Heckscher–Ohlin model links trade patterns to relative factor endowments under restrictive assumptions. Specific-factors and new trade theories explain distributional effects, scale economies and product differentiation. Tariffs can protect selected producers but raise domestic prices and create efficiency losses; distributional effects matter.

Public finance and development

Public economics studies market failure, taxation, public goods, externalities and redistribution. Pigouvian taxes aim to align private and social marginal costs; subsidies may address positive externalities. Development economics examines structural transformation, institutions, human capital, poverty traps, credit constraints, coordination failures and inequality. No single development theory explains every country or period.

Econometrics foundations

The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator minimises the sum of squared residuals. Under the classical linear model assumptions, OLS has desirable properties; omitted variables, simultaneity, measurement error, selection and non-random missingness can bias estimates. Correlation alone does not establish causation. Interpret coefficients with units, uncertainty intervals, sample definition and model assumptions.

Postgraduate Economics: advanced theory, identification and policy research

Microeconomic foundations

Graduate microeconomics formalises preferences, choice under uncertainty, producer optimisation, general equilibrium and welfare. The First Welfare Theorem requires assumptions such as competitive markets, complete markets and absence of relevant externalities; it does not imply that actual markets are efficient or that outcomes are equitable. The Second Welfare Theorem concerns decentralisation of Pareto-efficient allocations under convexity assumptions and suitable redistribution.

Game theory and information economics

Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile in which no player can gain by unilateral deviation. Subgame perfection refines dynamic-game equilibria by requiring optimal play in every subgame. Adverse selection arises from hidden characteristics before a transaction; moral hazard arises from hidden actions after an agreement. Signalling and screening models examine how parties respond to asymmetric information.

Advanced macroeconomics

Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models describe optimising agents and market-clearing or other equilibrium conditions under shocks and frictions. New Keynesian models incorporate nominal rigidities and monetary policy rules. The expectations-augmented Phillips curve links inflation to expected inflation, economic slack and supply shocks under model-specific assumptions. Evaluate identification, calibration, estimation, policy invariance and external validity.

Causal inference and econometric identification

Potential-outcomes notation defines an individual treatment effect as Yᵢ(1) − Yᵢ(0), but only one potential outcome is observed for each unit. Randomisation identifies average treatment effects under design integrity and appropriate handling of attrition and interference. Difference-in-differences requires a credible parallel-trends assumption; event studies help inspect dynamics but do not prove the assumption. Instrumental variables require relevance and an exclusion restriction, with additional assumptions for particular estimands. Regression discontinuity relies on continuity around a threshold and no precise manipulation. State assumptions explicitly and test what can be tested.

Time series, panel data and policy evaluation

Time-series analysis considers stationarity, unit roots, cointegration, autocorrelation and structural breaks. Panel methods exploit repeated observations across units; fixed effects control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity but do not solve time-varying confounding. Policy evaluation should define the estimand, counterfactual, treatment timing, spillovers, distributional effects, costs and implementation context.

Research standards

Pre-specify hypotheses where appropriate, document data provenance and transformations, report uncertainty, disclose robustness checks, avoid selective specification searching and provide replication materials where ethically and legally possible. Distinguish statistical significance, economic magnitude and policy relevance.

Methods, diagrams and worked applications

Demand and supply diagram

Vertical axis: price (P); horizontal axis: quantity (Q). Demand slopes downward; supply slopes upward; their intersection is equilibrium (P*, Q*). A rise in input costs shifts supply left, raising equilibrium price and reducing equilibrium quantity, ceteris paribus.

Elasticity worked calculation

Price rises from 10 to 12; quantity falls from 100 to 80. Midpoint percentage change in quantity = −20/90 = −22.22%; midpoint percentage change in price = 2/11 = 18.18%. PED = −1.22 (absolute value 1.22), so demand is elastic over this interval. A 10% price rise would not automatically imply the same response outside this interval.

National income identity

For a simplified open economy, GDP = C + I + G + (X − M), where C is consumption, I investment, G government purchases, X exports and M imports. Transfers are not directly counted as government purchases; they affect disposable income and may influence consumption.

OLS interpretation

In Yᵢ = β₀ + β₁Xᵢ + uᵢ, β₁ is the expected change in Y associated with a one-unit increase in X under the model. A causal interpretation requires identification assumptions beyond fitting a regression. Report coefficient units, standard errors or confidence intervals, sample size and specification.

Original sample examination paper

Instructions: This is an original CBE Nexus practice paper, not an official past paper. Suggested time: 2 hours for diploma and 3 hours for degree/postgraduate sections. Answer as directed, show calculations or analytical steps, label diagrams and cite sources where appropriate.

Section A — Diploma (Answer all; 20 marks)

1. Define opportunity cost and scarcity (4). 2. Given Qd = 80 − 2P and Qs = 20 + P, calculate equilibrium price and quantity (6). 3. Distinguish a movement along demand from a shift in demand (4). 4. Explain two causes and two effects of inflation (6).

Section B — Undergraduate (Answer two; 40 marks)

5. Explain consumer equilibrium using indifference curves and the budget constraint. State the assumptions (20). 6. Compare perfect competition and monopoly in terms of output, price, efficiency and welfare (20). 7. Discuss the strengths and limitations of the Solow growth model for explaining long-run growth (20).

Section C — Postgraduate (Answer one; 20 marks)

8. Propose a credible difference-in-differences evaluation of a national cash-transfer programme. Define the estimand, treatment and comparison groups, parallel-trends assessment and threats to validity (20). 9. Explain how adverse selection and moral hazard can affect insurance markets and evaluate policy responses (20).

Indicative marking guidance

Q2: 80 − 2P = 20 + P; 60 = 3P; P = 20; Q = 40. Award calculation method and correct units. Essay marks reward model accuracy, assumptions, diagrams, welfare or identification analysis, evidence and limitations.

Self-assessment: A strong answer defines key concepts, applies appropriate theory, uses relevant examples or evidence, explains limitations and reaches a reasoned conclusion. Allocate marks to knowledge, application, analysis and communication rather than memorised phrases alone.

Further learning and integrity

Use your institution's library catalogue, peer-reviewed journals, official statistical agencies, national mapping or meteorological services, and reputable international datasets. Verify publication dates, definitions, geographical coverage and methods before using data. Never invent references, statistics, field observations or examination provenance.
