1. Anatomical language and levels of organisation

Anatomy describes body structures; physiology explains how those structures function. The standard anatomical position provides a shared reference: the person stands upright, faces forward, arms by the sides, palms facing forward and feet directed forward. Superior means toward the head; inferior toward the feet; anterior toward the front; posterior toward the back; medial toward the midline; lateral away from it; proximal nearer the trunk or point of origin; distal farther away.

The body is organised from chemical components to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems and the whole organism. Cells are the basic living units. Similar cells and extracellular material form tissues; tissues combine to form organs; organs work together in systems. This hierarchy helps nurses connect microscopic changes with observations at the bedside.

2. Cell structure and function

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer containing proteins, cholesterol and carbohydrate groups. It regulates movement, cell signalling and recognition. The nucleus stores most genetic material and regulates gene expression. Mitochondria generate much of the cell's ATP through aerobic metabolism. Ribosomes synthesise proteins; rough endoplasmic reticulum processes proteins for membranes or secretion; smooth endoplasmic reticulum participates in lipid synthesis and detoxification; the Golgi apparatus modifies and packages molecules.

Transport across membranes includes simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport and vesicular transport. Diffusion moves particles down a concentration gradient; osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane in response to effective solute differences. Active transport uses energy directly or indirectly to move substances against gradients. Cell function depends on oxygen, nutrients, temperature, pH and intact membranes.

3. Tissues and tissue repair

The four primary tissue classes are epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue. Epithelia cover surfaces, line cavities and form glands; they are classified by cell shape and number of layers. Connective tissue supports, binds and protects and includes loose and dense connective tissue, adipose tissue, cartilage, bone and blood. Muscle tissue contracts and occurs as skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle. Nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glial cells.

Tissue repair involves inflammation, cell proliferation, matrix deposition and remodelling. Healing differs by tissue type, blood supply, injury severity, infection, nutrition, age and underlying disease. Nurses observe wounds for changes in size, tissue appearance, exudate, surrounding skin, pain and systemic signs, and report concerns according to local protocols.

4. Integumentary system

The skin comprises epidermis and dermis, with the hypodermis beneath. The epidermis is keratinised stratified squamous epithelium; its strata include basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum in thick skin, and corneum. Keratinocytes form the principal barrier; melanocytes produce melanin; immune cells contribute to defence; sensory receptors detect touch, pressure, pain and temperature. The dermis contains connective tissue, vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands.

Skin functions include barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, vitamin D synthesis and limiting fluid loss. Sweat evaporation helps dissipate heat; cutaneous blood vessels alter heat exchange. Pressure, moisture, friction, shear, impaired perfusion and reduced mobility can damage skin. Skin assessment should consider colour changes across different skin tones, temperature, moisture, turgor, integrity, lesions and pressure-related risk.

5. Skeletal system, bones and joints

The adult skeleton is commonly described as 206 bones, although anatomical variation exists. The axial skeleton includes skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum; the appendicular skeleton includes limb bones and their girdles. Bone is living connective tissue containing cells and a mineralised collagen matrix. Osteoblasts form bone, osteoclasts resorb it and osteocytes maintain the matrix. Compact bone is dense; spongy bone contains trabeculae and marrow spaces.

Long bones have a diaphysis, epiphyses, metaphyses, periosteum, endosteum and medullary cavity. Joints are classified structurally as fibrous, cartilaginous or synovial and functionally by movement. Synovial joints contain a cavity, articular cartilage, capsule and synovial fluid; ligaments reinforce them. Calcium and phosphate balance, mechanical loading, hormones and nutrition influence bone health.

6. Muscular system and movement

Skeletal muscle is voluntary striated muscle attached to bones or other structures. A muscle fibre contains myofibrils made of repeating sarcomeres. Thin actin and thick myosin filaments interact during contraction. Calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds troponin, moving tropomyosin and permitting cross-bridge cycling; ATP supports cross-bridge detachment and re-cocking. Motor neurons activate muscle fibres at neuromuscular junctions.

Muscles work in coordinated groups. Agonists produce a movement, antagonists oppose it and synergists assist or stabilise. Isometric contraction develops tension without a major change in muscle length; isotonic contraction changes length while moving a load. Mobility assessment considers strength, range of motion, balance, pain, coordination and functional ability.

7. Nervous system organisation

The nervous system includes the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and peripheral nervous system (cranial and spinal nerves and ganglia). Sensory pathways carry information toward the CNS; motor pathways carry commands to effectors. The somatic system controls skeletal muscle; the autonomic system regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands through sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.

Neurons contain a cell body, dendrites and an axon. Action potentials depend on voltage-gated ion channels and changes in membrane potential. Synapses transmit signals chemically or electrically. Glial cells support, protect and insulate neurons. Myelin increases conduction speed by enabling saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.

8. Brain, spinal cord and reflexes

The cerebrum supports conscious perception, voluntary movement, language, memory and executive functions. The diencephalon includes thalamic and hypothalamic structures; the brainstem comprises midbrain, pons and medulla; the cerebellum coordinates movement and balance. The spinal cord carries ascending sensory and descending motor pathways and integrates many reflexes.

A reflex arc typically includes receptor, sensory neuron, integration centre, motor neuron and effector. Reflexes can be protective or postural. Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid protect the CNS. Neurological observations must use approved assessment methods and be interpreted with baseline function, clinical context and escalation criteria.

9. Special senses

Vision begins when light passes through the cornea and lens and is focused on the retina. Rods are highly sensitive in low light; cones support colour and high-acuity vision. Photoreceptors convert light into electrical signals that travel through retinal circuits and the optic nerve. Hearing begins with sound waves moving the tympanic membrane and ossicles; movement of cochlear fluid stimulates hair cells that signal through the vestibulocochlear nerve.

The vestibular apparatus contributes to balance and head-position sensing. Olfaction detects airborne chemicals; taste receptors detect dissolved substances. Sensory function affects communication, mobility, nutrition and safety. Assessments should account for glasses, hearing aids, language, lighting and environmental barriers.

10. Endocrine system foundations

Endocrine glands release hormones into the circulation. Hormones act on target cells with appropriate receptors and regulate metabolism, growth, reproduction, stress responses, fluid balance and other functions. The hypothalamus links nervous and endocrine regulation; the pituitary influences several endocrine glands. Thyroid hormones regulate metabolic activity; parathyroid hormone contributes to calcium regulation; adrenal hormones participate in stress and salt-water balance; pancreatic islets regulate blood glucose.

Hormone secretion is often controlled by negative feedback. A change in a regulated variable triggers responses that oppose the initial change. Positive feedback occurs in selected processes, such as oxytocin-associated uterine contractions during labour. Endocrine assessment links symptoms, medicines, laboratory results and clinical context; reference intervals and interpretation vary by laboratory.

Integrated case-based learning

Case: A student nurse is assigned to observe an adult patient during a supervised learning placement. The patient has a change from their usual condition and expresses concern about the proposed care. The student must gather relevant information, listen respectfully, check identity and consent, consider immediate safety, communicate findings to the supervising nurse and document objectively.

Discussion: Identify the information that is subjective and objective. Which findings require prompt escalation? What additional context would help interpretation? How can the student preserve dignity and confidentiality? Which actions are within the student's authorised competence, and which require direct supervision? Explain how the chapter's concepts support safe decisions without attempting to diagnose or prescribe independently.

Learning point: Sound nursing practice combines scientific knowledge, careful observation, communication, ethical conduct, teamwork and timely escalation. A textbook scenario is not a substitute for local protocols or direct clinical supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For longer questions, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and relate the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define nursing and identify four major purposes of nursing practice.

    Show answer guidance

    Nursing is a professional discipline that promotes health, prevents illness, supports recovery, relieves suffering and protects dignity. Examples include health promotion, prevention, care coordination and rehabilitation.

  2. Outline the five stages of the nursing process.

    Show answer guidance

    Assessment; analysis or nursing diagnosis; planning; implementation; evaluation. The process is iterative and should be revised when new information emerges.

  3. Differentiate autonomy and beneficence.

    Show answer guidance

    Autonomy concerns respect for a person's informed choices; beneficence concerns acting to promote wellbeing. Ethical decisions may require balancing these and other principles.

  4. State three features of effective therapeutic communication.

    Show answer guidance

    Active listening, open questions, clarification, empathy, summarising and checking understanding are examples.

  5. Explain why accurate documentation is important.

    Show answer guidance

    It supports continuity, communication, accountability, evaluation, legal record-keeping and patient safety.

  6. Differentiate anatomy from physiology.

    Show answer guidance

    Anatomy studies body structures and their relationships; physiology studies how those structures function.

  7. Name the four primary tissue classes.

    Show answer guidance

    Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

  8. Describe the role of the plasma membrane.

    Show answer guidance

    It forms a selectively permeable boundary, regulates transport, supports signalling and contributes to cell recognition.

  9. State two functions of the integumentary system.

    Show answer guidance

    Barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, limiting water loss and vitamin D synthesis.

  10. Explain the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems.

    Show answer guidance

    The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS.

  11. Trace the normal flow of blood through the heart.

    Show answer guidance

    Venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  12. What is cardiac output?

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute; it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.

  13. State the main function of alveoli.

    Show answer guidance

    Alveoli provide a large, thin surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

  14. Name the three basic processes of urine formation.

    Show answer guidance

    Glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion.

  15. List the links in the chain of infection.

    Show answer guidance

    Infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host.
