Clinical anatomy masterclass: orientation, tissue architecture and the locomotor system

Purpose of this model section. This expanded teaching unit demonstrates the level of anatomical precision expected throughout the chapter. It connects standard anatomical language to three-dimensional relationships, tissue structure, movement and clinical localisation. 1. Anatomical position is the fixed reference, not the patient's posture

The standard anatomical position is an upright body with the head and eyes directed anteriorly, upper limbs alongside the trunk, elbows extended, forearms supinated and palms facing anteriorly, lower limbs together or slightly separated, and feet directed forwards. Anatomical descriptions are referenced to this position even when a person is supine, prone, seated or moving. This convention prevents the meaning of right, left, medial and lateral from changing with the observer's viewpoint. Right and left refer to the subject's own sides, not the examiner's.

Use anterior and posterior for front and back; superior and inferior for toward the head and feet; medial and lateral for nearer to and farther from the median plane; proximal and distal for nearer to and farther from the trunk or point of origin, especially in the limbs; and superficial and deep for nearer to and farther from the body surface. The terms are relational: the sternum is anterior to the heart, while the heart is posterior to the sternum. The radius is lateral to the ulna in anatomical position. In pronation, the radius crosses the ulna; the bones do not exchange their anatomical identities.

2. Movements must be described relative to a plane and axis

Flexion and extension usually occur in the sagittal plane around a mediolateral axis. Abduction and adduction usually occur in the frontal plane around an anteroposterior axis. Medial and lateral rotation occur around the longitudinal axis of a segment. Circumduction combines flexion, extension, abduction and adduction in sequence. At the forearm, pronation and supination occur at the proximal and distal radioulnar joints: in supination the radius and ulna are parallel; in pronation the radius crosses anterior to the ulna. At the ankle, dorsiflexion brings the dorsum of the foot toward the leg; plantarflexion points the foot away. Inversion turns the plantar surface medially and eversion turns it laterally.

Do not confuse movement names with the direction in which a joint happens to appear in a photograph. For example, shoulder abduction moves the humerus away from the trunk in the frontal plane; it is not simply “lifting the arm” because arm elevation can also occur in the sagittal plane or scapular plane. The scapula contributes upward rotation, posterior tilt and external rotation during full overhead elevation. Glenohumeral movement and scapulothoracic motion are functionally coupled.

3. Levels of organisation: from extracellular matrix to organ system

Cells specialise through differential gene expression, cytoskeletal organisation, membrane transport and intercellular signalling. A tissue is an organised group of cells and extracellular material performing related functions. The four basic tissue classes are epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue. Organs combine multiple tissue classes; systems coordinate organs to achieve integrated functions. The extracellular matrix (ECM) is not inert packing material: its collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, glycoproteins and bound signalling molecules influence tissue strength, elasticity, hydration, cell adhesion and cell behaviour.

Epithelial tissue forms coverings, linings and glands. It is characterised by closely apposed cells, apical-basal polarity, specialised cell junctions, attachment to a basement membrane, and absence of blood vessels within the epithelium itself. Nutrients diffuse from underlying connective tissue. Classification uses the number of cell layers (simple, stratified, pseudostratified) and the shape of the surface cells (squamous, cuboidal, columnar). Examples include simple squamous endothelium lining blood vessels, simple cuboidal epithelium in many renal tubules, and stratified squamous epithelium in the epidermis and oesophagus (keratinised in skin; generally non-keratinised in the oesophagus).

Connective tissue contains cells dispersed within ECM. Fibroblasts synthesise collagen and other matrix components; adipocytes store lipid and also act as endocrine cells; macrophages and other immune cells contribute to defence and repair. Loose connective tissue supports and binds structures, dense regular connective tissue forms many tendons and ligaments, and dense irregular connective tissue resists stress from multiple directions, as in the reticular dermis. Cartilage is avascular and receives nutrients mainly by diffusion; articular cartilage has no perichondrium over its free joint surface. Bone is vascular, mineralised connective tissue remodelled by osteoblasts, osteoclasts and osteocytes.

Muscle tissue generates force. Skeletal muscle is striated and usually under voluntary control; cardiac muscle is striated, branched and electrically coupled through intercalated discs; smooth muscle is non-striated and regulates the calibre and movement of hollow organs and vessels. Nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glial cells; it receives, integrates and transmits information. Tissue identification in histology therefore depends on architecture and cell relationships, not on a single isolated cell.

4. Bone architecture and remodelling

Compact bone is organised into osteons in many load-bearing regions. Each osteon contains concentric lamellae around a central (Haversian) canal carrying vessels and nerves; perforating (Volkmann) canals connect vascular channels. Osteocytes occupy lacunae and communicate through canaliculi. Trabecular (cancellous) bone forms a lattice aligned broadly with habitual loading; marrow occupies spaces between trabeculae. The periosteum covers most external bone surfaces and contains an outer fibrous layer and an inner osteogenic layer; the endosteum lines internal surfaces.

Osteoblasts produce osteoid, an organic matrix rich in type I collagen, which subsequently mineralises. Osteocytes are mature osteoblast-lineage cells embedded in matrix; they sense mechanical strain and help regulate remodelling. Osteoclasts are multinucleated cells derived from the monocyte-macrophage lineage and resorb bone using acid and proteolytic enzymes. Remodelling repairs microdamage and adapts bone to mechanical and metabolic demands. Parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, sex steroids, mechanical loading and local RANK-RANKL-OPG signalling influence skeletal turnover. A fracture initiates inflammation and haematoma formation, recruitment of repair cells, soft callus formation, hard callus formation and prolonged remodelling; timing varies with age, vascularity, fracture pattern, stability, infection and systemic health.

5. Synovial joints: structure explains mobility and vulnerability

A typical synovial joint has articular surfaces covered by hyaline cartilage, a joint cavity containing a small volume of synovial fluid, and an articular capsule. The capsule has an outer fibrous layer and an inner synovial membrane, which does not cover the articular cartilage. Synovial fluid lubricates, helps distribute load and supports avascular articular cartilage through diffusion. Accessory structures vary by joint and may include menisci, articular discs, labra, fat pads, bursae, ligaments and tendon sheaths.

Joint classification can be structural (fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial) or functional (synarthrosis, amphiarthrosis, diarthrosis). Synovial joints are freely movable but differ in geometry and permitted movement: plane, hinge, pivot, condyloid, saddle and ball-and-socket. These labels describe typical mechanics, not an absolute guarantee that a joint moves in only one direction. Stability depends on articular congruence, capsule and ligament strength, muscle-tendon control, labral or meniscal structures where present, and neuromuscular coordination.

6. Limb anatomy: compartments, innervation and applied localisation

The upper limb is organised around mobility and skilled manipulation. The pectoral girdle comprises the clavicle and scapula; the humerus forms the arm; radius and ulna form the forearm; and the hand contains eight carpal bones, five metacarpals and fourteen phalanges. The brachial plexus is formed principally by anterior rami of C5-T1 spinal nerves, classically arranged as roots, trunks, divisions, cords and terminal branches. The major terminal nerves are musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median and ulnar. The axillary nerve supplies deltoid and teres minor and carries sensation from the superior-lateral arm; injury may follow shoulder dislocation or fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus. The radial nerve supplies extensor compartments; the median nerve supplies most forearm flexors and thenar muscles; the ulnar nerve supplies many intrinsic hand muscles.

The lower limb is adapted for weight transmission, balance and locomotion. The femur transmits load from hip to knee; the tibia is the principal weight-bearing bone of the leg, while the fibula contributes to ankle stability and provides muscle attachments. The femoral triangle contains the femoral nerve, artery and vein in a lateral-to-medial arrangement (nerve, artery, vein, then femoral canal/lymphatics); the femoral nerve is outside the femoral sheath. The sciatic nerve usually arises from L4-S3 and divides into tibial and common fibular components, often proximal to the popliteal fossa but with anatomical variation. The common fibular nerve is vulnerable near the fibular neck; injury can weaken dorsiflexion and eversion and produce foot drop.

Compartments are bounded by deep fascia, intermuscular septa and bone. They group muscles with related actions and neurovascular supply. In the leg, anterior compartment muscles dorsiflex the ankle and extend the toes and are supplied by the deep fibular nerve; lateral compartment muscles evert the foot and are supplied by the superficial fibular nerve; posterior compartment muscles plantarflex the ankle and flex the toes and are supplied by the tibial nerve. Acute compartment syndrome occurs when pressure rises within a closed fascial compartment, compromising tissue perfusion. Pain out of proportion to the apparent injury and pain on passive stretch are important early warning features; pulses may remain present and their presence does not exclude the diagnosis. This is a surgical emergency requiring immediate senior assessment and local emergency protocols.

7. Histology-to-clinic correlation: how to read a tissue slide

Before naming a tissue, identify the specimen orientation, stain, magnification and whether the section is longitudinal, transverse or oblique. At low power, assess overall architecture, boundaries, lumen, connective tissue framework and distribution of glands or vessels. At higher power, identify cell shape, nuclei, polarity, extracellular matrix and specialised structures. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) commonly stains nuclei blue-purple and much protein-rich cytoplasm and matrix pink; staining intensity varies with preparation. A single two-dimensional section can make a branching structure look isolated or make a longitudinally cut fibre appear different from a transverse one.

For epithelium, count layers and inspect the surface cell shape. For connective tissue, assess fibre density, orientation and cellularity. For skeletal muscle, look for long cylindrical fibres, peripheral nuclei and cross-striations when preservation permits. For cardiac muscle, look for branching fibres, central nuclei and intercalated discs (not always obvious in every section). Smooth muscle consists of spindle-shaped cells with central elongated nuclei and no visible striations on routine H&E. Bone may appear as compact lamellar osteons or trabeculae with marrow spaces. Cartilage shows chondrocytes in lacunae within a firm matrix; distinguish hyaline, elastic and fibrocartilage by matrix appearance and fibre composition, while remembering that routine stains and section quality affect visual cues.

8. Integrated clinical case: localising a peripheral nerve lesion

A 24-year-old patient sustains a fracture through the midshaft of the humerus. After initial stabilisation, examination identifies weak wrist and finger extension and reduced sensation in the dorsal first web space. The hand is warm and perfused. The findings suggest radial nerve dysfunction. The radial nerve runs in the radial (spiral) groove on the posterior humerus with the profunda brachii artery; a midshaft fracture can therefore place it at risk. Weakness of wrist extension may produce wrist drop. Sensory loss over the dorsal first web space is a useful autonomous-zone finding, although sensory territories overlap and examination must be interpreted with the full motor and sensory pattern.

Reasoning sequence: (1) define the deficit rather than naming a nerve immediately; (2) distinguish motor from sensory findings; (3) map the affected muscles and cutaneous territory; (4) identify the nerve carrying those functions; (5) trace the nerve's anatomical course relative to the injury; and (6) consider alternative or additional injuries, including vascular injury and more proximal plexus or root lesions. A real patient requires documented neurovascular examination before and after reduction or immobilisation, appropriate imaging, and management by the treating clinical team.

9. Applied learning questions with marking guidance

  1. Explain why anatomical position is necessary when describing a patient who is lying supine. (4 marks)
    Expected points: provides a fixed reference independent of posture (1); right/left refer to the patient's sides (1); directional terms retain consistent meanings (1); reduces ambiguity in clinical communication and imaging interpretation (1).
  2. Differentiate sagittal, frontal and transverse planes and give one clinical application of each. (6 marks)
    Expected points: sagittal divides right/left (1) and example such as midsagittal brain MRI (1); frontal divides anterior/posterior (1) and example such as coronal sinus or knee imaging (1); transverse divides superior/inferior (1) and example such as axial CT abdomen (1).
  3. Compare hyaline cartilage, elastic cartilage and fibrocartilage by matrix and typical location. (6 marks)
    Expected points: hyaline has type II collagen-rich glassy matrix; articular surfaces, costal cartilage or tracheal rings (2); elastic cartilage has abundant elastic fibres; auricle or epiglottis (2); fibrocartilage has dense type I collagen bundles and resists compression/tension; intervertebral discs or pubic symphysis (2).
  4. Describe the cellular roles in bone remodelling. (6 marks)
    Expected points: osteoblasts synthesise osteoid and support mineralisation (2); osteoclasts resorb mineralised bone (2); osteocytes sense mechanical environment and coordinate remodelling (2).
  5. Outline the structure of a typical synovial joint and explain two mechanisms that support stability. (6 marks)
    Expected points: articular cartilage, cavity with synovial fluid, capsule with fibrous and synovial layers (4); stability from ligament/capsule restraint, congruent surfaces/labrum, and dynamic muscle-tendon control (any two, 1 each).
  6. A patient develops foot drop after trauma near the fibular neck. Identify the likely nerve and explain the anatomical basis. (5 marks)
    Expected points: common fibular (peroneal) nerve (1); winds around fibular neck and is vulnerable there (1); deep fibular branch supplies anterior compartment dorsiflexors (1); loss of dorsiflexion causes foot drop (1); associated eversion weakness/sensory change over dorsum may occur depending on lesion level (1).
  7. Describe a systematic approach to identifying an unknown H&E tissue section. (5 marks)
    Expected points: confirm stain/magnification/orientation (1); assess low-power architecture (1); examine cell shape and layers (1); inspect nuclei and extracellular matrix (1); correlate features with tissue function and avoid diagnosis from one feature alone (1).
  8. Explain why intact distal pulses do not exclude acute compartment syndrome. (4 marks)
    Expected points: compartment pressure first compromises microvascular perfusion (1); arterial pressure may remain sufficient to maintain palpable large-vessel pulses (1); pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch are important early findings (1); urgent assessment is required and pulses should not reassure falsely (1).
