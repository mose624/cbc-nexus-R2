Unit overview

Optics studies the propagation and interaction of light. Geometrical optics models light using rays and is effective when optical dimensions are large compared with wavelength. Wave optics is required to explain interference, diffraction and polarisation. A complete first course connects ray construction with phase, coherence and measurable intensity.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Core textbook content

Wave optics is required to explain interference, diffraction and polarisation. A complete first course connects ray construction with phase, coherence and measurable intensity. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Nature of light and optical models

Light is an electromagnetic wave and, in quantum descriptions, energy is exchanged in photons. In a homogeneous isotropic medium, geometrical rays indicate the direction of energy propagation and are perpendicular to wavefronts. The vacuum speed is c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; in a transparent medium phase velocity is v = c/n, where n is refractive index. The ray approximation is useful when wavelength is much smaller than apertures, obstacles and curvature scales. Wave optics becomes essential near edges, narrow slits and coherent superposition regions.

Worked example / application. For glass with n = 1.50, phase speed v = c/n = 2.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. The frequency remains unchanged at a stationary boundary; wavelength decreases to λ₀/n.

2. Reflection and plane mirrors

The law of reflection states that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, with both angles measured from the normal. Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane. A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image of equal size, located the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. Lateral inversion is a relation between object and image coordinates, not a physical left-right reversal of the object. Multiple reflections between mirrors can create repeated images.

Worked example / application. An object is 1.2 m in front of a plane mirror. Its image is 1.2 m behind the mirror, so object-image separation is 2.4 m. A person moving 0.3 m toward the mirror reduces the separation by 0.6 m.

3. Refraction and Snell's law

At a boundary between media, the tangential component of wavevector is conserved for a stationary planar interface. This leads to Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Angles are measured from the normal. Entering a higher-index medium bends the ray toward the normal; entering a lower-index medium bends it away. Frequency is conserved while speed and wavelength change. Refraction underlies lenses, prisms, optical fibres and atmospheric mirages.

Worked example / application. Light enters glass n₂=1.50 from air at θ₁=30°. sin θ₂ = sin30°/1.50 = 1/3, so θ₂ ≈ 19.5°.

4. Total internal reflection and optical fibres

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and incidence angle exceeds the critical angle θc, where sin θc = n₂/n₁. Below the critical angle, part of the light transmits; above it, an evanescent field exists in the lower-index medium and ideal ray optics predicts complete reflection. Fibres guide light through a high-index core surrounded by lower-index cladding. Numerical aperture describes the acceptance of rays from an external medium and is linked to the indices of core and cladding.

Worked example / application. For core n₁=1.48 and cladding n₂=1.46, sin θc=1.46/1.48=0.9865, giving θc≈80.6°. The small index difference still confines rays incident above this internal critical angle.

5. Spherical mirrors and image formation

Concave and convex spherical mirrors obey the paraxial mirror equation 1/f = 1/u + 1/v under a consistent sign convention. For a spherical mirror of radius R, focal length magnitude is R/2. Ray diagrams use principal rays: parallel rays reflect through the focus for a concave mirror; a ray through the centre of curvature retraces its path. Magnification relates image and object heights and carries sign information about orientation. Paraxial assumptions fail for large apertures and strongly off-axis rays, producing spherical aberration and coma.

Worked example / application. A concave mirror with f=15 cm has an object at u=30 cm. Using 1/v=1/f−1/u=1/15−1/30=1/30, the image is 30 cm from the mirror, real and inverted with unit magnitude magnification.

6. Thin lenses and lens combinations

A thin lens in air has focal length determined by curvature and refractive index through the lensmaker relation 1/f=(n−1)(1/R₁−1/R₂), with radii defined using a consistent sign convention. The thin-lens equation is 1/f=1/u+1/v under the common real-is-positive convention. Converging lenses can form real or virtual images depending on object distance; diverging lenses form virtual upright images for real objects. For thin lenses in contact, optical powers add: P_total=P₁+P₂, with P=1/f in metres and unit dioptre.

Worked example / application. A converging lens of f=10 cm has an object 15 cm away. 1/v=1/10−1/15=1/30, so v=30 cm. Magnification m=−v/u=−2: the image is real, inverted and twice the object's height.

7. Prisms and dispersion

The refractive index of many transparent materials depends on wavelength. This dispersion causes different colours to refract by different amounts in a prism. For a prism of angle A at minimum deviation δm, the path is symmetric and n = sin[(A+δm)/2]/sin(A/2). Dispersion is important in spectrometers and optical design, but it can also cause chromatic aberration in lenses. Achromatic doublets combine glasses with different dispersive properties to reduce colour-dependent focal shift.

Worked example / application. For a prism A=60° and minimum deviation δm=40°, n=sin50°/sin30°≈1.532. The measurement assumes the minimum-deviation condition has been established.

8. Interference and coherence

When coherent waves overlap, their electric fields superpose. For two monochromatic waves with phase difference δ, intensity is I=I₁+I₂+2√(I₁I₂)cosδ. Constructive interference occurs for path difference mλ; destructive interference for (m+1/2)λ when amplitudes are equal. Coherence requires a stable phase relationship over the relevant time and distance. Fringe visibility depends on coherence, intensity balance and experimental stability. Interference is a direct demonstration that light cannot always be treated as independent rays.

Worked example / application. In Young's double-slit experiment, slit separation d=0.25 mm, screen distance D=2.0 m and wavelength λ=500 nm. Fringe spacing β=λD/d=4.0 mm.

9. Diffraction and resolving power

Diffraction is the spreading and redistribution of waves caused by finite apertures or obstacles. For a single slit of width a, minima satisfy a sinθ=mλ for non-zero integer m. A circular aperture produces an Airy pattern; the Rayleigh criterion gives angular resolution θ≈1.22λ/D for aperture diameter D. Diffraction limits the detail that microscopes, telescopes and cameras can resolve. The Fourier transform of an aperture describes its far-field diffraction pattern, linking optics to mathematical methods.

Worked example / application. A telescope objective of diameter 0.10 m observes wavelength 550 nm. θ≈1.22(550×10⁻⁹)/0.10=6.71×10⁻⁶ rad, the approximate Rayleigh angular separation.

10. Polarisation

Polarisation describes the orientation and evolution of the electric-field oscillation. Linear, circular and elliptical states are distinguished by the relative amplitudes and phase of orthogonal components. An ideal polariser transmits the component along its transmission axis. Malus's law gives I=I₀cos²θ for linearly polarised incident light and analyser angle θ. Unpolarised light passing an ideal polariser has mean transmitted intensity I₀/2. Polarisation is used in stress analysis, displays, glare reduction and optical communication.

Worked example / application. A linearly polarised beam of intensity 8.0 W m⁻² passes an analyser at 30°. I=8 cos²30°=6.0 W m⁻².

11. Optical instruments: microscope and telescope

A compound microscope uses an objective to create an enlarged real intermediate image and an eyepiece to magnify that image. A telescope collects light with an objective and presents an angularly magnified image through an eyepiece. Magnification must be considered alongside numerical aperture, field of view, aberrations, diffraction and brightness. The unaided eye has finite accommodation and resolution. Instrument performance is not captured by magnification alone: empty magnification enlarges a blurred image without adding detail.

Worked example / application. For an astronomical telescope in normal adjustment, objective focal length 100 cm and eyepiece focal length 5 cm give angular magnification magnitude M=f_o/f_e=20.

12. Optical experiments and uncertainty

A lens experiment should identify principal planes, object and image positions, alignment uncertainty and the sign convention used. Avoid parallax by aligning the eye with the scale and image marker. In a spectrometer, collimate the slit, focus the telescope, establish vernier readings and take repeated angular observations. Record wavelength, temperature where relevant, slit width and apparatus geometry. Plot transformed data only when the transformation follows from a model and account for uncertainty in both axes where significant.

Worked example / application. For a lens, plotting 1/v against 1/u can test the thin-lens relation but both coordinates contain measurement uncertainty. An alternative is to fit v as a function of u using the nonlinear equation and compare residuals.

13. Integrated optical experiment: determine wavelength

Young's double-slit arrangement provides λ=βd/D. Measure fringe spacing over several fringes rather than one adjacent pair to reduce relative position error. Measure slit separation independently, determine screen distance from the slit plane, and keep the apparatus aligned. Propagate independent uncertainties using (uλ/λ)²=(uβ/β)²+(ud/d)²+(uD/D)². Discuss finite slit width, imperfect coherence, background illumination, fringe-centre judgement and mechanical vibration.

Worked example / application. If β=3.20±0.05 mm, d=0.300±0.005 mm and D=1.80±0.01 m, λ=βd/D=5.33×10⁻⁷ m. Relative standard uncertainty is about 2.2%, so uλ≈1.2×10⁻⁸ m.

1. The nature of light and the scope of geometrical optics

Optics is the study of light, its production, propagation, interaction with matter and detection. Light exhibits wave behaviour, including interference, diffraction and polarisation, while in many everyday imaging systems its propagation can be modelled by rays. Geometrical optics is the approximation in which the wavelength is much smaller than the characteristic dimensions of apertures, lenses, mirrors and objects. Under this approximation, wavefronts may be represented by rays normal to their surfaces. Rays indicate the direction of energy transport in a homogeneous isotropic medium.

The vacuum speed of light is c=299 792 458 m s⁻¹ exactly in the SI system. In a transparent medium, the phase velocity is v=c/n, where n is the refractive index. For ordinary transparent materials n is greater than one. The refractive index can depend on wavelength, temperature, pressure, composition and propagation direction. A quoted refractive index is therefore incomplete unless its wavelength and conditions are stated. In dispersive media, different wavelengths travel with different phase velocities.

A ray model is useful when diffraction spreading is negligible and the optical system is not operating close to a scale comparable with the wavelength. It is appropriate for tracing images in lenses, mirrors, telescopes and many instruments. It is not sufficient to explain the finite resolution of an aperture, interference fringes, diffraction patterns or polarisation effects. Those require a wave description, developed in more advanced optics.

Optical systems are often described by object space and image space. An object point emits or scatters a bundle of rays. If the outgoing rays actually meet, the image is real and can be formed on a screen. If the rays diverge but their backward extensions meet, the image is virtual and cannot be projected onto a screen. A real image is not necessarily upright, and a virtual image is not necessarily magnified; these properties depend on the optical arrangement.

Optical axis is the reference line through the centres of curvature or optical centres of ideal centred components. Paraxial rays are close to the axis and make small angles with it. The paraxial approximation permits sinθ≈tanθ≈θ when θ is expressed in radians. This approximation simplifies derivations but fails for large ray angles, where spherical aberration and other departures from ideal imaging become important.

Worked example — wavelength in glass.

Light of vacuum wavelength 600 nm enters a glass with refractive index 1.50. Find its speed and wavelength in the glass.

Solution. v=c/n=(2.9979×10⁸)/1.50=2.00×10⁸ m s⁻¹. Frequency is unchanged at the boundary, so λ=λ₀/n=600/1.50=400 nm. The frequency is approximately 5.00×10¹⁴ Hz.

2. Reflection at plane and curved surfaces

The law of reflection states that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, with both angles measured from the normal to the surface. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane. Reflection from a smooth surface is specular: parallel incident rays remain parallel after reflection. A rough surface produces diffuse reflection because local surface normals vary; the law still applies locally.

A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. The image has the same size as the object and is laterally reversed in the familiar sense that left and right appear exchanged when the observer compares themselves with the image. The mirror does not physically swap coordinates; it reverses the component perpendicular to its plane.

Spherical mirrors are sections of spherical surfaces. A concave mirror converges paraxial parallel rays toward a real principal focus; a convex mirror diverges them as if from a virtual focus. The radius of curvature R and focal length f are related by f=R/2 for paraxial rays. This result follows from the geometry of a ray parallel to the principal axis reflecting at a point close to the vertex: the normal is approximately directed toward the centre of curvature, and the small-angle geometry gives equal angular deviations on either side.

Using the Cartesian sign convention adopted on this page, distances are measured from the mirror pole and the positive direction is the direction of incident light unless the diagram or formula convention states otherwise. Because textbooks use different sign conventions, always state the convention before substituting. A reliable method is to draw the object, mirror, principal axis, focus and image, then decide whether the image is real or virtual before assigning signs.

The paraxial mirror equation is 1/f=1/u+1/v when u and v are taken as positive magnitudes for the standard real-object arrangement and image sign is interpreted consistently. Magnification is m=hᵢ/hₒ=−v/u under the common Cartesian convention. The minus sign indicates inversion for a real image. Do not mix this convention with one in which all distances are positive magnitudes.

Worked example — concave mirror.

A concave mirror has focal length 15 cm. An object is 45 cm in front of it. Find image distance and magnification using positive real-object and real-image magnitudes.

Solution. 1/v=1/f−1/u=1/15−1/45=2/45 cm⁻¹, so v=22.5 cm. Magnification m=−v/u=−22.5/45=−0.50. The image is real, inverted and half the object's height.

3. Refraction and derivation of Snell's law

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave as it crosses a boundary at which its phase velocity changes. The frequency remains constant at a stationary boundary because the oscillation must remain continuous in time. Since v=fλ, the wavelength changes when the speed changes. A ray entering a medium of higher refractive index bends toward the normal; entering a lower-index medium, it bends away from the normal.

Snell's law is n₁ sinθ₁=n₂ sinθ₂. One derivation uses Huygens' construction. Let a plane wavefront reach a boundary first at point A. During a time Δt, a point B on the incident wavefront travels distance v₁Δt to the boundary, while a secondary wavelet from A in medium 2 expands a distance v₂Δt. The geometry of the two right triangles gives sinθ₁=v₁Δt/AB and sinθ₂=v₂Δt/AC with the common wavefront geometry; eliminating the shared distance and using n=c/v gives sinθ₁/sinθ₂=v₁/v₂=n₂/n₁, hence n₁sinθ₁=n₂sinθ₂.

The same law follows from Fermat's principle: the actual light path between two points makes the travel time stationary with respect to small path variations. For a boundary crossing at a variable point x, the travel time is t=(√(a²+x²))/v₁+(√(b²+(D−x)²))/v₂. Setting dt/dx=0 gives sinθ₁/v₁=sinθ₂/v₂, which is equivalent to Snell's law. The stationary path is generally the least-time path for simple planar arrangements, but the more general statement is stationary optical travel time.

At normal incidence θ₁=0 and the ray does not change direction, although its speed and wavelength change. At oblique incidence, the bending depends on the ratio of refractive indices and incident angle. Refraction is reversible: a ray that travels from point A to B along a permitted path can travel from B to A along the same path in the reverse direction.

Worked example — refraction into glass.

A ray travels from air (n₁=1.00) into glass (n₂=1.50) at 40°. Find the refracted angle.

Solution. sinθ₂=(1.00/1.50)sin40°=0.4285. Thus θ₂=arcsin(0.4285)=25.4°. The ray bends toward the normal because it enters the higher-index medium.

4. Critical angle and total internal reflection

When light travels from a medium of higher refractive index n₁ to one of lower refractive index n₂, the refracted ray bends away from the normal. As the incidence angle increases, the refracted angle approaches 90°. The incidence angle for which θ₂=90° is the critical angle θc. From Snell's law, n₁sinθc=n₂, so sinθc=n₂/n₁ and θc=arcsin(n₂/n₁), provided n₁>n₂.

For incidence greater than the critical angle, no propagating refracted ray exists in the second medium in the ideal ray model. The incident energy is reflected back into the first medium: total internal reflection. The phenomenon requires light to approach the boundary from the optically denser medium and the incidence angle to exceed the critical angle. It does not occur for light travelling from a lower refractive index into a higher one.

Total internal reflection is used in optical fibres, prisms, endoscopes and light guides. In a step-index fibre, the core has refractive index n₁ and the cladding has lower index n₂. Rays within the acceptance cone undergo repeated total internal reflection at the core-cladding interface. Real fibres also have attenuation, bending losses, scattering and dispersion; the simple ray picture describes guidance but not every transmission limitation.

Worked example — critical angle.

For a glass-air interface with n_glass=1.50 and n_air=1.00, calculate θc.

Solution. sinθc=1.00/1.50=0.6667, so θc=41.8°. Incidence inside the glass above 41.8° produces total internal reflection in the ideal interface model.

5. Optical fibres, numerical aperture and acceptance angle

An optical fibre consists of a transparent core surrounded by cladding and a protective jacket. Light is guided because the core refractive index n₁ exceeds the cladding index n₂. For a meridional ray entering from an external medium of index n₀, the maximum entrance angle is the acceptance half-angle θa. Applying Snell's law at the input face and the critical-angle condition at the core-cladding boundary gives n₀sinθa=√(n₁²−n₂²). The numerical aperture is NA=n₀sinθa; for air n₀≈1, NA≈sinθa.

The derivation assumes a step-index fibre, meridional rays, a planar entrance face and ideal interfaces. Define the internal ray angle relative to the fibre axis as r. At the sidewall, the incidence angle to the normal is 90°−r. Total internal reflection requires 90°−r≥θc. Thus sin r≤cosθc=√(1−sin²θc)=√(1−n₂²/n₁²). At the input face, n₀sinθa=n₁sin r. Substitution yields n₀sinθa≤√(n₁²−n₂²), which gives the numerical aperture.

Fibres are classified by refractive-index profile and supported modes. Step-index fibres have an abrupt core-cladding index change; graded-index fibres have a core index that decreases gradually with radius. Single-mode fibres support one spatial mode at the operating wavelength and are used for long-distance, high-bandwidth communication. Multimode fibres support many modes and can suffer modal dispersion because different modes take different path lengths.

Attenuation is often expressed in decibels per unit length: α=(10/L)log₁₀(P_in/P_out) dB per unit length when powers are used. Connector losses, bends, scattering and absorption reduce transmitted power. Dispersion broadens pulses and limits data rate. The optical power budget must allow for fibre attenuation, splices, connectors and safety margin.

Worked example — numerical aperture.

A step-index fibre has n₁=1.48 and n₂=1.46. Calculate NA and acceptance half-angle in air.

Solution. NA=√(1.48²−1.46²)=√(2.1904−2.1316)=√0.0588=0.2425. Therefore θa=arcsin(0.2425)=14.0°. This is the ideal meridional-ray acceptance half-angle.

6. Thin lenses and derivation of the lens equation

A converging lens is thicker at the centre than at the edges in its common form and brings paraxial parallel rays toward a real principal focus. A diverging lens is thinner at the centre and causes parallel rays to diverge as if from a virtual focus. The optical centre is the point through which a paraxial ray passes approximately undeviated for a thin lens in air. The principal axis passes through the centres of curvature of the two lens surfaces.

The thin-lens equation can be derived by similar triangles. For a thin converging lens, let object distance be u, image distance v and focal length f, with real distances treated as positive magnitudes for the standard real-object construction. The ray through the optical centre is approximately undeviated. Similar triangles give hᵢ/hₒ=v/u in magnitude. A ray parallel to the axis emerges through the focus; the geometry of the focal triangle gives hᵢ/hₒ=(v−f)/f. Equating the ratios gives v/u=(v−f)/f. Rearranging: vf=u(v−f), so uv=uf+vf, and dividing by uvf gives 1/f=1/u+1/v.

With the common Cartesian convention, object distance is negative for a real object to the left of the lens, image distance is positive for a real image to the right, and focal length is positive for a converging lens. The algebraic form is 1/f=1/v−1/u under that convention. This is the same physical equation written with signed distances. A diverging lens has negative focal length. Select one convention and use it consistently through the calculation.

Linear magnification is m=hᵢ/hₒ. In signed Cartesian form m=v/u, with sign indicating orientation. For a real image formed by a single converging lens from a real object, m is negative and the image is inverted. A virtual image is upright and has positive magnification in the same convention. Magnification is dimensionless because it is a ratio of lengths.

Worked example — converging lens.

A lens of focal length 10.0 cm forms an image of an object placed 15.0 cm from it. Find the image distance and magnification.

Solution. 1/v=1/f−1/u=1/10−1/15=1/30 cm⁻¹, hence v=30.0 cm. m=−v/u=−2.00 using positive real-distance magnitudes. The image is real, inverted and twice the object height.

7. Lens maker's equation and optical power

The focal length of a thin lens depends on its refractive index and surface curvatures. For a lens in air, the lens maker's equation is 1/f=(n−1)(1/R₁−1/R₂), using a stated radius sign convention. For a lens in a surrounding medium of refractive index n₀, replace the relative index factor by (n/n₀−1). The equation assumes a thin lens, spherical surfaces and paraxial rays.

One route to the lens maker equation is to apply refraction at each spherical surface. For the first surface, n₁/u+n₂/v=(n₂−n₁)/R under a consistent Cartesian convention. At the second surface, apply the same relation with the intermediate image acting as the object for the second interface. In a thin lens, the separation is negligible compared with object and image distances, so the intermediate image distance approximately cancels when the two equations are combined. For a lens in air, the resulting equivalent power is (n−1)(1/R₁−1/R₂).

Optical power P is the reciprocal of focal length in metres: P=1/f, measured in dioptres (D). A converging lens has positive power and a diverging lens negative power. For thin lenses in contact, powers add: P_total=P₁+P₂. For separated lenses, the equivalent focal length also depends on separation. A spectacle prescription is often expressed in dioptres, but practical prescriptions may include cylinder and axis terms to correct astigmatism.

Worked example — lens maker equation.

A glass lens has n=1.60, R₁=+0.20 m and R₂=−0.20 m under the stated convention. Find f and power.

Solution. 1/f=(1.60−1)[1/0.20−1/(−0.20)]=0.60(5+5)=6.0 m⁻¹. Thus f=0.1667 m and P=+6.0 D. The positive power identifies a converging lens.

8. Combinations of lenses and compound optical systems

Two thin lenses in contact have negligible separation, so the image formed by the first acts as the object for the second at essentially the same plane. Their powers add. For lenses separated by distance d in air, the equivalent power is P=P₁+P₂−dP₁P₂, where d is in metres and powers are in dioptres. This follows by applying the thin-lens equation sequentially and eliminating the intermediate image position. The separation term has units of dioptres because metres multiplied by dioptres squared gives dioptres.

Compound systems are analysed by tracing an image through each element. The image from one lens becomes the object for the next. An intermediate image can be real or virtual; a virtual intermediate object for the next lens is not an error. Draw a scale diagram and maintain a consistent coordinate convention. For thick lenses, principal planes replace the simple thin-lens optical centre and must be considered in accurate calculations.

Worked example — separated lenses.

Two thin lenses have powers +5.0 D and −2.0 D and are separated by 0.10 m in air. Find equivalent power and focal length.

Solution. P=5−2−(0.10)(5)(−2)=3+1=4.0 D. Hence f=1/4.0=0.25 m. The equivalent system is converging.

9. Aberrations and the limits of paraxial imaging

Aberration is a departure from ideal image formation. Spherical aberration occurs because rays far from the optical axis focus at a different axial position from paraxial rays. It can be reduced by stopping down the aperture, choosing suitable surface shapes or combining elements. Coma produces comet-like images of off-axis points. Astigmatism causes tangential and sagittal ray bundles to focus at different positions. Field curvature means a flat object may focus best on a curved image surface.

Distortion changes image geometry rather than necessarily blurring a point: barrel distortion makes straight lines bow outward, while pincushion distortion makes them bow inward. Chromatic aberration arises from dispersion: refractive index varies with wavelength, so different colours have different focal lengths. Longitudinal chromatic aberration separates colour foci along the axis; lateral chromatic aberration produces wavelength-dependent image magnification.

An achromatic doublet combines glasses with different dispersions to bring two selected wavelengths to a common focus. A simple achromat does not eliminate all chromatic aberration at every wavelength. Reflecting telescopes avoid the refractive chromatic aberration of a single objective lens, although mirrors have their own aberrations and alignment requirements.

The aperture stop controls the cone of rays and influences brightness, depth of field and aberrations. The field stop limits the field of view. Vignetting is a reduction in illumination toward the edge of the image, often because optical components restrict off-axis ray bundles. These effects are central to real camera lenses, microscopes and telescopes.

Worked example — chromatic focal shift.

A lens has focal lengths 100.0 mm for blue light and 102.0 mm for red light. Calculate the longitudinal chromatic separation and its fractional value relative to the mean focal length.

Solution. Separation=2.0 mm. Mean focal length=101.0 mm. Fractional separation=2/101=0.0198, or 1.98%. This simple comparison quantifies the focal shift between the two stated wavelengths.

10. Prisms, minimum deviation and dispersion

A prism is a transparent optical element bounded by two plane refracting surfaces inclined at an apex angle A. A ray is refracted at the first face, travels through the prism and is refracted again at the second face. The total deviation δ is the angle between the original incident direction and emergent direction. At minimum deviation, the path is symmetric: the internal refraction angles are equal and the incidence and emergence angles are equal.

At minimum deviation, r₁=r₂=A/2 and i=e=(A+δ_min)/2. Applying Snell's law at the first face gives n=sin i/sin r for a prism in air. Therefore n=sin[(A+δ_min)/2]/sin(A/2). This equation allows refractive index to be measured from the prism angle and minimum deviation. The prism must be aligned carefully and the minimum found by rotating through the turning point of the emergent ray.

Dispersion is the dependence of refractive index on wavelength. In normal dispersion, refractive index decreases as wavelength increases across much of the visible range. White light is therefore separated into colours by a prism. The angular dispersion depends on the wavelength derivative dn/dλ and prism geometry. Near absorption bands, anomalous dispersion can occur and the simple normal trend may reverse.

Worked example — prism refractive index.

A prism has apex angle A=60° and minimum deviation δmin=40°. Calculate n in air.

Solution. n=sin[(60+40)/2]/sin30°=sin50°/0.5=1.532. The result assumes the prism is measured at the minimum-deviation condition and the surrounding medium is air.

11. The human eye and visual correction

The eye is an optical system in which the cornea provides much of the refracting power and the crystalline lens adjusts focus. The iris controls pupil diameter, the retina detects light and the ciliary muscles alter lens curvature during accommodation. A relaxed eye focuses distant objects on the retina. For near objects, accommodation increases the optical power so that the image remains on the retina.

Myopia (short-sightedness) occurs when distant objects focus in front of the retina under relaxed accommodation. A diverging lens reduces the effective convergence of incoming rays. Hyperopia (long-sightedness) occurs when the eye's optical power is insufficient or the eye is relatively short, so a converging correction is used. Astigmatism arises from unequal curvature in different meridians and is corrected using cylindrical or toric lens power with a specified axis.

The near point is the closest distance at which an object can be seen clearly with maximum accommodation. The far point is the farthest point seen clearly with the eye relaxed. The conventional least distance of distinct vision is often taken as approximately 25 cm for a standardised young adult eye, but it varies between people and with age. Presbyopia is the age-related reduction in accommodation and is commonly corrected with additional converging power for near tasks.

Worked example — correcting myopia.

An eye has a far point of 2.0 m. Find the approximate power of a spectacle lens that allows distant objects to form a virtual image at the far point, with the lens close to the eye.

Solution. For an object at infinity, the lens must form a virtual image at −2.0 m. Thus f≈−2.0 m and P=1/f=−0.50 D. Vertex distance and the detailed eye model are neglected.

12. Simple magnifiers and angular magnification

A simple magnifier is a converging lens used to view an object placed within its focal length. It produces an upright, virtual, magnified image. The relevant magnification is angular magnification, the ratio of the angle subtended at the eye with the instrument to the angle subtended by the unaided object at the conventional near point. It is not simply the linear image-to-object size ratio.

For a relaxed eye with the final image at infinity, the object is at the focal plane. If the near point is D and focal length is f, the unaided angle is approximately h/D and the magnified angle is h/f. Therefore M=D/f. If the final virtual image is at the near point, the angular magnification is approximately 1+D/f. These expressions use small-angle approximations and a conventional near-point distance D.

Worked example — magnifying glass.

A magnifier has focal length 5.0 cm. Estimate its angular magnification for a relaxed eye, taking D=25 cm.

Solution. M=D/f=25/5=5. The magnifier provides approximately five times the angular size compared with unaided viewing at the near point.

13. Compound microscope: image formation and magnifying power

A compound microscope uses an objective lens of short focal length to form a real, inverted, enlarged intermediate image. An eyepiece acts as a magnifier for this intermediate image. The objective is placed close to the specimen, while the eyepiece is positioned so that the intermediate image lies near its focal plane. The tube length and focal lengths determine the magnifying power.

For a final image at infinity, the intermediate image is approximately at the eyepiece focal plane. If the objective produces linear magnification mₒ≈L/fₒ for tube length L much greater than fₒ, and the eyepiece angular magnification is Mₑ≈D/fₑ, the total magnitude is M≈(L/fₒ)(D/fₑ). This is an approximation; accurate design uses principal planes, conjugate distances and the specified reference planes.

The microscope's resolving ability depends on wavelength, numerical aperture, illumination and coherence. Increasing magnification without improving resolution produces empty magnification: the image becomes larger but no additional detail is resolved. Numerical aperture is NA=n sinα, where α is the half-angle of the light cone accepted by the objective and n is the refractive index of the medium between specimen and objective.

Worked example — microscope magnification.

An objective has focal length 4.0 mm, the effective tube length is 160 mm and the eyepiece focal length is 25 mm. Estimate magnification for relaxed viewing using D=250 mm.

Solution. Objective magnitude≈160/4=40. Eyepiece angular magnification≈250/25=10. Total magnitude≈400. The image is inverted relative to the original specimen and the estimate neglects detailed principal-plane corrections.

14. Astronomical telescope and angular magnification

An astronomical refracting telescope uses an objective lens of long focal length fₒ and an eyepiece of shorter focal length fₑ. The objective forms a real, inverted image of a distant object near its focal plane. The eyepiece is positioned so that this image lies at its own focal plane, producing parallel emergent rays for relaxed viewing. The tube length is approximately fₒ+fₑ.

For a distant object subtending a small angle α, the objective image height is h≈fₒα. At the eyepiece, the emergent angle β≈h/fₑ. Therefore angular magnification M=β/α≈−fₒ/fₑ, where the negative sign indicates image inversion. The magnitude is fₒ/fₑ. A terrestrial telescope may include additional optics to erect the image, usually increasing length or complexity.

A reflecting telescope uses a concave primary mirror to collect light. Reflectors can be made with large apertures without the chromatic aberration of a refracting objective. Important practical issues include mirror figure, alignment, central obstruction, support deformation, thermal equilibrium and atmospheric seeing. The diffraction-limited angular resolution of an ideal circular aperture is approximately 1.22λ/D radians, where D is aperture diameter; atmospheric turbulence can make actual ground-based resolution worse.

Worked example — telescope.

An astronomical telescope has objective focal length 1.20 m and eyepiece focal length 0.040 m. Find the angular magnification and relaxed-eye tube length.

Solution. M=−1.20/0.040=−30, so magnitude is 30 and the image is inverted. Tube length≈1.20+0.040=1.24 m.

15. Laboratory experiment: determine the focal length of a converging lens

Mount an illuminated object, lens and screen on an optical bench. Align their centres at the same height and keep the principal axis horizontal. For a sharp real image, adjust the lens or screen and record object distance u and image distance v from the lens reference plane. Repeat for several object positions. Use the lens equation 1/f=1/u+1/v to calculate f for each pair, or plot 1/v against 1/u. The intercept and gradient can be used to assess consistency with the model.

For the graph, let y=1/v and x=1/u. The equation becomes y=−x+1/f. The expected gradient is −1 and intercept is 1/f. Uncertainties in u and v propagate nonlinearly into reciprocal values; a weighted fit should account for those uncertainties. An alternative is to measure u and v over a range and calculate f=uv/(u+v), then examine the spread and possible systematic trends.

Sources of uncertainty include the finite thickness of the lens, ambiguity in the optical centre, screen position, focus judgement, object width, optical bench scale and alignment. Use a small bright object and a screen with a clearly defined image. Avoid positions where u is very close to f because the image distance becomes large and highly sensitive to small changes. Repeat readings and state the criterion used for sharp focus.

Worked example — focal length from conjugates.

For u=30.0±0.2 cm and v=60.0±0.5 cm, calculate f and its approximate standard uncertainty assuming independent inputs.

Solution. f=uv/(u+v)=1800/90=20.0 cm. The derivatives are ∂f/∂u=v²/(u+v)²=3600/8100=0.4444 and ∂f/∂v=u²/(u+v)²=900/8100=0.1111. Thus u(f)=√[(0.4444×0.2)²+(0.1111×0.5)²]=0.097 cm. Report f=(20.00±0.10) cm as a standard uncertainty.

16. Laboratory experiment: refractive index using a spectrometer and prism

A spectrometer measures angular positions of a collimated beam. Focus the telescope on a distant slit image and adjust the collimator for parallel rays. Place the prism on the table with its refracting edge vertical. Measure reflected positions from the two prism faces to determine apex angle A. Then observe the refracted emergent ray while rotating the prism and identify the minimum-deviation turning point.

The prism angle can be determined from the angle between the two reflected telescope positions: the angle between reflected rays is 2A, so A is half their angular separation. At minimum deviation, measure the angle between the direct ray and emergent ray to obtain δmin. Calculate n=sin[(A+δmin)/2]/sin(A/2). Take repeated vernier readings and account for least count, backlash and uncertainty in locating the minimum.

Do not assume the vernier reading is exact to its smallest division. The slit width, focus, collimation, table levelling, prism alignment and operator judgement all affect the result. Record both vernier readings where the instrument has two scales and average only after applying the correct angular convention. Avoid parallax by focusing the cross-wires and slit image appropriately.

Worked example — spectrometer uncertainty idea.

Suppose A=60.0° and δmin=40.0°. Use n=1.532. Explain why repeating the angle readings is valuable.

Solution. Repetition estimates the variability in setting the telescope and reading the scales. The sensitivity to the angles follows differentiation of n=sin[(A+δ)/2]/sin(A/2); the uncertainties in A and δ should be propagated with derivatives and covariance if the same scale readings contribute to both.

17. Optical alignment, laser safety and laboratory practice

Optical benches should be stable, clean and free from unnecessary reflective objects. Align components at a common height and establish a reference axis. Secure lenses and mirrors to prevent movement. Use low-power alignment sources where possible. Never look directly into a laser beam or its specular reflection. A beam that appears dim may still be hazardous, particularly in the near-infrared where the eye cannot provide a reliable aversion response.

Laser controls depend on wavelength, output power, pulse duration, beam diameter and exposure conditions. Use a suitable enclosure or beam path, beam stops, warning signs, access control and wavelength-rated protective eyewear where the risk assessment requires it. Eyewear is not a substitute for engineering controls. Remove jewellery and tools that could create specular reflections. Keep the beam below or above eye level where appropriate and terminate it safely.

For bright lamps and ultraviolet sources, assess eye and skin exposure. Hot lamps can burn and may require cooling periods before handling. Electrical supplies for optical equipment require safe wiring and grounding. Use manufacturer instructions and institutional risk assessments; never improvise a high-power optical setup without supervision and approved controls.

18. Optical measurement, resolution and uncertainty

Optical measurements often infer a quantity from image position, angle, fringe spacing or detector output. The measured quantity may be a pixel coordinate, angular displacement or intensity, while the desired measurand is focal length, refractive index, wavelength or surface displacement. Write the measurement model explicitly and identify all inputs. Calibration of a camera scale, spectrometer angle or photodetector response can dominate the final uncertainty.

For a derived quantity y=f(x), use sensitivity coefficients ∂f/∂x. For a lens focal length f=uv/(u+v), the derivatives are ∂f/∂u=v²/(u+v)² and ∂f/∂v=u²/(u+v)². If u and v are independent, u²(f)=[v²/(u+v)²]²u²(u)+[u²/(u+v)²]²u²(v). This equation shows why a small uncertainty in a distance can have a different effect depending on the geometry.

For a refractive index determined from a prism, n=sin[(A+δ)/2]/sin(A/2). Differentiating with angles in radians gives ∂n/∂δ=cos[(A+δ)/2]/[2sin(A/2)] and ∂n/∂A={cos[(A+δ)/2]sin(A/2)−sin[(A+δ)/2]cos(A/2)}/[2sin²(A/2)]. The uncertainty is obtained from these coefficients and the angle uncertainties. If A and δ share readings, include covariance.

Resolution is not the same as optical resolving power. Instrument resolution concerns the smallest displayed or detectable change; optical resolution concerns the ability to distinguish two nearby object points. The Rayleigh criterion for a circular aperture gives θ_min≈1.22λ/D. For a microscope objective in a medium, a common lateral resolution estimate is d≈0.61λ/NA. These expressions are approximations and depend on illumination, coherence, detector sampling and the chosen resolution criterion.

Worked example — diffraction-limited angular resolution.

A telescope has aperture diameter 0.20 m and observes wavelength 550 nm. Estimate the Rayleigh angular resolution.

Solution. θ≈1.22λ/D=1.22(550×10⁻⁹)/0.20=3.36×10⁻⁶ rad. In arcseconds, multiply by 206265, giving approximately 0.69 arcsec under ideal diffraction-limited conditions.

19. Extended university-level worked problems

Problem 1 — apparent depth.

A coin lies at the bottom of a tank 1.20 m deep. The refractive index of water is 1.33. Estimate the apparent depth for near-normal viewing from air.

Derivation and solution. For small angles, Snell's law gives n_w sinθ_w=n_a sinθ_a. With sinθ≈tanθ, the lateral displacement at the surface is approximately h tanθ_w for the underwater ray and h_app tanθ_a for the back-projected ray. Equating these gives h_app/h≈tanθ_w/tanθ_a≈n_a/n_w. Hence h_app=1.20/1.33=0.902 m. The coin appears about 0.30 m shallower.

Problem 2 — two-lens image sequence.

A converging lens of focal length 12 cm is followed 40 cm later by a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. An object is 30 cm before the first lens. Find the final image position relative to the second lens.

Solution. First lens: 1/v₁=1/12−1/30=1/20, so v₁=20 cm to its right. The second lens is 40 cm to the right of the first, so its object is 20 cm to its left. For lens 2, 1/v₂=1/10−1/20=1/20, hence v₂=20 cm to the right of lens 2. The final image is real. The total signed transverse magnification is (−20/30)(−20/20)=+2/3, so it is upright relative to the original object and two-thirds its height.

Problem 3 — uncertainty in optical power.

A lens focal length is measured as 0.250±0.002 m. Find its optical power and standard uncertainty.

Solution. P=1/f=4.00 D. Differentiating gives dP/df=−1/f². Therefore u(P)=u(f)/f²=0.002/(0.250²)=0.032 D. Report P=(4.00±0.03) D as a standard uncertainty.

Problem 4 — critical angle with uncertainty.

A material has refractive index n=1.520±0.005 and is in air. Estimate its critical angle and uncertainty.

Solution. θc=arcsin(1/n)=41.14°. Differentiating gives dθc/dn=−1/[n√(n²−1)] radians per refractive-index unit. Its magnitude is approximately 0.568 rad. Thus u(θc)=0.568×0.005=0.00284 rad=0.163°. Result approximately (41.14±0.16)°.

Problem 5 — telescope objective diameter.

Estimate the minimum aperture needed for a diffraction-limited angular resolution of 1.0 arcsecond at 500 nm.

Solution. Convert 1 arcsecond to 1/206265 rad=4.848×10⁻⁶ rad. From θ=1.22λ/D, D=1.22λ/θ=1.22(500×10⁻⁹)/(4.848×10⁻⁶)=0.126 m. Atmospheric seeing and optical imperfections may require a larger aperture to realise this theoretical limit.

20. University examination questions with model answers

  1. State the conditions under which geometrical optics is valid.

    The wavelength must be small compared with relevant apertures and component dimensions, and diffraction effects must be negligible for the required accuracy. The ray approximation is then useful for image construction and optical design.

  2. Derive Snell's law using Fermat's principle.

    Write travel time as t=√(a²+x²)/v₁+√(b²+(D−x)²)/v₂. Set dt/dx=0. The resulting relation is sinθ₁/v₁=sinθ₂/v₂. Since n=c/v, n₁sinθ₁=n₂sinθ₂.

  3. Explain why frequency is unchanged at a stationary refracting boundary.

    The electromagnetic field oscillation must have a common time dependence on both sides of a stationary boundary. The frequency is therefore continuous; speed and wavelength change according to v=fλ.

  4. Derive the critical-angle expression.

    Set the refracted angle to 90° in n₁sinθ₁=n₂sinθ₂. Then n₁sinθc=n₂ and θc=arcsin(n₂/n₁), requiring n₁>n₂.

  5. Derive numerical aperture for a step-index fibre.

    At the core boundary, total internal reflection requires the internal ray angle r to satisfy sin r≤√(1−n₂²/n₁²). At the input face, n₀sinθa=n₁sin r. Combining gives NA=n₀sinθa=√(n₁²−n₂²).

  6. Derive the thin-lens equation using similar triangles.

    The undeviated central ray gives |m|=v/u. The parallel incident ray through the focal point gives |m|=(v−f)/f. Equating and rearranging gives uv=uf+vf, hence 1/f=1/u+1/v for positive real-distance magnitudes.

  7. Explain the sign of magnification for real and virtual images.

    Under a consistent Cartesian convention, a negative transverse magnification indicates inversion and a positive value indicates an upright image. Real images from a single converging lens with a real object are inverted; virtual images are upright.

  8. Derive the lens maker's equation and state its assumptions.

    Apply the spherical refraction equation at each lens surface and eliminate the intermediate image distance. In the thin-lens limit the surface powers add, giving 1/f=(n−1)(1/R₁−1/R₂) for a lens in air. It assumes paraxial rays and spherical surfaces.

  9. What is chromatic aberration and how can it be reduced?

    Dispersion makes refractive index and focal length wavelength-dependent. An achromatic doublet uses glasses of different dispersions to bring two selected wavelengths to a common focus. It does not eliminate all chromatic error.

  10. Derive the prism minimum-deviation formula.

    At minimum deviation the path is symmetric, so r₁=r₂=A/2 and i=e=(A+δmin)/2. Snell's law gives n=sin i/sin r=sin[(A+δmin)/2]/sin(A/2).

  11. Distinguish angular magnification from linear magnification.

    Linear magnification is image height divided by object height. Angular magnification is the angle subtended at the eye with the instrument divided by the angle subtended unaided at a reference viewing distance. A magnifier is primarily specified by angular magnification.

  12. Derive the angular magnification of an astronomical telescope.

    For a distant object, the objective image height is h≈fₒα. The eyepiece gives β≈h/fₑ. Therefore M=β/α≈−fₒ/fₑ, with the sign indicating inversion.

  13. Explain why increased microscope magnification may not reveal more detail.

    Resolution is limited by wavelength, numerical aperture, coherence and system aberrations. Increasing magnification beyond the resolving capability enlarges the same unresolved detail and produces empty magnification.

  14. Discuss spherical aberration and two ways to reduce it.

    Marginal and paraxial rays focus at different axial positions. Reduce it by restricting aperture with a stop, using aspheric surfaces or selecting suitable lens combinations.

  15. Describe how focal length is measured using an optical bench.

    Align object, lens and screen, obtain a sharp real image, measure u and v from the lens reference plane and calculate f=uv/(u+v). Repeat over several conjugate pairs and evaluate uncertainty and systematic alignment effects.

  16. Explain why minimum deviation is used in prism refractometry.

    The ray path is symmetric at the turning point, making the relation between prism angle, deviation and refractive index simple. The minimum is identified by reversal in the direction of emergent-ray movement as the prism is rotated.

  17. Calculate the critical angle for n=1.60 in air.

    θc=arcsin(1/1.60)=38.7°. Total internal reflection occurs for internal incidence angles greater than this value.

  18. A lens has f=20 cm and u=60 cm. Find v and magnification.

    1/v=1/20−1/60=1/30, so v=30 cm. Magnification is −v/u=−0.50. The image is real, inverted and half-size.

  19. Discuss the major loss mechanisms in an optical fibre.

    Absorption, Rayleigh scattering, bending losses, splice and connector losses reduce power. Modal and chromatic dispersion broaden pulses and reduce bandwidth. The mechanisms should be measured or specified over the operating wavelength range.

  20. Explain why a refractive-index result must specify wavelength.

    Most optical materials are dispersive, so n varies with wavelength. A value measured with red light may differ from one measured with blue light. Temperature and material conditions should also be stated.

  21. Describe the distinction between real and virtual images.

    A real image is formed where rays physically converge and can be captured on a screen. A virtual image is located where backward extensions of diverging rays meet and cannot be projected onto a screen.

21. Chapter summary, formula sheet and practical checklist

Geometrical optics models light as rays when wavelength-scale effects can be neglected. Reflection obeys equal incidence and reflection angles. Refraction obeys Snell's law, derived from wavefront construction or stationary optical travel time. Total internal reflection occurs only when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and incidence exceeds the critical angle. Optical fibres use this principle for light guidance.

Mirrors and lenses form images described by paraxial equations and magnification. The lens maker equation links focal length to refractive index and surface curvature. Prisms provide refractive-index measurements at minimum deviation. Magnifiers, microscopes and telescopes use angular magnification. Real instruments depart from ideal models because of aberrations, dispersion, finite apertures, alignment errors and measurement uncertainty.

22. Principal ray construction and image interpretation

Ray diagrams are not decorative sketches: they are geometric tests of the image predicted by an optical model. For a converging lens, draw a ray from the top of the object parallel to the principal axis; after refraction it passes through the image-side focal point. Draw a second ray through the optical centre; in the thin-lens approximation it continues undeviated. Their intersection locates the image top. A third ray directed through the object-side focal point emerges parallel to the axis and provides an independent check. For a diverging lens, extend the refracted rays backward to locate the virtual image.

For a concave mirror, a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through the focus, while a ray through the centre of curvature retraces its path. A ray through the focus reflects parallel to the axis. For a convex mirror, reflected rays diverge and their backward extensions meet behind the mirror. The image is virtual, upright and diminished for a real object at any finite distance. Always distinguish a physical ray from its dotted backward extension.

Ray diagrams should be consistent with the lens or mirror equation and magnification. If the calculated image is real but the diagram shows diverging rays, check the sign convention or construction. If the image height has the wrong sign, check whether inversion has been included. A diagram can reveal a mistaken assumption before arithmetic is completed, especially in multi-element systems where intermediate images may act as virtual objects for later components.

23. Wavefront interpretation and the boundary between ray and wave optics

A wavefront is a surface of constant phase. In a homogeneous isotropic medium, rays are perpendicular to wavefronts. A plane wave has parallel wavefronts and parallel rays; a spherical wave from a point source has concentric spherical wavefronts and radial rays. Refraction changes the spacing of successive wavefronts because the wavelength changes, while frequency remains fixed. The direction change follows from the requirement that the phase along the boundary remains continuous.

Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront can be treated as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is the forward envelope of those wavelets. This construction explains reflection and refraction and also leads toward diffraction when an aperture truncates a wavefront. Geometrical optics emerges when the wavelength is sufficiently small that wavefront curvature and diffraction spreading can be neglected over the region of interest.

The ray model and wave model are complementary, not competing descriptions. Use rays to locate images and estimate magnification; use wavefronts and wave optics to understand interference, diffraction, polarisation and resolution. A finite aperture cannot produce a mathematically perfect point image because waves spread. This is why a telescope with a larger aperture can resolve finer angular detail, and why microscope resolution depends on wavelength and numerical aperture as well as magnification.
