Unit overview

Optics studies the propagation and interaction of light. Geometrical optics models light using rays and is effective when optical dimensions are large compared with wavelength. Wave optics is required to explain interference, diffraction and polarisation. A complete first course connects ray construction with phase, coherence and measurable intensity.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Core textbook content

Wave optics is required to explain interference, diffraction and polarisation. A complete first course connects ray construction with phase, coherence and measurable intensity. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Nature of light and optical models

Light is an electromagnetic wave and, in quantum descriptions, energy is exchanged in photons. In a homogeneous isotropic medium, geometrical rays indicate the direction of energy propagation and are perpendicular to wavefronts. The vacuum speed is c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; in a transparent medium phase velocity is v = c/n, where n is refractive index. The ray approximation is useful when wavelength is much smaller than apertures, obstacles and curvature scales. Wave optics becomes essential near edges, narrow slits and coherent superposition regions.

Worked example / application. For glass with n = 1.50, phase speed v = c/n = 2.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. The frequency remains unchanged at a stationary boundary; wavelength decreases to λ₀/n.

2. Reflection and plane mirrors

The law of reflection states that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, with both angles measured from the normal. Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane. A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image of equal size, located the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. Lateral inversion is a relation between object and image coordinates, not a physical left-right reversal of the object. Multiple reflections between mirrors can create repeated images.

Worked example / application. An object is 1.2 m in front of a plane mirror. Its image is 1.2 m behind the mirror, so object-image separation is 2.4 m. A person moving 0.3 m toward the mirror reduces the separation by 0.6 m.

3. Refraction and Snell's law

At a boundary between media, the tangential component of wavevector is conserved for a stationary planar interface. This leads to Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Angles are measured from the normal. Entering a higher-index medium bends the ray toward the normal; entering a lower-index medium bends it away. Frequency is conserved while speed and wavelength change. Refraction underlies lenses, prisms, optical fibres and atmospheric mirages.

Worked example / application. Light enters glass n₂=1.50 from air at θ₁=30°. sin θ₂ = sin30°/1.50 = 1/3, so θ₂ ≈ 19.5°.

4. Total internal reflection and optical fibres

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and incidence angle exceeds the critical angle θc, where sin θc = n₂/n₁. Below the critical angle, part of the light transmits; above it, an evanescent field exists in the lower-index medium and ideal ray optics predicts complete reflection. Fibres guide light through a high-index core surrounded by lower-index cladding. Numerical aperture describes the acceptance of rays from an external medium and is linked to the indices of core and cladding.

Worked example / application. For core n₁=1.48 and cladding n₂=1.46, sin θc=1.46/1.48=0.9865, giving θc≈80.6°. The small index difference still confines rays incident above this internal critical angle.

5. Spherical mirrors and image formation

Concave and convex spherical mirrors obey the paraxial mirror equation 1/f = 1/u + 1/v under a consistent sign convention. For a spherical mirror of radius R, focal length magnitude is R/2. Ray diagrams use principal rays: parallel rays reflect through the focus for a concave mirror; a ray through the centre of curvature retraces its path. Magnification relates image and object heights and carries sign information about orientation. Paraxial assumptions fail for large apertures and strongly off-axis rays, producing spherical aberration and coma.

Worked example / application. A concave mirror with f=15 cm has an object at u=30 cm. Using 1/v=1/f−1/u=1/15−1/30=1/30, the image is 30 cm from the mirror, real and inverted with unit magnitude magnification.

6. Thin lenses and lens combinations

A thin lens in air has focal length determined by curvature and refractive index through the lensmaker relation 1/f=(n−1)(1/R₁−1/R₂), with radii defined using a consistent sign convention. The thin-lens equation is 1/f=1/u+1/v under the common real-is-positive convention. Converging lenses can form real or virtual images depending on object distance; diverging lenses form virtual upright images for real objects. For thin lenses in contact, optical powers add: P_total=P₁+P₂, with P=1/f in metres and unit dioptre.

Worked example / application. A converging lens of f=10 cm has an object 15 cm away. 1/v=1/10−1/15=1/30, so v=30 cm. Magnification m=−v/u=−2: the image is real, inverted and twice the object's height.

7. Prisms and dispersion

The refractive index of many transparent materials depends on wavelength. This dispersion causes different colours to refract by different amounts in a prism. For a prism of angle A at minimum deviation δm, the path is symmetric and n = sin[(A+δm)/2]/sin(A/2). Dispersion is important in spectrometers and optical design, but it can also cause chromatic aberration in lenses. Achromatic doublets combine glasses with different dispersive properties to reduce colour-dependent focal shift.

Worked example / application. For a prism A=60° and minimum deviation δm=40°, n=sin50°/sin30°≈1.532. The measurement assumes the minimum-deviation condition has been established.

8. Interference and coherence

When coherent waves overlap, their electric fields superpose. For two monochromatic waves with phase difference δ, intensity is I=I₁+I₂+2√(I₁I₂)cosδ. Constructive interference occurs for path difference mλ; destructive interference for (m+1/2)λ when amplitudes are equal. Coherence requires a stable phase relationship over the relevant time and distance. Fringe visibility depends on coherence, intensity balance and experimental stability. Interference is a direct demonstration that light cannot always be treated as independent rays.

Worked example / application. In Young's double-slit experiment, slit separation d=0.25 mm, screen distance D=2.0 m and wavelength λ=500 nm. Fringe spacing β=λD/d=4.0 mm.

9. Diffraction and resolving power

Diffraction is the spreading and redistribution of waves caused by finite apertures or obstacles. For a single slit of width a, minima satisfy a sinθ=mλ for non-zero integer m. A circular aperture produces an Airy pattern; the Rayleigh criterion gives angular resolution θ≈1.22λ/D for aperture diameter D. Diffraction limits the detail that microscopes, telescopes and cameras can resolve. The Fourier transform of an aperture describes its far-field diffraction pattern, linking optics to mathematical methods.

Worked example / application. A telescope objective of diameter 0.10 m observes wavelength 550 nm. θ≈1.22(550×10⁻⁹)/0.10=6.71×10⁻⁶ rad, the approximate Rayleigh angular separation.

10. Polarisation

Polarisation describes the orientation and evolution of the electric-field oscillation. Linear, circular and elliptical states are distinguished by the relative amplitudes and phase of orthogonal components. An ideal polariser transmits the component along its transmission axis. Malus's law gives I=I₀cos²θ for linearly polarised incident light and analyser angle θ. Unpolarised light passing an ideal polariser has mean transmitted intensity I₀/2. Polarisation is used in stress analysis, displays, glare reduction and optical communication.

Worked example / application. A linearly polarised beam of intensity 8.0 W m⁻² passes an analyser at 30°. I=8 cos²30°=6.0 W m⁻².

11. Optical instruments: microscope and telescope

A compound microscope uses an objective to create an enlarged real intermediate image and an eyepiece to magnify that image. A telescope collects light with an objective and presents an angularly magnified image through an eyepiece. Magnification must be considered alongside numerical aperture, field of view, aberrations, diffraction and brightness. The unaided eye has finite accommodation and resolution. Instrument performance is not captured by magnification alone: empty magnification enlarges a blurred image without adding detail.

Worked example / application. For an astronomical telescope in normal adjustment, objective focal length 100 cm and eyepiece focal length 5 cm give angular magnification magnitude M=f_o/f_e=20.

12. Optical experiments and uncertainty

A lens experiment should identify principal planes, object and image positions, alignment uncertainty and the sign convention used. Avoid parallax by aligning the eye with the scale and image marker. In a spectrometer, collimate the slit, focus the telescope, establish vernier readings and take repeated angular observations. Record wavelength, temperature where relevant, slit width and apparatus geometry. Plot transformed data only when the transformation follows from a model and account for uncertainty in both axes where significant.

Worked example / application. For a lens, plotting 1/v against 1/u can test the thin-lens relation but both coordinates contain measurement uncertainty. An alternative is to fit v as a function of u using the nonlinear equation and compare residuals.

13. Integrated optical experiment: determine wavelength

Young's double-slit arrangement provides λ=βd/D. Measure fringe spacing over several fringes rather than one adjacent pair to reduce relative position error. Measure slit separation independently, determine screen distance from the slit plane, and keep the apparatus aligned. Propagate independent uncertainties using (uλ/λ)²=(uβ/β)²+(ud/d)²+(uD/D)². Discuss finite slit width, imperfect coherence, background illumination, fringe-centre judgement and mechanical vibration.

Worked example / application. If β=3.20±0.05 mm, d=0.300±0.005 mm and D=1.80±0.01 m, λ=βd/D=5.33×10⁻⁷ m. Relative standard uncertainty is about 2.2%, so uλ≈1.2×10⁻⁸ m.
