Learning outcomes and study pathway

Engineering applies mathematics and scientific principles to design, analyse, build and improve systems that meet defined needs under constraints such as safety, cost, reliability, sustainability and regulation. Technology study connects hardware, software, data and human use.

Diploma foundations

Engineering mathematics and measurement

Core skills include algebra, trigonometry, vectors, simultaneous equations, basic calculus, graph interpretation and unit conversion. A vector has magnitude and direction; scalar quantities have magnitude only. Resolve an inclined force F into horizontal and vertical components: Fx=F cos θ and Fy=F sin θ when θ is measured from the horizontal.

Worked example: A 200 N force acts at 30° above the horizontal. Fx=200 cos30°≈173.2 N; Fy=200 sin30°=100 N. State the angle convention before resolving components.

Statics, mechanics and materials

For a body in static equilibrium, the resultant force and resultant moment are zero: ΣF=0 and ΣM=0. Stress is σ=F/A; strain is ε=ΔL/L; within the linear elastic region, Young's modulus E=σ/ε. Distinguish elastic deformation from permanent plastic deformation, and recognise that real materials may fail through yielding, fracture, fatigue, creep or corrosion.

Electrical and electronic fundamentals

Ohm's law is V=IR for an ohmic element under constant physical conditions. Electrical power is P=VI=I²R=V²/R. In a series circuit, current is common and voltage divides; in a parallel circuit, voltage is common and current divides. Kirchhoff's current law expresses charge conservation at a node; Kirchhoff's voltage law expresses energy conservation around a closed loop.

Computing foundations

Digital systems represent information using bits. Programming develops algorithms through sequence, selection and iteration. A useful algorithm specifies inputs, processing, outputs and error conditions. Data structures organise information; databases support persistent, queryable records; networks move data using agreed protocols.

Engineering design process

Need and stakeholders → requirements → concepts → analysis and modelling → prototype → test and validate → iterate → document and maintain

Design is an iterative decision process. Translate stakeholder needs into measurable requirements; compare alternatives against constraints; analyse failure modes; test prototypes; and document evidence that the design satisfies its intended use. Verification asks whether the design meets specifications; validation asks whether it solves the real user problem.

Numerical modelling and data

Models simplify reality. Every model should identify variables, governing equations, boundary conditions, parameter sources and assumptions. Numerical solutions require checks for convergence, stability, discretisation error and sensitivity to inputs. Calibration aligns a model with observations; validation tests performance against independent evidence.

Postgraduate and research-level study

Advanced work may involve computational fluid dynamics, finite-element analysis, advanced materials, power electronics, embedded systems, machine learning, robotics, optimisation, digital twins, resilient infrastructure and sustainable manufacturing. Research should identify a gap, justify a method, quantify uncertainty and compare results with credible literature or benchmark data.

Reliability and risk

Reliability is the probability that a system performs its required function for a stated period under stated conditions. Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) identifies failure modes, causes, effects and controls. Risk combines likelihood and consequence; numerical risk scores are prioritisation aids, not proof of safety. Apply relevant standards and independent review for safety-critical designs.

Sustainability and responsible innovation

Evaluate energy, materials, water, emissions, repairability, end-of-life recovery, accessibility and social impacts across a product or infrastructure life cycle. Optimising one metric can transfer burdens elsewhere; use transparent system boundaries and sensitivity analysis.

Worked design and analysis method

Example: A resistive heater operates from a 24 V supply and has resistance 12 Ω. Current I=V/R=24/12=2 A. Power P=VI=24×2=48 W. If it operates for 10 minutes, energy E=Pt=48×600=28,800 J=28.8 kJ. This ideal calculation assumes constant resistance and ignores heat losses; a real design must check conductor rating, insulation, enclosure temperature, protection and applicable electrical codes.

Good technical reporting

  • Define the problem, users, constraints and success criteria.
  • List assumptions and provide a labelled system diagram.
  • Show equations, units, substitutions and significant figures.
  • Compare predicted and measured results and explain discrepancies.
  • Address uncertainty, safety, ethics, accessibility and environmental impacts.

Practice questions with guidance

1. A simply supported beam carries a central point load. What equilibrium equations are required?
Use ΣFx=0, ΣFy=0 and ΣM=0. For a symmetric beam with a central vertical load W, each support reaction is W/2.
2. Explain the difference between verification and validation.
Verification checks conformance to specified requirements; validation checks whether the system meets the intended need in its real context.
3. A 10 Ω resistor is connected to 5 V. Calculate current and power.
I=V/R=0.5 A; P=VI=2.5 W.
4. Discuss two limitations of a finite-element model.
Possible limitations include idealised geometry/material assumptions, mesh dependence, uncertain boundary conditions, poor input data and numerical approximation error. Explain how convergence and validation tests reduce—but do not eliminate—these limitations.
5. Design a small research project evaluating a solar-powered water-pumping system.
Define demand and site conditions; measure solar resource, flow, head and energy; specify pump and storage; model seasonal performance; test a prototype; analyse reliability, lifecycle cost, water access and environmental impacts; state uncertainty and ethical considerations.

Chapter 1: Engineering Foundations, Design Thinking and Professional Practice

Learning outcomes. Explain the engineering design process; translate needs into measurable requirements; distinguish constraints from objectives; use models and calculations responsibly; and evaluate safety, sustainability, cost and ethical impacts.

1.1 Engineering as applied problem solving

Engineering applies mathematics, science, experience and design judgement to create or improve products, infrastructure, processes and services. An engineering problem is rarely a calculation alone. It begins with a human or operational need and must be translated into requirements that can be tested. A bridge, for example, must carry specified loads, meet serviceability limits, withstand environmental exposure, be constructible and maintainable, fit a budget and comply with applicable codes. A technically elegant design that cannot be safely built or maintained is not a successful engineering solution.

1.2 The design process

A typical process includes problem definition, stakeholder analysis, research, requirements, concept generation, feasibility assessment, modelling, detailed design, prototyping, verification, validation, implementation and review. The process is iterative: test results may reveal that the original requirements were incomplete or that a proposed concept is impractical. Verification asks whether the design meets its specified requirements; validation asks whether it solves the intended real-world problem for its users.

Need and context → requirements → concepts → analysis and selection → detailed design → prototype → verification and validation → implementation → monitoring

1.3 Requirements, constraints and trade-offs

Requirements should be measurable wherever possible. “Low cost” is vague; “capital cost below the approved budget while meeting the specified load and service life” is testable. Constraints may include safety, regulations, available materials, dimensions, operating environment, energy supply, manufacturing capability and accessibility. Objectives may include minimising mass, cost, energy use or maintenance while maximising reliability, performance or user value. Multi-objective problems rarely have one perfect solution; engineers document trade-offs and explain why a design is acceptable.

1.4 Models and assumptions

Models simplify reality to make analysis possible. A beam may initially be modelled as slender and linearly elastic; a fluid as incompressible; an electrical component as ideal. These assumptions must be checked against the actual system. A model is useful when its simplifications preserve the behaviour important to the decision. Sensitivity analysis explores how results change when uncertain inputs vary. Numerical precision does not compensate for an inappropriate model.

1.5 Engineering calculations and units

Engineering calculations should begin with a diagram, known quantities, unknown quantities, assumptions and governing principles. Use coherent units and show intermediate steps. Dimensional analysis can identify errors: force is measured in newtons (kg m s⁻²), energy in joules (N m), power in watts (J s⁻¹), and pressure in pascals (N m⁻²). Significant figures should reflect the quality of input data. Where safety margins are required, distinguish characteristic loads, design loads, material strengths and code-prescribed factors rather than applying an unexplained multiplier.

Worked example – power: A motor transfers 18,000 J of useful energy in 60 s. Average output power P = E/t = 18,000/60 = 300 W. If the motor's input power is 400 W, efficiency η = useful output/input × 100% = 75%. The calculation assumes the energy and time measurements refer to the same operating interval.

1.6 Safety, risk and failure

Risk assessment identifies hazards, possible consequences, exposure and controls. The hierarchy of controls prioritises elimination, substitution and engineering controls before relying on administrative rules or personal protective equipment. Failure mode analysis considers how a component or process might fail, the effects of failure and ways to reduce likelihood or consequence. Safety factors and reliability methods must be applied within the relevant design code and professional context. A student example is not a certified design.

1.7 Materials and sustainability

Material selection considers strength, stiffness, toughness, fatigue resistance, corrosion, density, thermal and electrical properties, manufacturability, availability, cost and end-of-life impacts. Life-cycle thinking considers extraction, processing, transport, use, maintenance and disposal or reuse. A lighter component may reduce operating energy but require a more energy-intensive material; the full system boundary matters. Circular design seeks durability, repairability, reuse and recovery of materials.

1.8 Engineering ethics and communication

Engineers have duties to protect public safety, communicate limitations, avoid misleading claims, manage conflicts of interest and work within their competence. Drawings, specifications, calculations, test reports and change records must be clear enough for review. If evidence shows a design is unsafe, the concern must be escalated through appropriate professional and organisational channels. Ethical practice includes accessibility, environmental stewardship and consideration of communities affected by infrastructure and technology.

Chapter summary and review

  • Engineering begins with a defined need and measurable requirements.
  • Design is iterative and balances performance, safety, cost, sustainability and usability.
  • Models rely on assumptions that must be checked for the decision at hand.
  • Calculations require consistent units, traceable inputs and explicit limitations.
  • Risk management, professional ethics and clear communication are integral to engineering.

Chapter 2: Engineering Mechanics — Statics, Dynamics and Strength of Materials

2.1 Mechanics and idealisation

Engineering mechanics studies the effects of forces on bodies at rest or in motion. Statics examines equilibrium; dynamics examines motion and the forces associated with it. A free-body diagram isolates the body of interest and represents external forces, moments and support reactions. Correct diagrams are essential: calculations based on an incomplete force system can be mathematically consistent yet physically wrong. State whether a body is modelled as a particle, rigid body or deformable continuum.

2.2 Force vectors and equilibrium

Force is a vector with magnitude, direction and point of application. In Cartesian coordinates, a force can be resolved into perpendicular components. For a particle in planar equilibrium, the vector sum of forces is zero, giving ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. For a rigid body, rotational equilibrium is also required: the sum of moments about any point is zero. A moment is the turning effect of a force and equals force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action.

Worked example: A 200 N load is supported by two vertical cables. If cable A carries 80 N and the system is in vertical equilibrium, cable B carries 120 N because ΣFy = 80 + B − 200 = 0. This assumes both cable forces are vertical and no other vertical force acts.

2.3 Distributed loads and support reactions

Loads may be concentrated, distributed or applied as couples. A uniformly distributed load w over length L has a resultant wL acting at the centroid of the loaded length. Support models must match physical restraint: a pin can provide two force components in a planar model, a roller one normal reaction, and a fixed support force components and a moment. Over-constraining or under-constraining a model changes the reaction calculation and may indicate a statically indeterminate or unstable structure.

2.4 Stress, strain and elastic behaviour

Normal stress is average axial force divided by cross-sectional area, σ = F/A, under the assumptions of a suitable uniform load path and stress distribution. Normal strain is change in length divided by original length, ε = ΔL/L. In a linear elastic range, Hooke's law relates stress and strain through Young's modulus, E: σ = Eε. Shear stress and shear strain describe tangential deformation. Real materials may be anisotropic, nonlinear, temperature-dependent or time-dependent, so simple equations require appropriate material models.

2.5 Axial deformation and thermal effects

For a uniform prismatic bar under axial load in the linear elastic range, extension is δ = FL/(AE). The expression shows that extension increases with force and length and decreases with area and stiffness. Temperature changes can also cause expansion or contraction. If thermal movement is restrained, thermal stresses may develop. Design must consider connections, stress concentrations, fatigue, creep, corrosion and manufacturing tolerances in addition to average stress.

2.6 Bending and torsion

Beams resist transverse loads through internal shear force and bending moment. For simple elastic bending of a prismatic beam, the flexure formula relates normal stress to bending moment, distance from the neutral axis and second moment of area. Torsion describes twisting under applied torque; for circular shafts in the elastic range, shear stress varies with radius and polar second moment of area. These models have assumptions about geometry, material behaviour and loading; they are not substitutes for code-based design.

2.7 Motion and Newton's laws

Newton's first law describes inertia; the second relates resultant force to rate of change of momentum and, for constant mass, gives ΣF = ma; the third describes equal and opposite interaction forces acting on different bodies. Kinematics describes displacement, velocity and acceleration without requiring the forces that caused motion. Dynamics links motion to forces. Work is force integrated along displacement; kinetic energy is associated with motion; power is the rate of energy transfer.

2.8 Engineering judgement and safety

Mechanics calculations depend on boundary conditions, load combinations, material properties and geometric idealisations. Validate models with independent checks, experiments or recognised standards. Where failure could harm people, design must be performed and reviewed by appropriately qualified professionals under the relevant codes and regulatory requirements. Student calculations illustrate principles but do not certify real structures or machinery.

Chapter 3: Materials Engineering, Selection and Failure

3.1 Material families and structure

Engineering materials are commonly grouped as metals and alloys, ceramics and glasses, polymers, composites and semiconductors. Their properties arise from atomic bonding, crystal structure, microstructure, defects and processing history. Metals often combine ductility with electrical and thermal conductivity; ceramics often offer hardness, high-temperature stability and chemical resistance but may be brittle; polymers are generally lightweight and can be readily shaped, but their stiffness and temperature resistance vary widely. Composites combine phases to obtain tailored properties.

3.2 Mechanical properties

Strength describes resistance to applied stress; stiffness describes resistance to elastic deformation; toughness concerns energy absorption before fracture; hardness relates to resistance to local indentation or scratching; ductility describes plastic deformation before fracture; and resilience describes recoverable elastic energy. These properties are distinct. A very strong material may be brittle, while a ductile material may deform substantially before failure. Selection should use test data relevant to loading mode, temperature, environment and manufacturing route.

3.3 Stress-strain behaviour

A tensile test records force and extension and converts them into engineering stress and strain using original area and gauge length. The initial linear region may be used to estimate Young's modulus. Yield strength marks the onset of significant plastic deformation under a stated definition; ultimate tensile strength is the maximum engineering stress; fracture occurs when the specimen separates. Necking causes local area reduction, so true stress differs from engineering stress at large deformation.

3.4 Fatigue, creep and fracture

Fatigue is progressive damage under repeated or fluctuating loading and can occur below static strength. Surface condition, stress concentrations, corrosion and load cycles influence fatigue life. Creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained load, especially important at elevated temperature. Fracture mechanics examines crack growth and stress intensity; defects can reduce structural integrity even when average stress is below nominal strength. Inspection, design details, material quality and maintenance are part of lifecycle safety.

3.5 Corrosion and protection

Corrosion is degradation through chemical or electrochemical interaction with the environment. Control strategies include material selection, coatings, inhibitors, cathodic protection, drainage, design that avoids crevices and appropriate maintenance. Corrosion protection should be evaluated over the whole service life, including coating damage, inspection access and environmental effects. Dissimilar-metal contact in an electrolyte can create galvanic corrosion when conditions support it.

3.6 Materials selection methods

A rational selection process begins with function, constraints and objectives. Identify loading, temperature, environment, geometry, joining, manufacturing, regulatory requirements and service life. Screen out materials that fail mandatory constraints; then rank feasible candidates using weighted criteria or performance indices. Sensitivity analysis tests whether the preferred material changes when assumptions or weights change. Cost should include processing, maintenance, replacement, energy use and end-of-life management, not only purchase price.

3.7 Manufacturing and quality

Manufacturing routes include casting, forming, machining, joining, additive manufacturing and polymer processing. Each route affects microstructure, tolerances, surface finish, residual stress, defects and cost. Quality assurance defines processes and records; quality control checks outputs against specifications. Statistical process control can detect variation and shifts, but it does not replace sound process design or product safety validation.

3.8 Case study: selecting a lightweight structural member

Assume a design team must select a member for a non-critical educational prototype. Candidates include an aluminium alloy, a steel grade and a fibre-reinforced polymer. The team first defines loads, deflection limits, environment, connection methods, available fabrication tools and budget. It compares density, elastic modulus, strength, corrosion behaviour, joining, cost and recycling. The composite may offer high specific stiffness but require specialised connections and careful inspection; aluminium may reduce mass and resist atmospheric corrosion but require attention to fatigue and joining; steel may be inexpensive and stiff but heavier and may need corrosion protection. The decision depends on constraints and verified data, not a single headline property.

Chapter 4: Electrical Circuits, Electronics and Measurement

4.1 Charge, current, voltage and resistance

Electric charge is a property of matter; current is the rate of charge flow. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge. Resistance describes opposition to current under specified conditions. Ohm's law, V = IR, applies to an ohmic element when its resistance is approximately constant over the operating range. Components such as diodes, lamps and semiconductor devices may have nonlinear current-voltage relationships.

Source (+) ── switch ── resistor ── return (−)
Conventional current direction is from higher to lower potential through the external circuit.

4.2 Series and parallel networks

In a series circuit, the same current flows through each component and supply voltage is shared. In a parallel circuit, each branch has the same potential difference and total current is the sum of branch currents. Equivalent resistance in series is the sum of resistances; for ideal parallel resistors, reciprocals add. Kirchhoff's current law expresses conservation of charge at a node; Kirchhoff's voltage law expresses energy conservation around a closed loop under the lumped-circuit model.

Worked example: A 12 V source is connected across a 6 Ω resistor. Current I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A. Power dissipated P = VI = 24 W. The resistor must be rated appropriately, with a suitable safety margin and thermal environment.

4.3 Power, energy and electrical safety

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer. For a resistive element, P = VI = I²R = V²/R under the applicable assumptions. Wiring, insulation, protective devices, earthing, isolation and enclosure design are critical safety considerations. Work on mains or high-energy electrical systems must be performed by competent authorised personnel under local regulations. Classroom circuit calculations are not instructions for unsafe live electrical work.

4.4 Capacitors, inductors and transient behaviour

Capacitors store energy in an electric field and oppose rapid changes in voltage; inductors store energy in a magnetic field and oppose rapid changes in current. In simple RC and RL circuits, time constants describe the characteristic rate of transient response. Real components have parasitic resistance, leakage, tolerances and frequency-dependent behaviour. AC circuits require attention to phase, impedance, frequency and power factor.

4.5 Semiconductor devices

Semiconductors have electrical properties between conductors and insulators and can be controlled through doping, junctions and applied fields. A diode permits current preferentially in one direction under suitable bias; a transistor can act as a switch or amplifier. Integrated circuits combine many devices on a substrate. Device behaviour depends on temperature, bias, fabrication and operating limits. Datasheets provide absolute maximum ratings and recommended operating conditions that must not be confused.

4.6 Sensors and instrumentation

Sensors convert a physical, chemical or biological quantity into a measurable signal. Important characteristics include range, sensitivity, resolution, linearity, hysteresis, repeatability, response time and drift. Signal conditioning may include amplification, filtering, isolation and analogue-to-digital conversion. Calibration, uncertainty analysis, grounding and electromagnetic compatibility influence measurement quality. A digital display does not guarantee accuracy.

  2. Calculate the equivalent resistance of two 4 Ω resistors in series and in parallel.
  3. State Kirchhoff's current law and explain its physical basis.
  4. Compare the energy-storage mechanisms of capacitors and inductors.

Chapter 5: Thermodynamics, Fluid Mechanics and Heat Transfer

5.1 Thermodynamic systems and properties

A thermodynamic system is a defined quantity of matter or region in space selected for analysis. A closed system exchanges energy but not mass with its surroundings; an open system exchanges both; an isolated system exchanges neither ideally. State properties include pressure, temperature, volume, internal energy and enthalpy. Equations of state relate properties for a specified substance and model. Engineers distinguish intensive properties, which do not depend on system size, from extensive properties, which do.

5.2 First law and energy balances

The first law is conservation of energy. For a closed system, change in total energy equals heat added minus work done by the system under a common sign convention. For steady-flow devices, energy balances include enthalpy flow, kinetic and potential energy, heat and shaft work. Turbines, compressors, pumps, nozzles, heat exchangers and throttling valves are analysed using control-volume models. Assumptions such as steady state, negligible kinetic-energy change or adiabatic operation must be justified.

Control volume: mass in + energy in → [device] → mass out + energy out
Account for heat, shaft work, enthalpy, kinetic energy and potential energy.

5.3 Second law and efficiency

The second law explains directionality and limits on converting heat into work. Entropy is a state property used to quantify energy dispersal and irreversibility within thermodynamic models. No heat engine operating in a cycle can convert all absorbed heat into work while rejecting no heat to a lower-temperature reservoir. Thermal efficiency is useful work output divided by heat input. Real devices have irreversibilities due to friction, finite temperature differences, mixing and pressure losses.

5.4 Fluid properties and pressure

Fluid mechanics studies liquids and gases at rest and in motion. Density is mass per unit volume; specific weight is weight per unit volume; viscosity characterises resistance to shear deformation. Pressure is normal force per unit area and in a static fluid increases with depth according to hydrostatic principles. Pressure measurement may be absolute, gauge or differential; the reference must be stated.

5.5 Continuity and Bernoulli principles

Mass conservation gives the continuity equation. For steady incompressible flow through a single inlet and outlet, area multiplied by average velocity is constant when there are no branches or leaks. Bernoulli's equation relates pressure, velocity and elevation along a streamline for ideal steady incompressible inviscid flow with no shaft work or losses. Real systems require head-loss and pump/turbine terms. Applying the ideal equation without checking assumptions can produce unsafe or inaccurate designs.

Worked example: Water flows through a pipe whose area reduces from 0.020 m² to 0.010 m². If average inlet velocity is 1.5 m/s and flow is steady and incompressible, continuity gives outlet velocity v₂ = A₁v₁/A₂ = 3.0 m/s.

5.6 Reynolds number and flow regimes

Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous effects and helps characterise flow regimes. In internal pipe flow, low Reynolds numbers are associated with laminar behaviour, while high values are commonly associated with turbulence; transition depends on disturbances, geometry and conditions. Turbulent flow increases mixing and often pressure loss. Friction-factor correlations and Moody-chart methods depend on Reynolds number and relative roughness.

5.7 Heat exchangers and thermal design

Heat exchangers transfer energy between fluids while keeping them separated in many designs. Parallel-flow and counter-flow arrangements have different temperature profiles. Design methods include log-mean temperature difference and effectiveness-NTU approaches. Fouling, corrosion, pressure drop, thermal expansion, leakage and maintainability affect real performance. Insulation reduces unwanted heat transfer but must be selected for temperature, moisture, fire and environmental conditions.

Chapter 6: Computing, Programming and Data Systems for Engineers

6.1 Computational thinking

Computational thinking decomposes complex problems into manageable parts, identifies patterns, abstracts essential features and develops algorithms. An algorithm is a finite, unambiguous sequence of steps for solving a problem. A flowchart or pseudocode can clarify logic before implementation. Engineers should define inputs, outputs, constraints, error conditions and acceptance tests before writing code.

6.2 Programming fundamentals

Programs use variables, data types, operators, control structures, functions and data structures. Conditional statements select actions based on logical tests; loops repeat operations; functions encapsulate reusable logic. Modular design improves testing and maintainability. Naming, comments and documentation should explain intent rather than restate obvious syntax. Input validation prevents malformed or unsafe data from propagating through a system.

Input → validation → processing/algorithm → output
↘ error handling and logging

6.3 Data structures and algorithms

Arrays, lists, stacks, queues, trees, graphs, hash tables and databases organise information for different operations. Algorithmic complexity describes how resource use grows with input size. Big-O notation gives an asymptotic description, not an exact runtime. A linear search may be adequate for small datasets; indexed or hashed lookup may be more efficient for larger workloads. Choose structures based on access patterns, memory, reliability and maintainability.

6.4 Numerical methods and simulation

Numerical methods approximate solutions where analytical solutions are unavailable or impractical. Root-finding, numerical integration, finite-difference methods and iterative solvers are widely used. Discretisation introduces approximation error; round-off and truncation errors must be considered. Verification checks that equations are implemented correctly; validation compares model predictions with physical reality or trusted data. A simulation should report assumptions, parameter sources, convergence checks and sensitivity.

6.5 Data acquisition and databases

Engineering data systems may collect sensor readings, machine events, quality records and maintenance histories. Data pipelines acquire, validate, timestamp, transform, store and analyse records. Database design uses schemas, keys, constraints and transactions to maintain consistency. Time-series data require attention to sampling rate, missing observations, clock synchronisation and calibration metadata. Data retention and access should follow privacy, security and operational requirements.

6.6 Cybersecurity and dependable systems

Security principles include confidentiality, integrity and availability. Threat modelling identifies assets, adversaries, attack surfaces and possible controls. Authentication verifies identity; authorisation controls permissions; encryption protects data in transit or at rest when correctly implemented. Secure engineering includes patch management, least privilege, backups, monitoring, incident response and safe defaults. Connected industrial systems require special attention to operational continuity and physical safety.

6.7 Artificial intelligence and engineering judgement

Machine-learning systems infer patterns from data and can support prediction, classification, optimisation and anomaly detection. Performance depends on data quality, representativeness, labels, feature design, validation and deployment conditions. Models can drift when the operating environment changes. Explainability, bias, privacy, security and accountability matter where predictions influence people or safety-critical operations. AI output should be checked against engineering principles and independently validated before use.

Review questions

  1. Explain decomposition and abstraction using a sensor-monitoring problem.
  2. Compare arrays, queues and hash tables for different access patterns.
  3. Distinguish verification from validation in a numerical simulation.
  4. Identify three sources of error in a data-acquisition pipeline.
  5. Explain confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  6. Why must an AI model be monitored after deployment?

Chapter 7: Engineering Project Management, Quality and Capstone Design

7.1 Project scope and deliverables

An engineering project converts a defined need into a controlled set of deliverables within constraints of time, cost, quality, safety and resources. Scope states what is included and excluded. A work breakdown structure divides the project into manageable work packages. Deliverables should be observable and accepted against documented criteria. Uncontrolled scope changes create schedule and cost risk; change requests should record rationale, impacts, approval and revised baselines.

7.2 Scheduling and dependencies

Project schedules identify activities, durations, dependencies, milestones and resource constraints. A network diagram shows logical relationships; the critical path is the longest-duration path through a dependency network and determines the earliest possible completion under the model. Delays on critical activities affect the finish date unless mitigated. Estimates should include uncertainty and be updated as evidence improves. A Gantt chart communicates timing but does not by itself explain resource conflicts or logic.

Requirements → concept design → detailed design → procurement → build → test → handover
Feedback loops: testing may require redesign, re-procurement or scope adjustment.

7.3 Cost estimation and lifecycle value

Cost estimates may be conceptual, preliminary or detailed depending on design maturity. They include labour, materials, equipment, overhead, contingency, testing, commissioning, maintenance and disposal. Contingency addresses identified uncertainty and should not be treated as an unexplained reserve. Lifecycle cost compares alternatives over a defined service period and may include energy, downtime, replacement and environmental externalities. Discounting is used where appropriate to compare cash flows occurring at different times.

7.4 Quality management

Quality planning defines requirements, standards, inspection points and responsibilities. Quality assurance focuses on confidence that processes are capable; quality control examines outputs. Root-cause analysis investigates why a defect occurred rather than merely correcting the visible symptom. Tools include process maps, check sheets, Pareto analysis, cause-and-effect diagrams and control charts. Statistical process control distinguishes common-cause variation from signals that may indicate a special cause.

7.5 Testing, verification and validation

Test plans link requirements to test methods, instruments, acceptance criteria and records. Verification may use inspection, analysis, demonstration or testing. Validation checks whether the system meets stakeholder needs in its intended environment. Testing should consider normal, boundary, overload, fault and environmental conditions where relevant. Instrument calibration and traceability matter; a failed test should trigger investigation and controlled retesting rather than undocumented adjustment.

7.6 Reliability and maintenance

Reliability is the probability that a system performs a required function for a specified time under stated conditions. Maintainability concerns how effectively a system can be restored. Availability combines reliability and maintainability over time. Preventive maintenance is scheduled; condition-based maintenance uses indicators of equipment condition; corrective maintenance follows failure. Failure data can support reliability analysis, but small samples and changing operating conditions limit prediction.

7.7 Professional communication and handover

Engineering handover includes drawings, specifications, operating instructions, maintenance schedules, spare-parts information, test certificates, risk registers and training. Technical reports should state purpose, methods, results, uncertainty, limitations and recommendations. Visuals must be labelled and traceable. Decisions and assumptions should be recorded so future teams can understand why a design was selected.

7.8 Capstone design brief and assessment

Brief: Design a low-cost rainwater monitoring and storage system for a community facility. Establish stakeholder requirements, estimate demand, propose tank capacity, select sensors and materials, design safe overflow and access, consider water quality and maintenance, estimate lifecycle cost and create a verification plan. Do not present the student design as a certified potable-water installation.

Assessment questions: (1) Produce a stakeholder and requirements matrix. (2) Draw a system block diagram showing collection, storage, sensing and user notification. (3) Identify three failure modes and controls. (4) Prepare a project schedule with dependencies. (5) Explain how prototype testing would verify accuracy and validate usability. (6) Evaluate sustainability and lifecycle cost.

Chapter 8: Control Systems, Automation and Robotics

8.1 Control-system architecture

A control system regulates a process to achieve a desired behaviour. Open-loop control applies an action without measuring the output; closed-loop control uses feedback to compare measured output with a reference and adjust the input. Feedback can improve disturbance rejection and accuracy but may introduce instability if designed poorly. Sensors, controllers, actuators and the physical process form the basic loop.

Reference/setpoint → comparator → controller → actuator → process → output
↑ ↓
└──────── sensor/feedback ─────┘

8.2 Dynamic response

Dynamic systems are described by differential equations, transfer functions or state-space models. First-order systems respond with a characteristic time constant; second-order systems may be underdamped, critically damped or overdamped. Transient response includes rise time, overshoot and settling time; steady-state error measures the final difference from the reference. Model parameters should be estimated from physical principles or validated data.

8.3 Proportional, integral and derivative control

A proportional controller responds to current error; integral action accumulates error over time and can remove steady-state offset; derivative action responds to the rate of error change and may improve damping but amplify measurement noise. PID control combines these terms. Tuning balances speed, stability, overshoot, robustness and actuator limits. Anti-windup strategies address integral accumulation when actuators saturate.

8.4 Stability and robustness

A stable system returns toward an acceptable operating condition after a disturbance under the model assumptions. Stability analysis may use characteristic equations, root locations, frequency response or Lyapunov methods. Robustness asks whether performance remains acceptable despite uncertainty, parameter variation and unmodelled dynamics. Safety-critical control requires hazard analysis, independent protection layers and verification beyond nominal simulation.

8.5 Robotics and sensing

Robotics combines mechanics, electronics, sensing, control and computation. A robot's workspace, degrees of freedom, payload, repeatability and accuracy are distinct characteristics. Encoders measure joint position; inertial sensors estimate motion; cameras and range sensors provide environmental information. Sensor fusion combines data while accounting for uncertainty. Calibration, coordinate frames and latency affect perception and control.

8.6 Industrial automation and human factors

Industrial automation may use programmable logic controllers, supervisory control systems, industrial networks and human-machine interfaces. Design must account for interlocks, emergency stops, safe states, maintenance access and operator workload. Cybersecurity and functional safety are related but distinct disciplines. Automation should be assessed for impacts on workers, accessibility, skills, reliability and accountability.

  4. Define overshoot, settling time and steady-state error.

Chapter 9: Manufacturing Systems, Industrial Engineering and Sustainability

9.1 Manufacturing processes

Manufacturing transforms materials into products through processes such as casting, forming, machining, joining, moulding, additive manufacturing and surface treatment. Process selection depends on geometry, material, tolerance, surface finish, production volume, tooling, quality, cost and environmental impact. Design for manufacture reduces unnecessary complexity and improves repeatability. Design for assembly considers access, part count, fastening, alignment and maintenance.

9.2 Production systems and capacity

Production systems range from job shops and batch production to assembly lines and continuous processes. Capacity is the maximum sustainable output under stated conditions; effective capacity accounts for planned downtime and operating constraints. Bottlenecks limit throughput. Little's Law relates average work in progress, throughput and flow time under stable conditions. Lean methods seek to reduce waste while protecting quality and worker safety; poorly implemented targets can create overwork or hide risk.

9.3 Quality and statistical process control

Variation occurs in all processes. Control charts distinguish common-cause variation from signals that may indicate special causes. Capability indices compare process variation with specification limits, but they are meaningful only when the process is stable and data assumptions are appropriate. Inspection detects defects; process improvement prevents them. Root-cause analysis should test hypotheses with evidence rather than assign blame prematurely.

9.4 Additive manufacturing and digital production

Additive manufacturing builds components layer by layer from digital models. It can enable complex geometries, customisation and reduced material waste in some applications. Limitations include build rate, anisotropy, residual stress, surface finish, material qualification, post-processing and inspection. Digital twins connect models with operational data, but model fidelity, data quality, cybersecurity and governance determine usefulness.

9.5 Sustainable engineering and circularity

Sustainable engineering considers energy, materials, water, emissions, waste, worker conditions and community impacts. Life-cycle assessment can identify burden shifting between production, use and disposal. Design strategies include lightweighting, modularity, repairability, reuse, remanufacture and recycling. Recyclability depends on material separation, contamination, infrastructure and economics; it should not be claimed solely from a product label.

9.6 Industrial safety and human factors

Industrial systems should be designed around human capabilities and limitations. Ergonomics reduces physical strain and cognitive overload. Safety engineering identifies hazards across design, installation, operation, maintenance and decommissioning. Safe systems use guarding, interlocks, isolation, procedures, training and emergency response. Incident investigation examines system conditions and organisational factors rather than focusing only on individual error.

9.7 Case study: production-line improvement

A factory experiences long lead times and inconsistent product quality. The team maps the process, measures cycle time and defects, identifies a bottleneck, studies changeover and material flow, and tests a small improvement. It establishes baseline performance, checks worker feedback, validates quality and monitors whether gains persist. The team should not optimise a single workstation if doing so increases downstream inventory or creates a safety hazard.

  4. Describe four design strategies that support circularity.

Chapter 10: Energy Engineering, Renewable Power and Electrical Grids

10.1 Energy resources and conversion

Energy systems convert primary resources into useful services such as mobility, heating, cooling, lighting and computation. Sources include fossil fuels, nuclear energy, solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal and sustainable bioenergy. Comparisons require a defined system boundary and functional output. Conversion efficiency, capacity factor, dispatchability, lifecycle emissions, land and water use, materials, reliability and cost are different dimensions of performance.

10.2 Solar photovoltaic systems

Photovoltaic cells convert incident light into electrical energy through semiconductor junctions. Module output depends on irradiance, temperature, orientation, shading, degradation and electrical configuration. Inverters convert direct current to alternating current and may provide grid-support functions. System design includes mounting, cabling, protection, isolation, earthing, monitoring and safe access. Actual yield estimates should use local solar-resource data and account for losses.

10.3 Wind and hydropower

Wind turbines extract kinetic energy from moving air; power depends on air density, swept area and wind speed, subject to aerodynamic and practical limits. Turbine siting considers wind resource, turbulence, grid connection, noise, wildlife and community impacts. Hydropower converts gravitational potential and flow energy into electricity. Reservoir systems can provide storage and dispatchability but may alter river ecology, sediment movement, land use and community livelihoods.

10.4 Energy storage and grid integration

Storage technologies include electrochemical batteries, pumped hydro, thermal storage, compressed air and other emerging systems. They differ in power rating, energy capacity, response time, efficiency, cycle life, safety and material requirements. Electricity grids balance supply and demand continuously. Variable renewable generation can be integrated through forecasting, flexible demand, storage, interconnection, dispatchable generation and grid-forming controls. Reliability requires planning for extreme events and equipment failure.

10.5 Power electronics and protection

Power electronics control conversion and flow of electrical energy using semiconductor switches. Converters may regulate voltage, frequency and power factor. Protection systems detect abnormal conditions and isolate faults to limit damage. Coordination of relays, breakers, grounding and system protection is specialised work governed by standards and regulations. Educational descriptions are not a substitute for certified electrical design or installation.

10.6 Energy efficiency and demand management

Efficiency reduces energy required to deliver a service, while demand response shifts or reduces consumption in response to system conditions. Building efficiency may involve insulation, passive design, efficient lighting, controls and heat pumps. Industrial efficiency may involve motors, compressed air, process integration and waste-heat recovery. Rebound effects can reduce expected savings if lower operating costs lead to increased use.

10.7 Energy transition planning

Energy transition planning integrates technology, economics, policy, workforce, infrastructure, environmental justice and reliability. Scenarios should be transparent about assumptions and uncertainty. A least-cost system model may not capture every social or ecological value. Community consultation, fair compensation, skills development, local supply chains and access to affordable energy influence transition legitimacy and durability.

  4. Explain why storage is not the only method of integrating variable renewable generation.

Chapter 11: Engineering Analysis, Optimisation and Design Verification

11.1 Engineering problem formulation

Engineering optimisation begins by defining design variables, objective functions, constraints and system boundaries. A single-objective problem may minimise cost or mass while meeting performance constraints; multi-objective problems examine trade-offs such as cost, reliability, energy and environmental impact. The mathematical formulation should reflect physical reality and stakeholder priorities. An optimum under an incomplete model may be unacceptable in practice.

11.2 Analytical, numerical and experimental methods

Analytical methods derive solutions from governing equations under simplifying assumptions. Numerical methods approximate solutions for complex geometries or nonlinear systems. Experimental methods measure physical behaviour. The three approaches can complement one another: analytical estimates check plausibility, numerical models explore detail, and experiments validate predictions. Disagreement should trigger investigation of assumptions, boundary conditions, measurement error and implementation.

11.3 Finite element analysis

Finite element analysis divides a domain into smaller elements connected at nodes. Governing equations are approximated over the mesh and solved for quantities such as displacement, temperature or stress. Mesh refinement can improve accuracy but increases computational cost. Convergence studies, appropriate element selection, boundary conditions, material models and load application are essential. Singularities at idealised corners or point loads require careful interpretation; a colourful contour plot is not proof of a correct model.

11.4 Optimisation and sensitivity

Optimisation algorithms search for candidate solutions under constraints. Gradient-based methods use local derivatives; heuristic and evolutionary methods can explore complex spaces but may not guarantee a global optimum. Sensitivity analysis identifies parameters that strongly affect outputs. Robust design considers variation in manufacturing, loading and environment. Uncertainty should be quantified where possible and communicated to decision-makers.

11.5 Verification and validation framework

Verification asks whether the mathematical model and software are solved correctly; validation asks whether the model represents the physical system sufficiently for its intended use. Code verification may use analytical benchmarks; solution verification examines numerical convergence; validation compares model predictions with independent experimental data. Calibration adjusts parameters using data and should not be confused with independent validation.

Requirements → conceptual model → equations → numerical implementation → verification → validation against measurements → design decision

11.6 Engineering ethics in modelling

Engineers must not conceal model limitations, selectively report favourable results or present unvalidated simulations as certification. Assumptions, data sources, software versions and sensitivity checks should be recorded. Safety-critical decisions require independent review and relevant standards. Models support professional judgement; they do not transfer accountability from the engineer to software.

  4. Distinguish calibration, verification and validation.
  5. Identify ethical risks in presenting a simulation to a client.
  6. Propose a robust design approach for a component exposed to uncertain loads.
