How to use this History course

History is the disciplined study of human societies across time. It investigates how people organised power, produced wealth, exchanged ideas, built institutions, migrated, fought, cooperated and interpreted their experiences. Historical knowledge is not simply a list of dates. It is an evidence-based explanation of change, continuity, causation, context and historical significance.

These independent notes are arranged as a progressive learning pathway. Diploma learners should concentrate on concepts, chronology, evidence and clear explanation. Undergraduate learners should compare interpretations, evaluate sources and construct sustained arguments. Postgraduate learners should interrogate historiography, research design, archives, ethics and the limits of existing explanations.

Course learning outcomes

By the end of the relevant pathway, learners should be able to:

  • Explain chronology, periodisation, continuity, change, causation and historical significance.
  • Distinguish primary, secondary and tertiary sources and evaluate their strengths and limitations.
  • Situate historical events within their political, economic, social, cultural and environmental contexts.
  • Compare historical developments across African, Asian, European, American and other world regions without assuming that one region's experience represents all societies.
  • Analyse empire, trade, slavery, colonial rule, resistance, nationalism, decolonisation and state formation.
  • Compare historiographical interpretations and support claims with traceable evidence.
  • Write coherent historical essays, source commentaries, research proposals and appropriately referenced projects.

Diploma History Notes

Level focus: introductory disciplinary knowledge, broad historical surveys, source literacy and practical communication. Learners should be able to explain major developments accurately and support descriptions with examples.

Diploma · Module 1

Introduction to the study of History

1.1 Meaning and scope

History studies the human past through critically examined evidence. It includes political institutions, economic systems, social relations, religion, technology, environment, migration, ideas and everyday life. Political history remains important, but a complete account also asks how ordinary people, workers, women, children, minorities and communities experienced events.

1.2 Key concepts

Chronology: the arrangement of events in time. Periodisation: dividing the past into useful periods for analysis. Continuity: features that persist over time. Change: transformations in institutions, practices or relationships. Causation: the factors that help explain why an event occurred. Historical significance: the importance assigned to an event because of its consequences, scale, symbolism or influence.

Period labels such as “ancient”, “medieval” and “modern” are analytical conventions. They do not describe every society equally well. For example, the European label “Middle Ages” should not be used as though African, Asian and American societies experienced the same chronology or institutions.

1.3 Why study History?

  • To understand the origins and development of institutions, borders, laws and social identities.
  • To evaluate claims about the past using evidence rather than unsupported memory or propaganda.
  • To understand how historical processes shape present inequalities, conflicts and opportunities.
  • To develop research, communication, comparison and critical-thinking skills.

1.4 Historical explanation

A strong explanation separates immediate triggers from deeper structural conditions. A revolution, for example, may have an immediate political trigger, but its explanation may also involve taxation, inequality, food prices, state debt, elite conflict, political ideas and organisation. Avoid explaining complex events through a single cause unless the evidence justifies that claim.

Historical question → locate evidence → evaluate source and context → compare explanations → build an argument → cite evidence

Check your understanding

  1. Define history as an academic discipline.
  2. Distinguish chronology from periodisation.
  3. Explain the difference between continuity and change.
  4. Give two reasons why a single-cause explanation may be inadequate.
Diploma · Module 2

Historical sources and evidence

2.1 Primary, secondary and tertiary sources

A primary source is evidence produced in the period or context being investigated, or by a participant or witness relevant to the question. Examples include letters, treaties, court records, oral testimony, photographs, archaeological objects, newspapers, census returns and government files. A secondary source is a later scholarly interpretation based on evidence, such as a journal article or history monograph. A tertiary source compiles or summarises information, such as an encyclopaedia or reference chronology.

These labels depend on the research question. A newspaper may be a primary source for studying public rhetoric in a particular year, but a secondary source for an event it reports retrospectively. Classify sources in relation to the question, not by format alone.

2.2 The source-criticism questions

Who?

Identify author, creator, institution, intended audience and social position.

When and where?

Establish date, location, production circumstances and historical context.

Why?

Investigate purpose, interests, intended effect and possible bias.

What is missing?

Identify silence, exclusions, uncertain claims and voices not represented.

2.3 Reliability and usefulness

Reliability is not the same as usefulness. A colonial administrator's report may contain inaccurate assumptions about local communities, yet remain valuable evidence of administrative priorities and official language. A personal letter may reveal emotion and everyday experience, but it may not represent the wider population. Corroborate claims by comparing independent sources and considering how each was produced.

Worked source analysis

Hypothetical source: a district officer reports that a new tax was “welcomed” by local residents. Do not accept the statement as a neutral description. Ask whether the officer understood local languages, how “welcome” was measured, whether dissent was recorded, who was consulted, and whether tax registers, petitions, oral histories or contemporaneous letters support the claim. The report is evidence of the officer's account; further evidence is needed to establish community responses.

Practice

  1. Classify an 1890 treaty, a 2020 journal article about it and a modern encyclopaedia entry.
  2. Explain why an eyewitness account may still be incomplete.
  3. List four questions you would ask before quoting a historical photograph.
Diploma · Module 3

Early societies, states and long-distance exchange

Human societies developed varied political and economic systems in response to local environments, technologies, belief systems, trade opportunities and social organisation. Early states emerged in several world regions; their development was neither simultaneous nor identical.

3.1 Agriculture, settlement and social complexity

In several regions, the domestication of plants and animals supported more settled communities, population growth and food storage. Surplus production could support specialists, trade, taxation and political authority. However, agriculture did not automatically create states, cities or social equality. Pastoral, fishing, hunting and mobile communities remained historically significant and often interacted with settled populations.

3.2 African historical foundations

African history includes diverse societies and long-distance connections. Ancient Egypt and Nubian/Kushite states developed along the Nile. Aksum connected the Horn of Africa with Red Sea and Indian Ocean networks. Swahili coastal towns participated in Indian Ocean trade, linking African hinterlands with Arabia, Persia, India and beyond. In West Africa, Ghana, Mali and Songhai were among the major states associated with trans-Saharan commerce, political authority and scholarship. These examples should be studied in their specific contexts rather than treated as one uniform African experience.

3.3 Trade and cultural exchange

Trade routes moved more than commodities. They also carried languages, technologies, religious traditions, artistic styles, diplomatic practices and knowledge. The trans-Saharan routes connected North Africa with western and central regions of the continent. Indian Ocean networks linked ports and inland producers across eastern Africa, Arabia, South Asia and Southeast Asia. Exchange was shaped by merchants, rulers, sailors, artisans, religious scholars and local communities.

Production and specialisation ↔ markets and trade ↔ taxation and political power ↔ urban growth and cultural exchange

Key terms

State: a political organisation exercising authority over a population and territory. Empire: a political formation governing multiple peoples or territories, often through unequal relationships. Urbanisation: growth of towns and cities. Commercial network: connected routes and institutions through which goods, capital and information move.

Diploma · Module 4

Revolutions and the making of the modern world

Revolutions are major episodes of rapid political or social transformation. Their outcomes are shaped by long-term tensions, institutional weaknesses, economic pressures, political ideas, organisation and contingent events.

4.1 The Atlantic revolutions

The American Revolution (1775–1783) resulted in independence from British rule and the creation of a republic, while slavery persisted and political rights remained restricted for many people. The French Revolution beginning in 1789 challenged monarchy and privilege, advanced ideas of citizenship and popular sovereignty, and also involved violence, war and changing political regimes. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), led by enslaved and formerly enslaved people, destroyed French colonial slavery in Saint-Domingue and established Haiti as an independent state in 1804. These revolutions shared connections but had distinct social foundations and consequences.

4.2 Industrialisation

Industrialisation involved changes in production, energy use, transport, labour organisation, finance and urban life. Mechanised production expanded first in Britain and later in other regions, but industrial change was uneven and depended on resources, capital, institutions, imperial relationships, technology and labour. It created new forms of wealth and productivity while also producing dangerous working conditions, urban crowding, environmental damage and new class relations.

4.3 How to compare revolutions

Compare causes, social groups involved, leadership, political ideas, economic structures, violence, international influences and long-term outcomes. Avoid assuming that a revolution is successful or unsuccessful on one measure alone. Constitutional change, social equality, economic redistribution and national independence may move in different directions.

Diploma · Module 5

Colonialism, resistance and independence

Colonialism refers to systems in which an external power establishes and maintains political control over another territory or population. It took different forms across time and place. European imperial expansion in Africa accelerated during the late nineteenth century, although African societies had long histories of state formation, diplomacy, commerce and resistance before colonial conquest.

5.1 The Scramble for Africa

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885 established rules among European powers for claims and commercial access in Africa; it did not itself draw every modern African boundary or represent African consent. Subsequent conquest, treaties, military campaigns and administrative arrangements extended colonial rule unevenly. Local rulers and communities responded through diplomacy, accommodation, armed resistance, migration, petitioning and other strategies.

5.2 Colonial systems and their effects

Colonial administrations varied, but commonly sought revenue, labour, land, raw materials and strategic control. Taxation, cash-crop production, labour recruitment, infrastructure and legal changes reshaped many communities. Railways and ports could facilitate mobility and trade while also serving extraction and military control. Effects differed by region, class, gender, ecology and local political conditions.

5.3 Resistance and nationalism

Resistance included armed struggles, religious movements, strikes, boycotts, petitions, cultural preservation, political associations and everyday acts of refusal. Nationalist movements developed through changing combinations of urban associations, trade unions, veterans, students, intellectuals, rural organisations, religious networks and international developments. Independence processes differed: some involved negotiated transfers, others prolonged wars or settler conflict.

5.4 Decolonisation

Decolonisation is the process through which colonial rule ends and political sovereignty is established. Formal independence did not automatically remove economic dependency, unequal landholding, inherited borders, institutional weaknesses or global power inequalities. Historians therefore examine both the achievements of independence movements and the constraints faced by new states.

Undergraduate History Notes

Level focus: conceptual depth, comparative history, historiography, sustained argument and independent source evaluation. Undergraduate essays should move beyond narrative to explain how and why historical interpretations differ.

Undergraduate · Module 1

Historiography and historical interpretation

1.1 What is historiography?

Historiography is the study of how history has been written and interpreted. It examines historians' questions, methods, evidence, conceptual frameworks, institutional settings and debates. It is not simply a list of historians. A historiographical essay identifies competing explanations, explains why they differ and evaluates how evidence supports each interpretation.

1.2 Major approaches

  • Political and diplomatic history: investigates states, leaders, institutions, wars, treaties and diplomacy.
  • Social history: studies social groups, class, family, labour, education, health and everyday life.
  • Economic history: examines production, exchange, markets, finance, taxation, labour and material living standards.
  • Marxist approaches: analyse class relations, modes of production, material conditions and conflict, while historians debate how much explanatory weight these factors should carry.
  • Annales-inspired approaches: often emphasise long-term structures, geography, demography and material life alongside short-term events.
  • Postcolonial approaches: examine how colonial power shaped knowledge, categories, institutions and representations, and how formerly colonised societies contest these legacies.
  • Gender history: analyses how gender relations and ideas about masculinity, femininity and sexuality shape historical institutions and experiences.
  • Environmental history: investigates interactions between human societies, climate, landscapes, disease, animals and ecological change.

1.3 Building a historiographical argument

First define the historical problem. Next identify the main interpretations and the evidence each uses. Then compare their assumptions, scale, methods and explanatory strengths. Finally, state a reasoned position while acknowledging unresolved questions. Do not caricature an approach or assume that one school explains every event.

Example debate: Was a particular independence movement driven primarily by economic grievances, political organisation, ideological nationalism or international pressure? A strong answer tests how these factors interacted and whether their importance changed over time.
Undergraduate · Module 2

African history: state formation, commerce and social change

2.1 Studying African history

African history draws on written records, oral traditions, archaeology, linguistics, environmental evidence, material culture and visual sources. Written archives are important but uneven: many were created by states, merchants, missionaries, travellers or colonial administrations. Historians therefore compare genres and seek evidence from communities whose experiences were under-recorded.

2.2 State formation and political authority

Political authority in African societies has taken varied forms: centralised kingdoms and empires, city-states, federations, lineage-based systems, pastoral polities and decentralised communities. Authority could rest on control of trade, land, tribute, military power, religious legitimacy, kinship, age-grade institutions or negotiated alliances. Avoid treating centralisation as the only measure of political complexity.

2.3 Commerce and regional networks

Trans-Saharan trade connected North and West Africa through routes that moved gold, salt and other goods, as well as scholars, pilgrims and ideas. The Mali Empire's influence was connected to political authority and commercial networks, but its power changed over time and across space. In eastern Africa, Swahili coastal communities were African societies shaped by local histories and Indian Ocean connections. Their urban culture cannot be reduced to external influence alone.

2.4 Slavery and the slave trades

Slavery existed in different forms in many historical societies. The Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans across the ocean and became integral to plantation economies and Atlantic commercial systems. The trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean slave trades had different routes, chronologies, institutions and demographic effects. Historians distinguish these systems rather than collapsing them into a single undifferentiated process. Study coercion, resistance, family separation, gender, racialisation, abolition and long-term social consequences.

2.5 Evidence-led case study method

For a case study of a state or trading society, define the period and geographical scope; identify the political and economic question; compare oral, archaeological and written evidence; examine how later national or colonial narratives shaped interpretation; and explain the limits of the surviving record.

Undergraduate · Module 3

Colonial rule, resistance and the postcolonial state

3.1 Colonial governance

Colonial governance was not a uniform system. Administrations used combinations of direct bureaucratic control, alliances with local authorities, settler institutions, customary courts, missionary networks and commercial companies. The label “indirect rule” should be tested against local evidence: colonial officials often transformed the authority and meaning of institutions they claimed merely to preserve.

3.2 Political economy

Colonial economies were shaped by export agriculture, mining, taxation, labour migration, transport infrastructure and unequal access to land and capital. Analyse who controlled production, who bore costs, how profits were distributed and how local households adapted. Infrastructure may have connected regions while prioritising extraction or strategic movement.

3.3 Resistance and collaboration

People made strategic choices under unequal conditions. Resistance and collaboration were not always fixed identities; individuals and communities could negotiate, petition, refuse, migrate, fight or cooperate at different moments. Analyse the constraints and choices available rather than imposing simple moral categories without context.

3.4 Nationalism and independence

Nationalist politics drew on ideas of sovereignty, citizenship, self-determination and social justice. Movements were internally diverse and sometimes contested over class, ethnicity, religion, language, regional interests and political strategy. International developments—including the world wars, anti-colonial movements, the United Nations and Cold War politics—affected the context but did not replace local agency.

3.5 Postcolonial state formation

New governments inherited administrative borders and institutions created under colonial rule, but they also made choices and pursued distinct projects. Historical analysis considers nation-building, constitutional design, public services, land, military power, development strategies, opposition, regional diplomacy and global economic relationships. Avoid treating post-independence outcomes as inevitable consequences of colonialism alone.

Undergraduate · Module 4

Global history, empire and connected worlds

Global history studies connections and comparisons across regions. It asks how people, commodities, diseases, technologies, capital, religions and ideas moved, and how these movements changed local societies. It should not erase local differences or assume that global integration benefited all groups equally.

4.1 Empire and imperial systems

Empires have used military conquest, tribute, settlement, commercial privilege, diplomacy, legal hierarchy and cultural claims to govern diverse territories. Compare how empires negotiated with local elites, managed distance, collected revenue and legitimised rule. Distinguish formal territorial empire from informal influence through finance, trade or military pressure.

4.2 Industrial capitalism and global inequality

Industrialisation, finance, commodity production and imperial expansion were connected in complex ways. Historians debate the relative importance of domestic institutions, energy, technology, labour, resources, trade, slavery and imperial relationships. A strong answer identifies mechanisms and evidence rather than relying on slogans.

4.3 World wars and international order

The First World War (1914–1918) and Second World War (1939–1945) transformed states, societies, economies and international institutions. Their causes involved alliances, imperial rivalries, nationalism, militarism, economic crises, expansionist ideologies and specific political decisions. Examine both global military events and the experiences of colonised peoples, civilians, workers and displaced communities.

4.4 Comparative essay framework

Choose a clearly bounded question; define comparison criteria; establish similarities and differences; explain the historical conditions behind them; and evaluate what the comparison reveals. Do not compare societies as though they were identical units or assume that a development in one region must be the model for another.

Undergraduate · Module 5

Historical writing and evidence-based argument

A history essay advances a defensible answer to a question. The introduction defines the issue, establishes scope and presents a thesis. Body paragraphs develop one analytical point each, support it with evidence and explain its relevance. The conclusion answers the question and identifies the significance and limits of the argument.

Example of analytical paragraph structure

Claim: state the paragraph's main analytical point. Evidence: identify the source, event, data or scholarly interpretation supporting it. Analysis: explain what the evidence demonstrates and what it cannot establish. Link: connect the point to the essay question and overall thesis.

Referencing and quotation

Use the referencing system required by your department (for example Chicago notes and bibliography, APA or another specified style). Cite quotations, paraphrases, statistics, maps, archival documents and distinctive interpretations. Record full bibliographic details when you first consult a source. Never invent a citation or quote a work you have not checked.

Postgraduate History Notes

Level focus: advanced historiographical debate, original research design, archival and digital methods, ethical judgement and contribution to scholarly discussion. Master's and doctoral requirements differ; doctoral work normally requires a clearly justified original contribution.

Postgraduate · Module 1

Advanced historiography and philosophy of history

Postgraduate historiography investigates how historical knowledge is constructed, authorised, challenged and revised. It examines the relationship between evidence and interpretation, the role of concepts, the politics of archives, narrative form, scale and the limits of historical explanation.

1.1 Evidence does not interpret itself

Documents are produced within institutions and relationships of power. Archives preserve some records and lose or exclude others. Researchers must therefore ask why a record exists, whose work it served, how it was classified and what forms of experience it cannot capture. Archival silence is not proof that an event did not occur; it is a research problem requiring careful corroboration.

1.2 Objectivity, positionality and reflexivity

Historical objectivity is best treated as a disciplined commitment to transparent methods, accurate representation, fair engagement with evidence and openness to correction—not as a claim that researchers have no standpoint. Reflexivity requires researchers to explain how their assumptions, language, institutional position, access and relationships may shape research decisions.

1.3 Scale and explanation

Microhistory can reveal the complexity of a local event or individual life; macrohistory examines large-scale structures and long-term patterns. Transnational and global approaches follow connections across borders. Each scale makes some questions visible and others harder to see. Justify the scale in relation to the research question.

1.4 Evaluating a scholarly debate

Map the debate's central propositions, source bases, methods and chronology. Identify whether scholars disagree about facts, causation, definitions, scale or moral interpretation. Evaluate what new evidence or method could clarify the disagreement. A postgraduate review should synthesise scholarship, not merely list authors one by one.

Postgraduate · Module 2

Advanced research design and archival methods

2.1 From topic to researchable question

A topic such as “colonial education” is too broad for a dissertation. A researchable question specifies a population or institution, place, period, process and analytical problem. For example: “How did mission and colonial administrative priorities shape access to teacher training in [defined territory] between [specified years]?” The bracketed scope must be filled using the actual project and available evidence.

2.2 Research design sequence

Research problem → literature review → research question → conceptual framework → source strategy → methods and ethics → analysis → argument → dissemination

Each stage should align. A question about institutional policy may require administrative records and legislative sources; a question about memory may require oral histories and analysis of commemorative practice. Explain why selected sources can answer the question and what they cannot show.

2.3 Archives and provenance

Record repository, collection, series, file reference, document title, creator, date and folio or page where available. Preserve the chain between a claim and the item that supports it. Check whether a document is an original, copy, translation, extract or later transcription. Follow repository rules and copyright restrictions.

2.4 Oral history

Oral history can document experience, memory, interpretation and knowledge absent from written archives. It is not a transparent recording of the past: memory changes, interviews are shaped by questions and relationships, and later events influence recollection. Obtain informed consent, agree recording and quotation conditions, protect confidentiality where required and store recordings securely. Follow institutional ethics approval before recruitment.

2.5 Digital and quantitative methods

Digitised archives, text analysis, GIS, network analysis and databases can reveal patterns across large collections. Digital results depend on metadata quality, OCR accuracy, sampling, classification and software choices. Quantitative patterns require historical interpretation; a map or graph is not self-explanatory. Document data cleaning, exclusions, uncertainty and reproducible steps.

Postgraduate · Module 3

Comparative, transnational and postcolonial research

Comparative history examines similarities and differences between carefully selected cases. Transnational history follows relationships and movements that cross political borders. Postcolonial research investigates the afterlives of colonial power in institutions, knowledge systems, economies, identities and cultural representation.

3.1 Designing a defensible comparison

Explain why cases are comparable, define common analytical dimensions and identify differences in source availability. Avoid selecting cases only because they confirm a preferred argument. Consider whether apparent similarity results from direct connection, parallel development or coincidence.

3.2 Avoiding methodological nationalism

National borders are often useful units of analysis, but they can hide migration, trade, religious networks, ecological systems and political relationships that cross borders. A transnational design may reveal those connections while still accounting for state institutions and local experience.

3.3 Knowledge, power and representation

Examine how maps, censuses, ethnographic categories, museums, school curricula and official histories classified populations and territories. Analyse how communities adopted, negotiated or challenged these categories. Do not assume that all colonised people experienced or interpreted colonial rule in the same way.

Postgraduate · Module 4

Research ethics, thesis construction and scholarly publication

4.1 Research ethics

Historical research can involve living interviewees, sensitive personal data, contested heritage, vulnerable communities and restricted records. Address informed consent, privacy, data security, risks of harm, respectful representation, community expectations and the right to withdraw where applicable. Obtain institutional review before collecting data when required.

4.2 Thesis architecture

  1. Introduction: problem, question, scope, rationale, contribution and chapter map.
  2. Literature review: critical synthesis of scholarship and the research gap.
  3. Conceptual and methodological framework: concepts, sources, methods, limitations and ethics.
  4. Analytical chapters: evidence-led findings organised around the argument.
  5. Conclusion: answer to the question, contribution, limitations and future research.
  6. References and appendices: complete traceable sources and relevant supporting material.

4.3 Contribution and originality

Originality may come from new evidence, a new interpretation, a different comparison, a neglected population, a revised chronology or a methodological contribution. A claim of originality must be demonstrated through a rigorous literature review and a transparent account of what the project adds.

4.4 Peer review and publication

Prepare a clear abstract, explain the question and contribution, use a consistent citation system and ensure that claims match evidence. Respond to peer review by addressing substantive concerns and explaining justified disagreements professionally. Never fabricate sources, alter evidence deceptively or submit another person's work as your own.

Historical research methods: practical guide

Developing a research question

A good research question is focused, historically significant, answerable with accessible evidence and open to analysis. It should not merely ask for a description. “What happened during X?” may be a starting point; “How and why did different groups experience X differently, and what evidence explains those differences?” invites historical investigation.

Evaluating evidence systematically

DimensionQuestions for the researcherWhy it matters
ProvenanceWho created the item, when, where and under what conditions?Establishes origin and context.

Academic essay checklist

  • Answer the exact wording of the question.
  • Define key terms and specify the time and geographical scope.
  • Present a clear thesis rather than a list of events.
  • Use primary evidence and relevant scholarship appropriately.
  • Compare interpretations and explain why they differ.
  • Support claims with accurate citations and page references.
  • Conclude with a direct answer and acknowledge limitations.

Revision questions and marking guidance

These are original practice questions for learning and self-assessment. They are not official past examination papers from any university.

1. (Diploma) Define history and explain four reasons for studying it.
Marking guide: 2 marks for a sound definition; 4 marks for four explained reasons. Credit evidence-based understanding, not memorised slogans.
2. (Diploma) Distinguish primary and secondary sources, giving two examples of each.
Marking guide: Correct distinction (2); relevant examples (4). Explain that classification depends partly on the research question.
3. (Diploma) Explain three ways long-distance trade influenced societies in African history.
Marking guide: Up to 2 marks each for developed points such as movement of goods, state revenue, urban growth, religious and intellectual exchange, technology or migration. Use historically appropriate examples.
4. (Undergraduate) “Colonial rule transformed African economies, but its effects were uneven.” Discuss with reference to two regions or case studies.
Marking guide: Thesis and scope (3); accurate contextual evidence from two cases (6); comparison of labour, land, taxation, production or infrastructure (6); analysis of variation and local agency (3); conclusion and referencing (2).
5. (Undergraduate) Compare the causes and consequences of the French and Haitian Revolutions.
Marking guide: Explain shared Atlantic context while distinguishing social structures, slavery, race, political goals, actors and outcomes. Avoid presenting Haiti as merely an extension of events in France.
6. (Undergraduate) Evaluate the usefulness and limitations of oral testimony as historical evidence.
Marking guide: Discuss lived experience, memory, retrospective interpretation, interviewer influence, representativeness, ethics and corroboration.
7. (Postgraduate) Explain how archival silences can shape historical interpretation. Propose a method for addressing them.
Marking guide: Define archival silence; analyse institutional creation and preservation of records; distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence; propose triangulation using oral, material, visual, private or alternative archives; acknowledge limits and ethics.
8. (Postgraduate) Design a comparative research question on decolonisation and outline the evidence and methods required to answer it.
Marking guide: Focused question and justified cases; comparative dimensions; source strategy; method; feasibility; positionality and ethical considerations; limitations.
9. (Postgraduate) What constitutes an original contribution in a doctoral history thesis?
Marking guide: Explain possible contributions through new sources, interpretation, method, comparison, neglected experiences or conceptual reframing; show how a literature review establishes the contribution.
10. (All levels) Why should historians distinguish historical explanation from moral judgement?
Marking guide: A strong answer explains that ethical evaluation may be relevant, but it does not replace contextual analysis, source criticism or causal explanation. Historians should make values explicit and avoid anachronism while recognising historical harms.

Suggested essay and project titles

  • Trade, political authority and cultural exchange in a selected African state.
  • Oral tradition and archaeology as complementary sources for reconstructing the past.
  • Labour, taxation and the colonial political economy in a selected territory.
  • Women, gender and social transformation in a defined historical period.
  • Memory, monuments and contested public history after independence.
  • Comparative approaches to nationalism and decolonisation in two countries.

Further reading and responsible research

Use your university library catalogue and scholarly databases to locate peer-reviewed books and articles. Useful starting points include the JSTOR research platform, WorldCat library catalogue, and Library of Congress collections. Check access conditions and the suitability of each item for your topic. Consult national archives, local repositories, oral-history collections and relevant specialist journals where appropriate.

CBE Nexus materials are independent educational resources, not official university publications or a substitute for prescribed readings, academic supervision or institutional assessment requirements. Always verify historical claims against traceable scholarly and primary sources.
