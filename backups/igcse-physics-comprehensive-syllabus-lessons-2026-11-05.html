TOPIC 1

1. Motion, forces and energy

1.1 Physical quantities and motion

Measure distance in metres (m), time in seconds (s), speed in metres per second (m/s) and acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²). Distance is the total path travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position in a specified direction. Speed is scalar; velocity is speed in a stated direction.

average speed = total distance ÷ total time

acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken

On a distance–time graph, gradient represents speed. A horizontal section means the object is stationary. On a speed–time graph, gradient represents acceleration and area under the graph represents distance travelled (for a speed–time graph).

Worked example: A runner covers 150 m in 12 s. Average speed = 150 ÷ 12 = 12.5 m/s.

1.2 Mass, weight and density

Mass is the amount of matter and is measured in kilograms (kg). Weight is the gravitational force on a mass, measured in newtons (N). W = mg, where g is gravitational field strength in N/kg. Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V. Convert units before substituting into equations.

Worked example: A 2.0 kg object has weight 19.6 N where g = 9.8 N/kg. W = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N.

1.3 Forces and their effects

A force can change an object's speed, direction or shape. Resultant force is the vector sum of all forces. If resultant force is zero, an object remains at rest or continues at constant velocity. Newton's second law: F = ma. Friction and air resistance oppose relative motion. At terminal velocity, drag balances weight, so resultant force and acceleration are zero.

Momentum is mass × velocity: p = mv. In a closed system, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it, provided external resultant force is negligible. Impulse equals change in momentum: FΔt = Δp.

1.4 Turning effects, pressure and moments

The moment of a force about a pivot is force × perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action. For equilibrium, clockwise moments equal anticlockwise moments. Pressure = force ÷ area. In liquids, pressure increases with depth and density: p = ρgh.

1.5 Energy, work and power

Energy is conserved: it cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between stores. Kinetic energy: Eₖ = ½mv². Gravitational potential energy change: ΔEₚ = mgΔh. Work done: W = Fd when force acts along displacement. Power is rate of energy transfer: P = E/t.

Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%. Some energy is dissipated to the surroundings, often by heating or sound.

Worked example: A 4 kg trolley moves at 3 m/s. Eₖ = ½ × 4 × 3² = 18 J. If lifted 2 m where g = 10 N/kg, its gain in GPE = 4 × 10 × 2 = 80 J.

1.6 Pressure in gases and fluids

Gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with container walls. Increasing temperature at constant volume increases average particle kinetic energy and pressure. Hydraulic systems transmit pressure through an enclosed liquid; pressure is transmitted equally in all directions (Pascal's principle).

1.2 Scalars, vectors and measurement

A scalar has magnitude only (mass, time, distance, speed, energy and temperature). A vector has magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and weight). Represent a vector by an arrow: its length represents magnitude and its arrowhead gives direction. When vectors act along one straight line, choose a positive direction and add signed values. For perpendicular vectors, use a scale drawing or Pythagoras where appropriate.

Measure length with a ruler or metre rule, time with a stopwatch or electronic timer, and small diameters with vernier calipers or a micrometer. Read analogue scales at eye level to avoid parallax. Record the instrument resolution and repeat measurements to reduce random error.

1.3 Distance–time and speed–time graphs

On a distance–time graph, the gradient is speed. A horizontal line means the object is stationary; a steeper gradient means greater speed. On a speed–time graph, the gradient is acceleration and the area under the graph is distance travelled. For a velocity–time graph, signed area gives displacement.

Worked example: A cyclist travels 180 m in 12 s. Average speed = distance ÷ time = 180 ÷ 12 = 15 m/s. If the cyclist then travels 60 m in 8 s, total average speed = (180 + 60) ÷ (12 + 8) = 12 m/s.

1.4 Acceleration and free fall

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time: a = (v − u)/t. A negative value indicates acceleration opposite to the chosen positive direction; it does not automatically mean the object is slowing down. Near Earth's surface, freely falling objects have gravitational acceleration g, approximately 9.8 m/s² (often taken as 10 m/s² when instructed). Air resistance increases with speed until it balances weight; the resultant force then becomes zero and terminal velocity is reached.

Worked example: A car increases velocity from 5 m/s to 23 m/s in 6 s. a = (23 − 5)/6 = 3.0 m/s².

1.5 Mass, weight and gravitational field strength

Mass measures inertia and is measured in kilograms using a balance. Weight is the gravitational force on a mass and is measured in newtons using a force meter. W = mg, where g is gravitational field strength in N/kg. Mass remains the same when an object moves to another planet; weight changes if gravitational field strength changes.

Worked example: A 4.5 kg bag is on Earth where g = 9.8 N/kg. W = 4.5 × 9.8 = 44.1 N.

1.6 Density and pressure

Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V. For a regular solid, calculate volume from measured dimensions. For an irregular object, use water displacement in a measuring cylinder or overflow can. Convert units before substitution: 1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³ and 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Pressure is force per unit area, p = F/A. The same force produces greater pressure on a smaller contact area. In a liquid, pressure increases with depth and density: p = ρgh (pressure due to the liquid column).

Worked example: A block has mass 540 g and volume 200 cm³. Density = 540/200 = 2.7 g/cm³ = 2700 kg/m³.

1.7 Turning effects, equilibrium and stability

The moment of a force about a pivot is force × perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action. For an object in equilibrium, resultant force is zero and total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment about any pivot. The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole weight may be considered to act. A lower centre of gravity and wider base generally increase stability. An object topples when the vertical line through its centre of gravity falls outside its base.

Worked example: A 20 N force acts perpendicularly 0.30 m from a pivot. Moment = 20 × 0.30 = 6.0 N m. To balance it at 0.60 m, the opposing force is 6.0/0.60 = 10 N.

1.8 Momentum and collisions

Momentum p = mv. In a closed system with no resultant external force, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it. Impulse equals change in momentum and is also force × time for a constant resultant force. Increasing collision time (for example with crumple zones, airbags or padding) reduces average force for the same momentum change.

Worked example: A 0.15 kg ball moving at 12 m/s is stopped. Change in momentum = 0 − (0.15 × 12) = −1.8 kg m/s. If it stops in 0.06 s, average force = −1.8/0.06 = −30 N; the negative sign shows the force opposes the initial motion.

1.9 Energy stores, transfers, work and power

Energy may be stored chemically, kinetically, gravitationally, elastically, internally, in magnetic fields, in electric fields or in nuclear stores. Energy is transferred mechanically, electrically, by heating or by radiation. Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. Kinetic energy = ½mv²; change in gravitational potential energy = mgΔh; elastic potential energy for a spring obeying Hooke's law = ½Fx. Power is energy transferred or work done per unit time, P = E/t = W/t. Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%.

Worked example: A 1200 kg car travels at 20 m/s. KE = ½ × 1200 × 20² = 240 000 J. If it rises 5 m, its GPE gain (g = 10 N/kg) = 1200 × 10 × 5 = 60 000 J.

1.10 Springs and Hooke's law

For a spring within its limit of proportionality, extension is directly proportional to applied force: F = kx, where k is the spring constant in N/m and x is extension in metres. Extension is the stretched length minus the original length. Beyond the limit of proportionality, force and extension are no longer directly proportional; beyond the elastic limit, the spring may not return to its original length.

1.11 Pressure in fluids and upthrust

Fluid pressure acts in all directions and increases with depth. A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of fluid displaced (Archimedes' principle). An object floats when upthrust balances its weight. If its average density is less than the fluid density it can float; if greater, it tends to sink. Atmospheric pressure is caused by the weight of air and decreases with altitude.

Mechanics practical skills

For a trolley investigation, measure distance with a metre rule and time with light gates or a ticker timer. For a density experiment, measure mass on a balance and volume by dimensions or displacement. For moments, measure perpendicular distances from the pivot. Identify control variables, repeat readings, plot a graph with units and use its gradient where the relationship requires it.

1.12 Resultant vectors and measurement accuracy

For two perpendicular vectors, draw them head-to-tail and use Pythagoras for the resultant magnitude: R = √(A² + B²). The direction can be found using tan θ = opposite/adjacent. For small lengths or times, measure several repeated units and divide by the number (for example, time 20 oscillations then divide by 20). This reduces the percentage uncertainty in a single short measurement. Use the correct instrument: ruler for length, measuring cylinder for liquid volume, balance for mass and digital timer for time.

Worked example: Two perpendicular forces are 6 N east and 8 N north. R = √(6² + 8²) = 10 N. θ = tan⁻¹(8/6) = 53° north of east.

1.13 Friction, drag and circular motion

Friction acts between surfaces in contact and opposes relative motion or its tendency. It may produce heating and wear. Drag acts on an object moving through a fluid (liquid or gas); it increases with speed and depends on shape and exposed area. Streamlined shapes reduce drag. For an object moving in a circle, its velocity changes because its direction changes even if speed is constant. A resultant force acts towards the centre, perpendicular to instantaneous motion. At constant mass and radius, greater speed requires greater centripetal force; at constant mass and speed, a smaller radius requires greater force. Do not use a centripetal-force equation unless supplied/required.

1.14 Energy resources and generation

In a fossil-fuel or biofuel power station, chemical energy heats water; steam turns a turbine connected to a generator. Hydroelectricity uses gravitational potential energy of stored water; tidal and wave systems use movement of seawater. Wind turns turbine blades. Geothermal systems use energy from hot rocks underground. Nuclear fission heats coolant/water to drive a turbine. Solar cells transfer light energy directly to electrical energy; solar thermal panels use radiation to heat water. A generator converts kinetic energy into electrical energy by electromagnetic induction.

Compare resources by renewability, reliability, availability, scale, cost and environmental impact. Fossil fuels are dispatchable but release carbon dioxide and pollutants. Wind and solar have low operating emissions but are intermittent. Hydroelectric dams can respond rapidly but affect habitats and communities. Nuclear power has high output and low operational carbon emissions but produces radioactive waste and requires rigorous safety. Geothermal is location-dependent. Biofuels are renewable if managed sustainably but require land and may compete with food production.

1.15 Sankey diagrams and efficiency

A Sankey diagram shows energy transfers; arrow width represents energy quantity. Useful output continues in the forward direction while wasted energy branches away. Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input × 100%, using energy or power consistently.

Worked example: A motor receives 500 J and transfers 350 J usefully. Efficiency = 350/500 × 100% = 70%; 150 J is dissipated to the surroundings.

1.16 Pressure in liquids

Liquid pressure increases with depth and density. The pressure difference between two depths is Δp = ρgΔh. This is pressure due to the liquid and does not include atmospheric pressure. A dam is made thicker at its base because water pressure is greater at greater depth.

Worked example: Water density = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 N/kg, depth = 3.0 m. Δp = 1000 × 9.8 × 3.0 = 29 400 Pa.

2. Thermal physics

2.1 Kinetic particle model

Solids have particles vibrating about fixed positions; liquids have particles close together that move past one another; gases have particles far apart moving rapidly and randomly. Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of particles. Internal energy is the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy of the particles.

2.2 Thermal expansion and temperature

Most substances expand when heated because particles vibrate or move more and their average separation increases. Temperature scales include Celsius and kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273 (approximately). A thermometer must be suitable for the temperature range and should reach thermal equilibrium with the object.

2.3 Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). E = mcΔT. Different materials have different specific heat capacities.

Worked example: 0.50 kg of water (c = 4200 J/kg °C) warms by 10 °C. E = 0.50 × 4200 × 10 = 21 000 J.

2.4 Changes of state and latent heat

During melting and boiling, energy changes particle separation and intermolecular potential energy; temperature remains constant during the change of state of a pure substance at constant pressure. Specific latent heat: E = mL. Fusion refers to melting/freezing; vaporisation refers to boiling/condensation.

2.5 Transfer of thermal energy

Conduction transfers energy through particles and (in metals) free electrons. Convection occurs in fluids when warmer, less dense fluid rises and cooler, denser fluid sinks. Infrared radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and does not require a medium. Dull, black surfaces are good absorbers and emitters; shiny, light surfaces are poor absorbers and emitters.

2.6 Gas pressure and temperature

Gas particles exert pressure through collisions with container walls. At constant volume, increasing absolute temperature increases pressure because particles move faster and collide more frequently and with greater momentum change.

EXTENDED LESSONS • PARTICLES AND HEAT

2.2 The kinetic particle model

Matter consists of particles in constant motion. In a solid, particles are closely spaced and vibrate about fixed positions. In a liquid, particles remain close but move past one another. In a gas, particles are widely spaced and move rapidly in random directions. Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of particles. Heating usually increases internal energy; it may increase particle kinetic energy (temperature rises) or potential energy as particles separate during a change of state.

2.3 Melting, boiling, evaporation and condensation

Melting changes a solid to a liquid; freezing is the reverse. Boiling occurs throughout a liquid at its boiling point, while evaporation occurs at the surface and can happen below the boiling point. Evaporation causes cooling because the highest-energy particles escape, lowering the average kinetic energy of those left behind. Condensation changes gas to liquid; sublimation is a direct solid–gas change. During a pure substance's change of state, temperature remains constant while energy changes particle separation and intermolecular potential energy.

2.4 Thermal expansion

Most materials expand when heated because their particles vibrate more strongly and their average separation increases. Expansion joints in bridges and gaps in railway tracks allow for this change. Liquids expand too and this principle is used in liquid-in-glass thermometers. Unequal expansion can bend a bimetallic strip; it is used in thermostats and fire alarms.

2.5 Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). E = mcΔT. A large specific heat capacity means more energy is needed for the same mass and temperature rise. Water's relatively high specific heat capacity helps moderate coastal climates and makes water useful as a coolant.

Worked example: Heat 0.50 kg of water (c = 4200 J/kg °C) by 15 °C. E = 0.50 × 4200 × 15 = 31 500 J.

2.6 Specific latent heat

Specific latent heat L is energy required per kilogram to change state without temperature change. E = mL. Specific latent heat of fusion applies to melting/freezing; specific latent heat of vaporisation applies to boiling/condensing. Vaporisation generally requires much more energy than melting because particles must separate substantially.

Worked example: 0.20 kg of ice melts at 0 °C. If Lf = 334 000 J/kg, E = 0.20 × 334 000 = 66 800 J.

2.7 Conduction, convection and radiation

Conduction transfers energy through a material without bulk movement. In metals, mobile electrons transfer energy quickly; lattice vibrations also contribute. Convection occurs in fluids: heated fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks, setting up a convection current. Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation and can travel through a vacuum. Dull black surfaces are good absorbers and emitters; shiny light surfaces are poor absorbers and emitters and good reflectors.

2.8 Evaporation and cooling investigation

Compare the temperature fall of equal amounts of volatile and less volatile liquids under the same conditions, or compare wet and dry thermometer bulbs. Keep airflow, starting temperature, exposed area and liquid volume controlled. Record temperature at equal time intervals and plot temperature against time. Take care with flammable liquids and follow teacher instructions.

Thermal practical skills and common errors

Use insulation to reduce energy transfer to surroundings, stir water gently for uniform temperature, measure mass and temperature carefully, and repeat trials. In calorimetry, energy supplied by a heater may not all enter the water; explain discrepancies using heat loss, heating of the container and measurement uncertainty.

2.9 Gas pressure and Brownian motion

Gas particles move randomly and collide with container walls. Each collision changes particle momentum and exerts a force; the combined force per unit area is gas pressure. Heating a fixed volume increases average particle kinetic energy, so collisions become more frequent and harder and pressure rises. Compressing a gas at constant temperature reduces volume, causing more frequent collisions with each unit area and increasing pressure. Brownian motion is the irregular motion of visible microscopic particles suspended in a fluid, caused by unequal random collisions from the much smaller, rapidly moving fluid molecules.

2.10 Kelvin temperature and gas laws

Convert Celsius temperature to kelvin using T(K) = θ(°C) + 273. Absolute zero is 0 K (−273 °C approximately), the lowest possible temperature. For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure × volume is constant: pV = constant. Therefore p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. A graph of p against V is a curve; p against 1/V is a straight line through the origin under the stated conditions.

Worked example: A gas occupies 600 cm³ at 100 kPa. It is compressed at constant temperature to 250 cm³. p₂ = p₁V₁/V₂ = 100 × 600/250 = 240 kPa.

2.11 Measuring specific heat capacity

For a solid, measure its mass, insert an electric heater and thermometer into suitable holes, insulate the block, record initial temperature, supply a measured electrical energy E = VIt and record temperature rise Δθ. Calculate c = E/(mΔθ). For a liquid, heat a known mass in an insulated container with an immersion heater and stir gently. Reduce heat loss, measure voltage/current/time accurately and repeat. The main limitations are energy lost to surroundings and energy absorbed by the apparatus.

2.12 Thermal equilibrium and Earth's temperature

An object remains at constant temperature when the rate of energy transfer into it equals the rate out. If it receives energy faster than it loses energy, its temperature rises; if it loses energy faster, its temperature falls. Earth's average temperature depends on the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing infrared radiation. Clouds, greenhouse gases, surface reflectivity (albedo) and atmospheric conditions affect this balance.

2.13 Practical comparison of infrared emitters and absorbers

Use identical metal cans with different surface finishes, fill them with equal volumes of hot water at the same starting temperature and record cooling at equal time intervals. For absorption, expose identical surfaces to the same infrared lamp at the same distance and measure temperature rise. Keep surface area, starting temperature, water volume, lamp distance and exposure time controlled. Dull black surfaces are generally better emitters and absorbers than shiny light surfaces.

3. Waves

3.1 Wave properties

Waves transfer energy without overall transfer of matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to it. Amplitude is maximum displacement from equilibrium; wavelength is distance between corresponding points on adjacent waves; frequency is number of complete waves per second.

wave speed v = frequency f × wavelength λ

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz), wavelength in metres (m), speed in m/s and period T in seconds. T = 1/f.

3.2 Reflection and refraction

Reflection obeys angle of incidence = angle of reflection, measured from the normal. Refraction occurs when wave speed changes at a boundary; frequency remains constant while wavelength changes. Waves entering a slower medium bend towards the normal; entering a faster medium bend away from it.

3.3 Diffraction and interference

Diffraction is spreading of waves at a gap or around an obstacle. It is most noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength. When waves overlap, superposition occurs: displacements add. Constructive interference increases amplitude; destructive interference reduces amplitude.

3.4 Sound

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave and needs a medium. It travels through solids, liquids and gases but not a vacuum. Pitch depends on frequency; loudness relates to amplitude/intensity. Echoes are reflected sound. Ultrasound has frequency above 20 kHz and is used in imaging and distance measurement.

3.5 Electromagnetic spectrum

All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. From longest wavelength/lowest frequency to shortest/highest: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. They differ in wavelength and frequency, not their vacuum speed.

3.6 Lenses

A converging lens brings parallel rays to a principal focus. A diverging lens spreads rays as if from a virtual focus. Ray diagrams use principal axis, optical centre, principal focus and focal length. A converging lens can form real or virtual images depending on object position.

Worked example: A wave has frequency 250 Hz and wavelength 1.2 m. v = fλ = 250 × 1.2 = 300 m/s.

3.2 Wave properties and wave equation

A wave transfers energy without net transfer of matter. Transverse waves vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel; longitudinal waves vibrate parallel to it and contain compressions and rarefactions. Amplitude is maximum displacement from equilibrium, wavelength λ is the distance between successive points in phase, frequency f is oscillations per second and period T = 1/f. Wave speed v = fλ.

Worked example: A wave has frequency 25 Hz and wavelength 1.2 m. v = 25 × 1.2 = 30 m/s. Its period is 1/25 = 0.040 s.

3.3 Reflection of waves

For reflection, angle of incidence equals angle of reflection, measured from the normal. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane. Plane mirrors form virtual, upright images that are the same size as the object and appear the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. Reflection of sound produces echoes; distance to a reflecting surface = speed of sound × echo time ÷ 2.

3.4 Refraction and total internal reflection

Refraction is a change in direction caused by a change in wave speed when a wave crosses a boundary. Frequency remains constant, while wavelength changes. Light entering a more optically dense medium slows and bends towards the normal; leaving it speeds up and bends away. Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle. It is used in optical fibres.

For a ray entering a material, refractive index n = sin i / sin r (for air to material, with angles measured from the normal). At a critical angle c, sin c = 1/n for a material-to-air boundary.

Worked example: Light travels from air into glass with i = 40° and r = 25°. n = sin 40° / sin 25° ≈ 0.643/0.423 = 1.52.

3.5 Lenses and image formation

A converging (convex) lens brings parallel rays to a principal focus; a diverging (concave) lens spreads them as if from a virtual focus. The principal axis passes through the optical centre and principal foci. A real image can be projected on a screen; a virtual image cannot. For a converging lens, an object beyond 2F forms a diminished, inverted real image between F and 2F; at 2F it forms an equal-sized real image at 2F; between F and 2F it forms an enlarged real image beyond 2F; inside F it forms an enlarged, upright virtual image.

Linear magnification = image height ÷ object height. For a thin lens, 1/f = 1/u + 1/v using consistent units and the appropriate sign convention if required by the course. Lens power P = 1/f (f in metres), measured in dioptres.

3.6 Dispersion and electromagnetic spectrum

White light is a mixture of colours. A prism disperses white light because different wavelengths refract by different amounts; violet deviates more than red in glass. The electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency is radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at about 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. As frequency increases, wavelength decreases.

Uses and risks: radio waves for communication; microwaves for satellite links and cooking; infrared for heaters and thermal imaging; visible light for vision and photography; ultraviolet for security marking and sterilisation (overexposure damages skin and eyes); X-rays for imaging (ionising); gamma rays for sterilisation and cancer treatment (ionising). Risk depends on exposure and dose.

3.7 Sound waves

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave and requires a medium; it cannot travel through a vacuum. Pitch depends mainly on frequency; loudness depends on amplitude/intensity. The speed of sound is generally greater in solids than in gases. Ultrasound has frequency above the human audible range (about 20 kHz); uses include medical imaging, cleaning and sonar. In medical ultrasound, pulses reflect at boundaries between tissues and return times are used to locate structures.

3.11 Critical angle and optical fibres

When light travels from glass or another optically denser medium to air, the refracted ray bends away from the normal. At the critical angle c, the refracted ray travels along the boundary. For incidence greater than c, total internal reflection occurs. n = 1/sin c for a material-to-air boundary. Optical fibres guide light by repeated total internal reflection; they carry communication signals over long distances with low loss.

Worked example: A material has refractive index 1.50. sin c = 1/1.50 = 0.667, so c = sin⁻¹(0.667) = 41.8°.

3.12 Electromagnetic communication and signals

Radio waves carry radio/TV broadcasts and are used in astronomy and RFID. Microwaves are used in mobile communication, satellite links and microwave ovens. Infrared is used in remote controls, thermal imaging and some fibre links. Visible light supports vision and photography. Ultraviolet is used in security marking and sterilisation; excessive exposure damages skin and eyes. X-rays image internal structures; gamma rays sterilise equipment and treat some cancers. Microwaves can heat body tissue; infrared can burn skin; UV, X-rays and gamma rays can damage cells. Use protective measures and limit exposure.

Analogue signals vary continuously; digital signals represent information using discrete values, commonly binary. Digital signals can be regenerated more accurately after transmission, reducing accumulated noise and supporting reliable data transfer. Optical fibres use pulses of light to transmit information; microwaves and radio waves support wireless communication.

3.13 Ultrasound applications

Ultrasound has frequency above 20 kHz. Pulses reflect from boundaries between materials or tissues. In medical imaging, short pulses and returning echoes build an image. In sonar, the time between transmission and return gives distance or depth. In non-destructive testing, echoes can reveal cracks inside metal.

Worked example: A sonar pulse returns after 0.12 s in water where sound speed is 1500 m/s. Depth = vt/2 = 1500 × 0.12/2 = 90 m.

4. Electricity and magnetism

4.1 Charge, current and potential difference

Current is rate of flow of charge: I = Q/t. It is measured in amperes (A). Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge: V = E/Q. A complete circuit is needed for steady current. Conventional current flows from positive to negative outside a source; electron flow is opposite.

4.2 Resistance and circuit rules

Ohm's law: for a conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference. V = IR. In series, current is the same everywhere and potential differences add; total resistance is the sum. In parallel, potential difference across each branch is the same and branch currents add. Equivalent resistance for two parallel resistors: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

4.3 Electrical power and safety

Electrical power: P = IV; energy transferred: E = IVt. A fuse melts if current is too large, breaking the circuit. Earthing provides a low-resistance path for fault current. Insulation, correct fuse ratings and avoiding overloaded sockets reduce electric-shock and fire risks.

4.4 Magnetism and electromagnetism

Like magnetic poles repel; unlike poles attract. Magnetic field lines show direction from north to south outside a magnet and are closer where the field is stronger. A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. A solenoid produces a field similar to a bar magnet; an iron core strengthens an electromagnet.

4.5 Electromagnetic induction and transformers

An emf is induced when magnetic flux linkage changes, for example when a magnet moves relative to a coil. Faster movement, stronger magnetic field or more turns can increase induced emf. A transformer uses alternating current and changing magnetic flux in a core to transfer energy between coils. Vₚ/Vₛ = Nₚ/Nₛ for an ideal transformer. Step-up increases voltage; step-down decreases voltage.

Worked example: A 6 Ω resistor is connected across 12 V. I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A; power = IV = 12 × 2 = 24 W.

4.4 Series and parallel circuits

In a series circuit, current is the same at every point, supply potential difference is shared, and total resistance is the sum of resistances. Adding another resistor in parallel reduces the total resistance. Domestic appliances are connected in parallel so each receives the supply voltage and can be switched independently.

4.5 Electrical power and energy

A fuse melts if current becomes too large; it is connected in the live wire and must have a suitable rating. Earthing provides a low-resistance path for fault current and helps protective devices disconnect the supply.

Worked example: A 2.0 kW heater runs for 3.5 h. Energy = 2.0 × 3.5 = 7.0 kWh. At 25 currency units per kWh, cost = 7 × 25 = 175 currency units.

4.6 Electromagnetic effects

A current in a wire produces a magnetic field. Around a straight wire, field lines are concentric circles; direction can be found with the right-hand grip rule. A coil/solenoid produces a field similar to a bar magnet; inserting a soft iron core strengthens an electromagnet. Strength increases with current, number of turns and a suitable core. Electromagnets are used in relays, bells, lifting cranes and circuit breakers.

4.7 Force on a current-carrying conductor and motors

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force when its current is not parallel to the field. The direction is given by Fleming's left-hand rule. Reversing current or magnetic field reverses force direction. In a simple d.c. motor, a coil experiences turning forces; a split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn to keep rotation in the same direction. Increasing current, magnetic field strength or number of turns increases turning effect.

4.8 Electromagnetic induction and transformers

An e.m.f. is induced when the magnetic field linking a conductor or coil changes. The induced e.m.f. is larger when the field changes faster, the magnet/coil moves faster, or the coil has more turns. The induced current direction opposes the change that produces it (Lenz's law). A transformer uses alternating current to create a changing magnetic field in its primary coil; this induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil. For an ideal transformer, Vp/Vs = Np/Ns and VpIp ≈ VsIs. Step-up transformers increase voltage; step-down transformers reduce it.

Worked example: A transformer has 500 primary turns and 100 secondary turns. If Vp = 240 V, Vs = 240 × 100/500 = 48 V.

4.9 Alternating and direct current

Direct current (d.c.) flows in one direction; alternating current (a.c.) repeatedly reverses direction. A.c. is used for mains distribution because transformers can change voltage. High transmission voltage reduces current for a given power and therefore reduces resistive power loss I²R in cables. Never experiment with mains electricity; use low-voltage, current-limited school apparatus under supervision.

4.13 Electrical safety and mains circuits

Mains electricity is dangerous. The live wire is at high potential; neutral completes the circuit and is near earth potential; the earth wire connects exposed metal casing to ground. A switch and fuse are placed in the live wire so switching off or a blown fuse disconnects the appliance from the live supply. A fuse melts when current exceeds its rating; a circuit breaker or residual-current device can disconnect a faulty circuit. Damaged insulation, damp conditions, overloaded sockets and overheating cables can cause shock or fire. Double-insulated appliances have non-conducting outer casings and do not require an earth connection. Never use mains voltage for school experiments.

4.14 A.C. generators and induction

An a.c. generator has a rotating coil in a magnetic field or a rotating magnet near a coil. Changing magnetic flux induces an e.m.f.; slip rings and brushes transfer current from a rotating coil to the external circuit. The induced e.m.f. varies cyclically: it is zero when the coil cuts no magnetic field lines effectively and reaches maxima when the rate of flux cutting is greatest. A faster rotation, stronger field or more turns increases peak induced e.m.f.

4.15 Relays, loudspeakers and transformers

A relay uses a small current in a coil to magnetise an iron core, attracting an armature and operating a separate switch; it allows a low-power circuit to control another circuit. A loudspeaker uses a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field; changing current produces changing force and vibration of a cone, creating sound. A transformer has primary and secondary coils wound on a laminated soft-iron core. Alternating current in the primary creates changing magnetic flux, inducing e.m.f. in the secondary. High-voltage transmission reduces current for a given power, reducing cable loss P = I²R.

Worked example: A transformer supplies 12 V at 4 A from a 240 V supply. For an ideal transformer, input current = (12 × 4)/240 = 0.20 A.

5. Nuclear physics

5.1 Atomic structure and isotopes

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. Proton number is the number of protons; nucleon number is protons plus neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers.

5.2 Radioactivity

Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random. Alpha particles are helium nuclei, beta particles are fast electrons (in beta-minus decay), and gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation. Alpha is strongly ionising but weakly penetrating; beta is moderately ionising and penetrating; gamma is weakly ionising but highly penetrating. Use paper to stop alpha, thin aluminium for beta, and thick lead/concrete to reduce gamma.

5.2 Structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. A proton has relative charge +1 and relative mass 1; a neutron has charge 0 and relative mass 1; an electron has charge −1 and a much smaller relative mass (about 1/1836). In a neutral atom, number of protons equals number of electrons. Proton number (atomic number) identifies the element; nucleon (mass) number is protons plus neutrons. Number of neutrons = nucleon number − proton number.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. They have similar chemical properties but may differ in nuclear stability.

5.3 Discovery and evidence for the nuclear atom

In the alpha-particle scattering experiment, most alpha particles passed through thin metal foil, showing atoms are mostly empty space. Some were deflected, and a very small number bounced back, showing that positive charge and most mass are concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus.

5.4 Nuclear radiation

Alpha radiation consists of helium nuclei (two protons and two neutrons); it is strongly ionising but has low penetration and is stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air. Beta-minus radiation consists of fast electrons emitted from the nucleus; it is moderately ionising and is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Gamma radiation is high-frequency electromagnetic radiation; it is weakly ionising and highly penetrating, reduced by thick lead or concrete. These are typical descriptions; shielding depends on energy and material thickness.

In alpha decay, nucleon number decreases by 4 and proton number by 2. In beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron; nucleon number is unchanged and proton number increases by 1. Gamma emission changes neither proton nor nucleon number. Nuclear equations must conserve both nucleon number and proton number.

5.5 Detecting and measuring radiation

A Geiger–Müller tube and counter detect ionising radiation and record count rate. Background radiation comes from natural sources such as rocks, cosmic rays and radon, and from artificial sources such as medical procedures. Measure background count for a fixed time and subtract it from the measured count to estimate the source's net count. Counts fluctuate randomly, so repeat and average measurements.

5.6 Half-life and decay

Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei, or activity, to fall to half its initial value. Radioactive decay is random for individual nuclei but predictable statistically for a large sample. After n half-lives, remaining fraction = (1/2)ⁿ. Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq), where 1 Bq is one decay per second.

Worked example: A source has activity 800 Bq and half-life 6 hours. After 18 hours, three half-lives have passed: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 Bq.

5.7 Uses, dangers and safety

Ionising radiation can damage or kill cells and may cause mutations or cancer. Risk depends on radiation type, energy, dose, exposure time and tissue. Medical uses include tracers for diagnosis, gamma sources for treatment and sterilisation, and radioactive sources in industrial thickness monitoring. Safety principles: minimise time near a source, maximise distance, use appropriate shielding, handle sources with tongs, store securely and follow local regulations. Never point a source at a person or handle it unnecessarily.

5.8 Fission and fusion

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large unstable nucleus, often after absorbing a neutron. It releases energy and additional neutrons; these may cause a chain reaction. In a controlled reactor, control rods absorb neutrons and a moderator slows them where required. Fusion joins light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus and releases energy; it powers stars but requires extremely high temperature and pressure to overcome electrostatic repulsion. Both processes convert a small amount of mass into energy, E = mc².

5.9 Ions, nuclide notation and nuclear equations

5.12 Nuclear safety and risk

Risk depends on activity, radiation type, energy, exposure duration, distance and shielding. Use time, distance and shielding to reduce exposure; use remote handling and secure storage. Distinguish irradiation (exposure to radiation) from contamination (radioactive material on or inside an object/person). Irradiated objects do not automatically become radioactive.

6. Space physics

6.1 The Solar System

The Solar System contains the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets. Planets orbit the Sun due to gravitational attraction. Moons orbit planets. The inner planets are mainly rocky; outer planets are much larger and include gas and ice giants.

6.2 The Earth's motion

Earth rotates once approximately every 24 hours, causing day and night. Earth orbits the Sun once in approximately one year. Its axis is tilted, causing seasons as the angle and duration of sunlight change during the year. The Moon orbits Earth; its changing appearance from Earth is due to the changing fraction of its sunlit half that we see.

6.2.1 The Moon's orbit and phases

The Moon takes approximately one month to complete an orbit around Earth. It does not produce its own visible light; it reflects sunlight. At every point in its orbit, half of the Moon is illuminated by the Sun. As the Moon moves around Earth, the fraction of this illuminated half visible from Earth changes, producing the phases: new Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon, waning gibbous, last (third) quarter and waning crescent. The cycle repeats approximately every 29.5 days. The phases are not caused by Earth's shadow; Earth's shadow causes a lunar eclipse only when Sun, Earth and Moon align appropriately.

Exam explanation: At new Moon, the illuminated side faces mostly away from Earth; at full Moon, the illuminated side faces Earth. During the waxing phases the visible illuminated fraction increases; during waning it decreases. The Moon's orbital period relative to the stars is about 27.3 days, while the phase cycle relative to the Sun is about 29.5 days.

6.2.2 Average orbital speed

For an approximately circular orbit, the distance travelled in one complete orbit is its circumference, 2πr. Average orbital speed is therefore:

v = 2πr ÷ T

where r is the average orbital radius in metres and T is the orbital period in seconds. Convert days to seconds before substituting if the answer is required in m/s.

Worked example: A satellite moves in a circular orbit of radius 7.0 × 10⁶ m and completes one orbit in 5.8 × 10³ s. v = 2 × π × 7.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 5.8 × 10³ = 7.6 × 10³ m/s (2 significant figures).

Common misconception: Seasons are caused by Earth's tilted axis, not by Earth being substantially nearer to or farther from the Sun. The Moon's phases are caused by viewing different fractions of its sunlit hemisphere, not by the Earth's shadow.

6.3 Gravity and orbits

Gravity is an attractive force between masses. A planet's orbital motion is caused by gravitational force providing centripetal force, continuously changing the direction of its velocity. A satellite in a stable circular orbit is continually accelerating towards the centre even if its speed is constant.

6.4 Stars and the Universe

Stars form when gas and dust in a nebula collapse under gravity. A protostar heats up; when the core becomes hot enough, nuclear fusion begins and a main-sequence star forms. In main-sequence stars, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing energy. A star's later evolution depends on its mass. The Universe contains galaxies; evidence for expansion includes redshift of light from distant galaxies. Greater redshift generally indicates greater recession speed.

6.5 Distances in space

Light-year is a unit of distance: the distance light travels in one year, not a unit of time. Astronomical distances are large, so astronomical units are useful within the Solar System and light-years for distances between stars.

6.3 Satellites and orbital motion

Artificial satellites are used for communication, navigation, weather observation and Earth monitoring. A geostationary satellite orbits above the equator in the same direction as Earth's rotation, with an orbital period of about 24 hours, so it appears above the same point on Earth. Lower-orbit satellites have shorter periods and are useful for imaging and some communication systems. A satellite's speed and orbital radius determine its period; it does not need continuous engine thrust to remain in an ideal orbit.

6.4 The Sun and stars

The Sun is a star whose energy is produced by nuclear fusion in its core. Hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium; a small amount of mass is converted into energy. A star forms when gravity causes a cloud of gas and dust to collapse. A protostar heats as gravitational potential energy is converted to internal energy. When the core becomes hot and dense enough, fusion begins and the star enters the main-sequence stage, where outward pressure balances inward gravitational attraction.

6.5 Stellar evolution

A star's later evolution depends mainly on its initial mass. A Sun-like star expands into a red giant, sheds outer layers to form a planetary nebula and leaves a white dwarf, which eventually cools. A much more massive star becomes a supergiant and may explode as a supernova; the remnant may be a neutron star or black hole. A black hole has gravity so strong that, within its event horizon, even light cannot escape.

6.6 Redshift and the expanding Universe

Light from many distant galaxies is shifted towards longer wavelengths (redshift). This is evidence that those galaxies are generally moving away from us and supports the idea that the Universe is expanding. The greater the distance of a galaxy, the greater its recession speed on average (Hubble's law). The Big Bang model describes the Universe expanding from an initially hot, dense state; evidence includes galactic redshift and cosmic microwave background radiation.

6.7 Astronomical distance and scale

An astronomical unit (AU) is approximately the mean Earth–Sun distance and is useful within the Solar System. A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, approximately 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m; it is a distance, not a time. A parsec is another astronomical distance unit. Because light takes time to travel, observing a distant star means seeing it as it was in the past.

Space physics calculations and interpretation

Worked example: Light travels at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. In one year (3.156 × 10⁷ s), distance = 3.0 × 10⁸ × 3.156 × 10⁷ = 9.47 × 10¹⁵ m, approximately one light-year.

When interpreting star-life-cycle diagrams, distinguish the low-mass and high-mass pathways. Do not state that every star becomes a supernova or black hole: the final remnant depends on initial mass.

6.8 Solar System structure and planetary motion

Compare planets by orbital distance, period, size and composition. The inner planets are rocky; the outer planets are much larger gas or ice giants. Dwarf planets orbit the Sun but have not cleared their orbital neighbourhood. Comets travel in elongated orbits and develop tails when near the Sun; the tail points generally away from the Sun due to solar radiation and solar wind. Asteroids are rocky bodies, many found in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

6.9 Gravity, orbital speed and satellite uses

Gravity provides the centripetal force that keeps a satellite in orbit. A low-orbit satellite travels quickly and has a short orbital period; a geostationary satellite has a period matching Earth's rotation and remains above the same equatorial point. Geostationary satellites are useful for continuous communication and weather monitoring over one region. Polar and low-Earth orbit satellites are useful for mapping, environmental monitoring and imaging because they pass over different parts of Earth.

6.10 Stellar brightness and observing the past

Stars differ in luminosity, surface temperature, size and colour. Blue-white stars have higher surface temperatures than red stars. Apparent brightness depends on both the star's luminosity and its distance from Earth. Because light travels at a finite speed, distant objects are observed as they were when the light left them; looking farther into space is looking further back in time.

6.11 The expanding Universe

Redshift occurs when spectral lines from a distant galaxy are shifted towards longer wavelengths. It indicates that the galaxy is receding. Observations show that more distant galaxies generally recede faster, supporting an expanding Universe. The Big Bang model describes the Universe expanding from a hot, dense early state. Cosmic microwave background radiation is remnant radiation from the early Universe and is important evidence supporting the model.

Practical assessment: planning, measurement and evaluation

Practical papers assess experimental skills rather than recall alone. Candidates should be able to select apparatus, identify independent/dependent/control variables, plan a fair test, take repeat readings, record data with units, plot graphs, calculate gradients, identify anomalies, draw conclusions supported by data and suggest improvements.

Measurement and uncertainty

Read scales at eye level. Record all readings to a consistent precision supported by the instrument. For repeated readings, calculate a mean and identify anomalous results. For a length measured with a ruler, consider the scale resolution and alignment. For timing an oscillation, time many oscillations and divide by the number. Avoid parallax and ensure apparatus is zeroed before use.

Tables and graphs

Give every column a heading with quantity and unit, e.g. time / s. Use a sensible scale that fills most of the grid. Plot points accurately with small crosses. Draw a best-fit straight line or smooth curve rather than joining dot-to-dot. Calculate a gradient using a large triangle on the best-fit line, not two adjacent plotted points. State the gradient unit. Identify the independent variable on the x-axis and dependent variable on the y-axis unless the task specifies otherwise.

Evaluation and improvements

Explain limitations in terms of the apparatus or method, not vague statements. Examples: reaction time affects stopwatch measurements; heat loss lowers measured temperature rise; friction affects trolley motion; parallax affects scale readings; a thick line or few points makes a gradient uncertain. Improvements should directly address the limitation: use light gates, insulation, repeat trials, larger measurement intervals, a fiducial marker, a set square or a more precise instrument.

Load–extension graphs for springs

Measure the spring's original length, add known loads one at a time, allow the spring to settle, and record its new length. Calculate extension by subtracting the original length. Plot load (force, N) on the vertical axis against extension (m) on the horizontal axis. In the straight-line region through the origin, the gradient is the spring constant k = F/x. Mark the limit of proportionality where the graph stops being straight. Remove loads carefully and do not exceed the safe range of the spring.

Worked example: A spring extends 0.040 m under a 2.0 N load. k = F/x = 2.0/0.040 = 50 N/m. If the load is increased to 3.0 N while the spring remains in the proportional region, extension = F/k = 3.0/50 = 0.060 m.

Using gradients, intercepts, interpolation and extrapolation

For a straight-line graph, gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. Choose two widely separated points on the best-fit line, form a large gradient triangle and show the coordinates used. Include the correct compound unit. The y-intercept is the value of y when x = 0 and may reveal a zero error or a physical starting value. Interpolation estimates a value between measured data points and is generally more reliable than extrapolation, which estimates beyond the measured range and should be treated cautiously. Read values from the best-fit line, not from an isolated anomalous point.

Accuracy, precision, repeatability and uncertainty

Accuracy describes closeness to the accepted value; precision describes the resolution or spread of measurements. Repeat measurements under the same conditions to assess repeatability and calculate a mean when appropriate. Record instrument resolution and quote units consistently. A systematic error shifts readings in one direction (for example, a zero error); a random error causes scatter (for example, reaction-time variation). Repeating readings can reduce the effect of random error but does not remove a systematic error.

Practical data analysis and conclusions

Identify the independent variable, dependent variable and variables that must be controlled. Take enough readings across a suitable range, including repeats where useful. Check whether the pattern is linear, curved, directly proportional or inversely related. Use interpolation only within the measured range; avoid unsupported extrapolation. A conclusion must answer the investigation question and cite numerical evidence, such as the trend, gradient or comparison between readings. State whether the evidence supports the predicted relationship.

Further syllabus lessons and commonly missed learning objectives

These lessons extend the earlier topic notes. They are organised by the Cambridge 0625 syllabus and include Core knowledge plus clearly identified Supplement material for Extended candidates.

1. Motion, forces and energy — conservation, motion graphs and applications

Conservation of momentum (Supplement)

In an isolated system, the total momentum before a collision or explosion equals the total momentum afterwards. Momentum is a vector, so choose a positive direction and use signed velocities. For two objects, m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. In an explosion, objects initially at rest have zero total momentum, so their final momenta are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The principle applies to one-dimensional problems in this syllabus.

Worked example: A 2.0 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m/s collides with a stationary 1.0 kg trolley. They stick together. Initial momentum = 2.0 × 3.0 = 6.0 kg m/s. Combined mass = 3.0 kg. Final speed = 6.0/3.0 = 2.0 m/s.

Terminal velocity and speed–time graphs

A falling object initially accelerates because its weight is greater than air resistance. As speed increases, drag increases and the resultant downward force decreases. When drag equals weight, resultant force and acceleration become zero: the object continues at constant terminal velocity. On a speed–time graph, the gradient is acceleration; a horizontal section represents constant speed. The area under the graph gives distance travelled. For a decelerating object, acceleration is negative when the chosen positive direction is its direction of initial motion.

Energy resources: comparison and environmental impact

Fossil fuels are dispatchable and energy-dense but finite and release carbon dioxide and pollutants. Biofuels are renewable when regrown sustainably, but require land and can compete with food production. Hydroelectric dams are controllable and renewable but flood habitats and depend on suitable geography. Tidal power is predictable but location-specific; wave and wind power are variable. Geothermal power can be reliable but is geographically limited. Nuclear fission produces large continuous output with low operational carbon emissions but creates radioactive waste and requires stringent safety. Solar cells generate electricity directly; solar thermal collectors heat water. Compare resources using renewability, availability, reliability, scale, cost and environmental impact. Supplement: most resources ultimately receive energy from the Sun, except geothermal, nuclear and tidal; the Sun releases energy by fusion, and controlled fusion research aims to make this energy useful on a large scale.

Efficiency and power calculations

Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%, or useful power output ÷ total power input × 100%. Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: P = W/t = ΔE/t. A Sankey diagram uses arrow widths to show the proportions of useful and wasted energy. The total input must equal the sum of all outputs when energy is conserved.

Pressure and density applications

Pressure p = F/A. The same force produces greater pressure over a smaller area, explaining sharp blades and wide snowshoes. Liquid pressure increases with depth and density. For a vertical depth difference, Δp = ρgΔh. For liquids that do not mix, the denser liquid settles below the less dense liquid; an object floats in a liquid when its average density is lower than the liquid's density. Density of a liquid can be found from its mass and measured volume; density of an irregular sinking solid can be found using water displacement.

2. Thermal physics — particle model, gas laws and heat transfer

States of matter and changes of state

In a solid, particles are close together in an ordered arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions. In a liquid, particles remain close but move past one another. In a gas, particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly. Melting changes solid to liquid; freezing changes liquid to solid; boiling and evaporation change liquid to gas; condensation changes gas to liquid. During a change of state, energy changes the arrangement/separation of particles rather than raising temperature, so the temperature remains constant for a pure substance while the change occurs. Evaporation occurs at the surface and can happen below the boiling point; faster particles escape, lowering the average energy of those left and cooling the liquid.

Gas pressure and absolute temperature (Supplement)

Gas particles move randomly and collide with container walls. Their momentum changes exert force; pressure is force per unit area. At constant volume, increasing the Kelvin temperature increases the particles' average kinetic energy and the pressure. Convert temperature using T(K) = θ(°C) + 273. For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pV = constant; therefore p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Use absolute temperature in gas-law calculations.

Worked example: A gas occupies 600 cm³ at 100 kPa. It is compressed to 400 cm³ at constant temperature. p₂ = p₁V₁/V₂ = 100 × 600/400 = 150 kPa.

Specific heat capacity and latent heat

Specific heat capacity c is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). E = mcΔT. Specific latent heat L is the energy transferred per kilogram during a change of state without temperature change: E = mL. In an electrical heating experiment, measure mass, initial and final temperature, voltage, current and heating time; electrical energy supplied is E = IVt. Insulate the apparatus, stir liquids, use a suitable thermometer and reduce heat loss. For melting or boiling, energy supplied changes state rather than temperature.

Conduction, convection and radiation in context

Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions; in metals, mobile electrons also transfer energy rapidly. Gases and most liquids are poor conductors because particles are relatively far apart. Convection occurs in fluids: heating causes expansion and lower density, so warmer fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks, setting up a circulation. Thermal radiation is infrared and needs no medium. Dull black surfaces are good absorbers and emitters; shiny light surfaces are poor absorbers and emitters but good reflectors. A body stays at constant temperature when incoming and outgoing energy-transfer rates balance. If incoming rate is greater, it warms; if outgoing rate is greater, it cools. Earth's temperature depends on the balance between absorbed solar radiation and emitted infrared radiation.

3. Waves — wave properties, light, sound and communication

Wave motion and wavefronts

Waves transfer energy without a net transfer of matter. Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium; wavelength is the distance between adjacent points in phase; frequency is the number of complete waves per second; period T = 1/f. Wave speed v = fλ. A transverse wave vibrates perpendicular to its direction of travel; examples include electromagnetic waves, water waves as a model and seismic S-waves. A longitudinal wave vibrates parallel to its direction of travel; sound in air and seismic P-waves are examples. Wavefronts join points in the same phase.

Ripple tank investigations

Use a shallow ripple tank and a vibrating dipper to produce straight or circular wavefronts. A barrier demonstrates reflection. A change in water depth changes wave speed and wavelength, demonstrating refraction; frequency remains constant. A narrow gap or obstacle edge demonstrates diffraction. Diffraction is greatest when the gap width is similar to or smaller than the wavelength. Increase wavelength or decrease gap size to increase spreading. Keep the dipper frequency constant when comparing depth or gap effects, and use a lamp and screen to make wavefronts visible.

Plane mirrors and image construction

Draw a normal perpendicular to the mirror at the point of incidence. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal. A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image of the same size as the object and the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. Use at least two rays and extend reflected rays backwards with dashed lines to locate the virtual image.

Refraction, refractive index and total internal reflection

Refraction is a change in direction caused by a change in wave speed at a boundary. Light entering a more optically dense medium slows and bends towards the normal; leaving it speeds up and bends away from the normal. Refractive index n = sin i/sin r for the stated ray geometry and n = 1/sin c at the critical angle. Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle. Optical fibres use repeated total internal reflection to guide signals, including telecommunications.

Worked example: If the critical angle of a material is 30°, n = 1/sin 30° = 2.0.

Lenses, images and vision

A converging lens brings parallel rays to its principal focus; a diverging lens spreads parallel rays as if they came from a virtual focus. The principal axis passes through the optical centre and principal focus. For a converging lens, an object beyond the focal length forms a real, inverted image; an object inside the focal length forms a virtual, upright, magnified image. Draw a ray parallel to the principal axis refracting through the focus, and a ray through the optical centre continuing undeviated. Short-sightedness is corrected using a diverging lens; long-sightedness is corrected using a converging lens.

Sound, ultrasound and digital communication

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave and cannot travel through a vacuum. Its speed can be found from distance/time; for an echo, the sound travels to the reflector and back, so distance to the reflector = speed × echo time ÷ 2. Ultrasound has frequency above the human hearing range and is used in medical imaging and sonar. In sonar, measure echo time and use the known speed of sound in water to calculate range. Analogue signals vary continuously; digital signals use discrete values, commonly binary 0 and 1. Digital signals can be regenerated and are generally less affected by accumulated noise.

4. Electricity and magnetism — full circuit and electromagnetic applications

Charge, current, e.m.f. and potential difference

Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. Charging by friction transfers electrons; protons do not move from one solid object to another in this process. Conductors contain mobile charge carriers; insulators do not allow charge to move freely. Charge is measured in coulombs. Current I = Q/t. Conventional current is from positive to negative; electron flow in a metal is from negative to positive. E.m.f. is energy supplied per unit charge by a source, E = W/Q. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge across a component, V = W/Q. Ammeters are connected in series; voltmeters are connected in parallel.

Resistance, I–V characteristics and circuit rules

Resistance R = V/I. For a metallic wire at constant temperature, resistance increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area. A fixed resistor has a straight-line I–V graph through the origin if its temperature remains constant. A filament lamp's resistance increases as its filament heats, so its graph curves and becomes less steep. A diode conducts mainly in one direction and has negligible current in reverse bias. In series, current is the same everywhere and resistances add. In parallel, the source current is the sum of branch currents; the combined resistance of two resistors is less than either resistor alone. For two resistors in parallel, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

Worked example: 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. 1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 3/6, so equivalent resistance = 2 Ω.

Electrical energy, power and domestic electricity

Electrical power P = IV; electrical energy E = IVt. The kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance operating for 1 hour. Energy used (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h); cost = energy used × tariff per kWh. Mains hazards include damaged insulation, overheating cables, damp conditions and overloading sockets. The live wire is dangerous and the switch must interrupt the live wire. A fuse melts when current is too high; a circuit breaker or trip switch disconnects the supply. The earth wire provides a low-resistance path during a fault. Double-insulated appliances have non-conducting outer cases and do not require an earth wire.

Worked example: A 2.0 kW heater operates for 3.5 h. Energy = 2.0 × 3.5 = 7.0 kWh. At KSh 25 per kWh, cost = 7.0 × 25 = KSh 175.

Magnetic fields and electromagnets

Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract. A magnetic field is a region where a magnetic pole experiences a force. Field lines outside a bar magnet go from north to south; closer spacing represents a stronger field. A compass plots field direction; iron filings show the pattern. Soft iron is suitable for temporary electromagnets because it magnetises and demagnetises readily; steel retains magnetism and is used for permanent magnets. A current-carrying coil produces a magnetic field; adding a soft-iron core strengthens the electromagnet.

Electromagnetic induction, generators and transformers

An e.m.f. is induced when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or when the magnetic field linking a conductor changes. A faster change, stronger field or more turns can increase induced e.m.f. The induced effect opposes the change producing it. An a.c. generator rotates a coil in a magnetic field and produces an alternating e.m.f.; a slip-ring arrangement allows the output to reverse direction every half-turn. A transformer has primary and secondary coils on a soft-iron core and works with changing current. Vp/Vs = Np/Ns. For an ideal transformer, IpVp = IsVs. High-voltage transmission reduces current for a given power; cable loss P = I²R is therefore reduced. A relay uses an electromagnet to operate a separate switch; a loudspeaker uses the force on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field to move a cone.

5. Nuclear physics — atomic structure, decay and applications

Atomic structure and Rutherford scattering

An atom has a tiny, positively charged nucleus containing most of its mass, with negatively charged electrons around it; most of the atom is empty space. In the alpha-scattering experiment, most alpha particles passed through thin metal foil, showing that atoms are mostly empty space. A small number were deflected through large angles or bounced back, showing that positive charge and most mass are concentrated in a very small nucleus. An atom becomes a positive ion by losing electrons and a negative ion by gaining electrons.

Nuclide notation and nuclear equations

Nuclide notation is ᴬZX, where Z is proton number and A is nucleon number. Neutrons = A − Z. Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. In alpha decay, mass number decreases by 4 and proton number by 2. In beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron; mass number is unchanged and proton number increases by 1. Gamma emission releases electromagnetic radiation from an excited nucleus and does not change A or Z. In every nuclear equation, conserve total nucleon number and proton number on both sides.

Background radiation and corrected count rate

Background radiation comes from radon gas, rocks and buildings, food and drink, and cosmic rays. A detector and counter measure count rate in counts/s or counts/min. Measure background count over a suitable interval and subtract its rate from the source-plus-background rate to obtain corrected source count rate. Repeat readings and use a mean where appropriate. Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random; it is not possible to predict when an individual nucleus will decay.

Worked example: A detector records 92 counts/min with a source present. Background is 12 counts/min. Corrected count rate = 92 − 12 = 80 counts/min.

Penetration, ionisation and shielding

Alpha particles are helium nuclei, strongly ionising and weakly penetrating; paper or skin stops them. Beta particles are fast electrons, moderately ionising and more penetrating; a thin sheet of aluminium reduces them. Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation, weakly ionising but highly penetrating; thick lead or concrete reduces their intensity. Alpha and beta are deflected in electric and magnetic fields because they are charged; gamma is not. Alpha and beta deflect in opposite directions. Ionising radiation can damage or kill cells, cause mutations and increase cancer risk. Reduce exposure by minimising time, maximising distance, using suitable shielding and handling sources with approved tools and procedures.

Half-life and isotope applications

Half-life is the time for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay. After each half-life, the activity or number of undecayed nuclei halves. For an isotope with a short half-life, activity falls quickly; a longer half-life gives a slower decrease. Smoke alarms use alpha radiation because it ionises air in the detector; smoke reduces the current. Gamma radiation can sterilise medical equipment and irradiate food because it penetrates packaging and kills microorganisms. Thickness monitoring uses beta radiation because the detected count changes with absorber thickness. Medical tracers require suitable radiation and a short enough half-life to limit patient exposure; gamma can escape the body for detection.

6. Space physics — orbits, stars and cosmology

Earth's rotation, orbit and seasons

Earth rotates once approximately every 24 hours, producing day and night. Earth orbits the Sun once in approximately one year. The Earth's axis is tilted relative to its orbital plane; this tilt causes seasonal changes in daylight duration and the angle of incoming sunlight. Seasons are not caused by Earth being substantially closer to or farther from the Sun. The Moon orbits Earth; the changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon produce the phases seen from Earth. A phase cycle takes approximately one month.

Orbital speed and satellite motion

Average orbital speed = circumference of orbit ÷ time period. For a circular orbit, circumference = 2πr, so v = 2πr/T. A satellite remains in orbit because gravity provides the centripetal force that continually changes its direction. A lower orbit generally has a shorter period and greater orbital speed than a higher orbit. Satellites may be used for communications, weather observation, navigation and scientific observation. A geostationary satellite orbits above the equator in the same direction as Earth's rotation and has a period of approximately 24 hours, so it appears above the same point on Earth.

Worked example: A satellite travels in a circular orbit of radius 7.0 × 10⁶ m in 5.8 × 10³ s. v = 2πr/T = 2π × 7.0 × 10⁶ / 5.8 × 10³ ≈ 7.6 × 10³ m/s.

Stars, luminosity, redshift and the Big Bang

A star is a luminous sphere of hot gas in which nuclear fusion releases energy. The Sun is a star. Stars differ in colour, surface temperature, size and brightness. Apparent brightness depends on both the star's luminosity and its distance from Earth; a very luminous distant star can appear dim. Looking at distant objects means observing light that left them in the past. Redshift is an increase in the wavelength of light from a receding galaxy. Light from most distant galaxies is redshifted, providing evidence that the Universe is expanding. The Big Bang model proposes that the Universe began in a hot, dense state and has expanded; the redshift of distant galaxies and the cosmic microwave background are important evidence.

    150 − 18 = 132 counts/min.

  7. A planet orbits at radius 1.5 × 10¹¹ m with period 3.2 × 10⁷ s. Calculate average orbital speed.
    Show answer

    v = 2πr/T ≈ 2π × 1.5 × 10¹¹ / 3.2 × 10⁷ = 2.9 × 10⁴ m/s.

Further detailed lessons: important concepts, experiments and exam applications

This extension develops areas that are often assessed through explanations, unfamiliar contexts, practical planning and multi-step calculations. Learn the definitions, describe the physical model, choose the correct equation, substitute values with units, and explain the result.

1.12 Resultant forces, vector calculations and circular motion

A resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together. For forces along one straight line, choose a positive direction, add forces in that direction and subtract forces in the opposite direction. If two perpendicular forces act, draw a right-angled vector triangle. The magnitude of the resultant is R = √(F₁² + F₂²), and its direction can be found using tan θ = opposite/adjacent. In this syllabus, perpendicular resultant calculations are limited to forces or velocities.

A body moving in a circular path changes direction continuously, even if its speed is constant, so its velocity changes. A force perpendicular to the instantaneous direction of motion is needed to keep it in the circular path. At constant mass and radius, a greater speed requires a greater force; at constant mass and speed, a smaller radius requires a greater force. Do not use F = mv²/r as a required equation for this syllabus.

1.13 Moments, centre of gravity and stability practical

To investigate moments, balance a metre rule on a pivot and hang known weights at measured perpendicular distances. Adjust one or more distances until the rule is horizontal. Calculate clockwise and anticlockwise moments about the pivot. Repeat with different loads. A balanced rule has zero resultant moment. For an irregular plane lamina, suspend it freely from one hole and hang a plumb line from the same point; draw the vertical line on the lamina. Repeat from a second suspension point. The intersection of the lines gives the centre of gravity. A wide base and low centre of gravity make an object more stable.

1.14 Pressure, liquid columns and hydraulic systems

Pressure is force per unit area, p = F/A, measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². For a liquid column, the pressure increase is Δp = ρgΔh. The pressure difference depends on liquid density and vertical depth, not on the container's shape. A hydraulic system uses an enclosed liquid to transmit pressure. If pistons have areas A₁ and A₂, equal pressure gives F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂. A larger output piston can provide a larger force, but it moves a smaller distance; energy is not created.

Worked example: Oil of density 800 kg/m³ is 2.5 m deep. Taking g = 10 N/kg, pressure increase = ρgh = 800 × 10 × 2.5 = 20 000 Pa.

2.6 Thermal expansion of solids, liquids and gases

When most substances are heated, their particles gain internal energy and their average separation increases. Solids expand in length, area and volume; liquids expand in volume; gases usually expand much more than solids and liquids for a similar temperature rise. Expansion gaps are left in bridges and railway tracks. Overhead wires sag more on hot days. A bimetallic strip bends when heated because its two metals expand by different amounts; it can operate a thermostat or fire alarm. An exception to the usual behaviour is water between 0 °C and 4 °C, which contracts as it is heated from 0 °C to 4 °C.

2.7 Specific heat capacity: meaning, equation and experiment

Specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). Use E = mcΔT, where E is energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms, c is in J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ and ΔT is the temperature change. A substance with high specific heat capacity requires more energy for the same mass and temperature rise.

Electrical method: Measure the mass of a metal block, insert an electric heater and thermometer into close-fitting holes, and wrap the block in insulation. Record initial temperature. Switch on the heater for a measured time, recording voltage and current. Calculate electrical energy E = VIt. Record final temperature and calculate ΔT. Then c = VIt/(mΔT). Reduce heat loss with insulation, use thermal paste for good contact, stir liquids where appropriate, and repeat readings. If the heater's energy is not all transferred to the sample, the calculated value may be too high or low depending on the correction and losses.

Worked example: A 0.50 kg block receives 6000 J and warms by 30 °C. c = 6000/(0.50 × 30) = 400 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

2.8 Specific latent heat and change of state

During melting or boiling, energy supplied changes the arrangement and separation of particles rather than increasing their average kinetic energy. Temperature therefore remains constant during a pure change of state at constant pressure. Specific latent heat L is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without a temperature change. Use E = mL. Specific latent heat of fusion refers to solid-to-liquid change; specific latent heat of vaporisation refers to liquid-to-gas change. Condensation and freezing release energy to the surroundings.

Experiment: Heat melting ice or boiling water using an electrical heater. Measure mass change and electrical energy supplied (E = VIt) over a measured interval. For melting, keep the ice-water mixture near 0 °C and ensure the heater is suitably positioned. For vaporisation, measure mass lost as steam escapes while temperature remains at the boiling point. Insulate the apparatus and account for energy transferred to the container and surroundings.

Worked example: 0.20 kg of a liquid is vaporised using 450 000 J at constant temperature. L = E/m = 450 000/0.20 = 2.25 × 10⁶ J/kg.

2.9 Gas laws, absolute temperature and particle collisions

Temperature in kelvin is T(K) = θ(°C) + 273. At constant volume, gas pressure is proportional to kelvin temperature: p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂. At constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. These relationships apply to a fixed mass of gas. Gas pressure results from particles colliding with the container walls. Heating increases average kinetic energy and, at constant volume, increases collision rate and momentum change per unit time. Compressing a gas at constant temperature increases collision frequency per unit wall area.

Worked example: A gas at 300 K has pressure 100 kPa. At constant volume its temperature rises to 360 K. p₂ = 100 × 360/300 = 120 kPa.

2.10 Thermal transfer: conduction, convection and radiation

Conduction: Energy passes through a substance without bulk movement. In metals, mobile electrons transfer energy rapidly; vibrating particles also transfer energy. Insulators have no freely moving electrons and usually conduct less effectively. Convection: In a fluid, heating causes expansion and a decrease in density; warmer fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks, forming a convection current. Radiation: Infrared radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and can travel through a vacuum. Dull black surfaces are good absorbers and emitters; shiny, light surfaces are poor absorbers and emitters and good reflectors.

Practical comparison: Compare cooling of identical containers with different surface finishes, keeping volume, starting temperature, surroundings and container dimensions the same. Record temperature at regular intervals. For conduction, compare rods of different materials heated at one end, using identical drawing pins fixed with wax at equal distances. For convection, add a safe visible tracer to water and heat gently at one point to observe circulation. State how the independent, dependent and control variables are managed.

3.5 Electromagnetic spectrum and applications

All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. In order of increasing frequency (and decreasing wavelength): radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All transfer energy; they differ in wavelength, frequency and interaction with matter. Radio waves are used for broadcasting and communications; microwaves for satellite/mobile links and cooking; infrared for heaters, thermal imaging and remote controls; visible light for vision and photography; ultraviolet for security marking and sterilisation; X-rays for medical imaging and security scanning; gamma rays for sterilising equipment and cancer treatment. Higher-frequency ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can ionise atoms and damage living tissue. Use appropriate shielding and exposure controls.

For every electromagnetic wave, c = fλ. When a wave enters a different medium, its frequency remains unchanged while speed and wavelength may change. Do not confuse the order of the spectrum with the order of increasing wavelength.

3.6 Dispersion and colour

White light contains a range of visible wavelengths. A glass prism disperses white light because different wavelengths refract by different amounts. Red light is refracted least and violet most in a typical glass prism. A coloured filter transmits its own colour (or a range including it) and absorbs much of the other visible light. An object appears coloured because of the light it reflects or transmits into the observer's eye; a red object under blue-only illumination may appear dark.

4.6 Electrostatics, charging and electric fields

There are two types of electric charge, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charging by friction transfers electrons from one material to another. The object gaining electrons becomes negatively charged; the object losing electrons becomes positively charged. Charge is conserved. A charged object can attract a neutral object because it causes charge separation (polarisation). Conductors allow charge to move; insulators hold charge in place. Earthing provides a conducting path for excess charge to flow to or from Earth.

An electric field is a region where a charge experiences a force. Field direction is the direction of force on a small positive test charge. Field lines point away from positive charges and towards negative charges; they never cross. Lines are closer together where the field is stronger. Between oppositely charged parallel plates, the field is approximately uniform away from the edges.

4.7 Potential difference, e.m.f. and charge calculations

Current is rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V = E/Q. E.m.f. is energy supplied per unit charge by a source. Both are measured in volts, but e.m.f. describes energy supplied by the source while p.d. describes energy transferred by a component. One coulomb passing through a 6 V component transfers 6 J of energy. An ammeter is placed in series and has very low resistance; a voltmeter is placed in parallel and has high resistance.

Worked example: A current of 0.40 A flows for 3 minutes. Q = It = 0.40 × 180 = 72 C. If a 12 V source transfers this charge, energy = VQ = 12 × 72 = 864 J.

4.8 Components, sensors and potential dividers

A fixed resistor has approximately constant resistance at constant temperature. A filament lamp's resistance increases as its filament gets hotter. A diode allows current mainly in one direction; an LED emits light when forward biased. An LDR has lower resistance in brighter light. An NTC thermistor has lower resistance at higher temperature. These components can be used in sensing circuits.

A potential divider uses series components to share the supply voltage. For two resistors R₁ and R₂ in series across a supply V, the voltage across R₂ is V₂ = V × R₂/(R₁ + R₂). If one resistor is an LDR or thermistor, the output voltage changes with light or temperature. Identify which component is connected to the output before predicting whether output voltage rises or falls.

Worked example: A 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ resistor are in series across 10 V. Voltage across the 3 kΩ resistor = 10 × 3/(2 + 3) = 6 V.

4.9 Magnetic fields, force on a current and the motor effect

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field around it. If the wire is placed in an external magnetic field, the fields interact and a force acts on the wire; this is the motor effect. The force is greatest when the current is perpendicular to the magnetic field and is zero when the wire is parallel to the field. Increasing current or magnetic field strength increases the force. Reverse the current or reverse the magnetic field to reverse the force direction. The direction can be found using Fleming's left-hand rule: first finger = field, second finger = conventional current, thumb = force/motion.

In a simple d.c. motor, a coil carrying current experiences opposite forces on its two sides, producing a turning effect. A split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn so that the turning effect remains in the same rotational direction. Brushes maintain electrical contact with the rotating commutator. A loudspeaker uses the motor effect: an alternating current in a coil interacts with a permanent magnet's field, moving the coil and cone to produce sound.

4.10 Electromagnetic induction and transformer calculations

An e.m.f. is induced when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or when magnetic flux linking a coil changes. The induced e.m.f. increases when the magnet or coil moves faster, the magnetic field is stronger, or the coil has more turns. The induced current produces a magnetic effect that opposes the change that caused it. In an a.c. generator, a coil rotates in a magnetic field and the induced e.m.f. changes direction every half-turn. Slip rings and brushes connect the rotating coil to the external circuit.

A transformer has primary and secondary coils wound on a laminated soft-iron core. It requires alternating current or a changing current. The turns and voltage relationship is Vp/Vs = Np/Ns. For an ideal transformer, input power equals output power: VpIp = VsIs. A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary; a step-down transformer has fewer. High-voltage transmission reduces current for a given power, reducing heating losses in cables because P_loss = I²R.

Worked example: A transformer has 500 turns on its primary and 100 turns on its secondary. If primary voltage is 240 V, secondary voltage = 240 × 100/500 = 48 V. For an ideal transformer supplying 2 A at 48 V, output power = 96 W and primary current = 96/240 = 0.40 A.

5.6 Nuclear fission, fusion and energy

In nuclear fission, a large unstable nucleus (for example uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and additional neutrons. These neutrons may cause further fissions, producing a chain reaction. A reactor controls the chain reaction: fuel provides fissile nuclei, a moderator slows neutrons, control rods absorb neutrons, and a coolant transfers thermal energy away. Thick shielding reduces radiation reaching workers and the environment. Radioactive waste must be contained and managed safely.

Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus and releases energy. Fusion powers stars. Very high temperature and pressure are needed to overcome electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei. Fusion does not produce the same long-lived fission products, but controlling hot plasma on Earth is technically difficult.

5.7 Radiation in medicine, industry and safety

Gamma emitters can be used as tracers when radiation can pass out of the body and be detected externally; a suitable isotope should have a short half-life and appropriate radiation. Gamma rays can destroy cancer cells but also damage healthy cells, so treatment is carefully targeted. Gamma radiation sterilises equipment and food. Beta radiation is useful in thickness monitoring: if a sheet becomes thicker, fewer beta particles reach the detector. Alpha sources can be used in smoke detectors because ionisation allows a small current to flow; smoke reduces this current. Radiation safety uses minimum exposure time, maximum practical distance, appropriate shielding, remote handling and secure storage.

6.5 Stellar evolution and the life cycle of stars

Stars form when gas and dust in a nebula are pulled together by gravity. As the protostar contracts, its core becomes hotter. When conditions allow hydrogen fusion, the star enters the main sequence, where outward pressure from energy release balances inward gravitational attraction. A star spends most of its life in the main-sequence stage.

A star similar in mass to the Sun expands into a red giant when hydrogen in its core is depleted. It later sheds outer layers, leaving a hot dense white dwarf that gradually cools. A much more massive star becomes a red supergiant and may explode as a supernova. The remaining core may become a neutron star or, if sufficiently massive, a black hole. The exact pathway depends mainly on the star's initial mass.

Practical skills masterclass: planning, measurement, graphs and evaluation

Planning: State the aim and hypothesis; identify the independent variable to change, dependent variable to measure and control variables to keep constant. Name the apparatus, describe a safe sequence of steps, state the range and intervals of readings, and explain how repeats will be used. Avoid changing more than one independent variable at a time.

Measurements: Read scales at eye level, use the correct range and resolution, avoid parallax, wait for readings to stabilise, and repeat measurements. For oscillations, time 10 or 20 oscillations and divide by the number to reduce percentage timing uncertainty. Record units in table headings and use consistent decimal places.

Graphs: Put the independent variable on the horizontal axis and dependent variable on the vertical axis. Choose a sensible uniform scale that uses most of the graph paper. Plot points accurately, draw a best-fit line or smooth curve, and do not simply join every point dot-to-dot unless the relationship requires it. Calculate gradient using a large triangle on the best-fit line, not two adjacent data points. Include units in the gradient.

Evaluation: Identify anomalous readings and repeat them if possible. Discuss random errors (which cause scatter) and systematic errors (which shift readings consistently). Suggest improvements that directly address the limitation: use a light gate instead of a hand stopwatch, insulate to reduce heat loss, use a larger range, repeat and average, or use a more precise instrument. A conclusion should refer to the data and state whether it supports the hypothesis.

Practical investigation templates

  • Density of an irregular solid: measure mass using a balance; find volume by displacement; repeat the volume reading; calculate ρ = m/V; take care to remove trapped air and read the meniscus at eye level.
  • Spring extension: measure original length, add loads in steps, allow oscillations to stop, record new length and extension, repeat readings, plot load against extension, identify the limit of proportionality and calculate k = F/x within the straight-line region.
  • Cooling: put equal volumes of hot water in identical containers with different surface finishes or insulation; record temperature at fixed time intervals; control starting temperature, volume and surroundings; compare cooling curves.
  • Resistance of a wire: connect an ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel; vary wire length while keeping material, diameter and temperature as constant as possible; calculate R = V/I and plot resistance against length.
  • Refraction: trace a glass block, draw a normal, use pins or a ray box to trace incident and emergent rays, measure i and r from the normal, repeat for several angles and calculate n = sin i/sin r.
  • Wave speed: measure frequency and wavelength, or measure distance and time for a pulse; calculate v = fλ or v = distance/time; repeat and explain uncertainty in locating wavefronts.
  • Radiation absorption: measure background count first; place absorbers of increasing thickness between source and detector; keep distance and counting time constant; subtract background and compare corrected count rates. Follow all source-handling and safety instructions.

  4. Give the final answer to a sensible number of significant figures and include the correct unit.
