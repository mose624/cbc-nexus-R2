1. States of matter

Learning outcomes: distinguish solids, liquids and gases; describe particle separation, arrangement and motion; explain changes of state; relate gas volume to temperature and pressure; and explain diffusion using kinetic particle theory.

1.1 The particle model

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions). The particles are in constant motion. Their spacing, arrangement, speed and attractive forces help explain the properties of each state.

SolidFixed shape and volume. Particles are close together in a regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions.
LiquidFixed volume but no fixed shape. Particles are close together, irregularly arranged and slide past one another.
GasNo fixed shape or volume. Particles are far apart, move rapidly and randomly, and fill the container.

1.2 Changes of state

Melting is the change from solid to liquid; freezing is liquid to solid; boiling and evaporation are liquid to gas; condensation is gas to liquid; sublimation is solid directly to gas. During a change of state, energy is transferred but the temperature of a pure substance remains constant while the change is taking place at a fixed pressure.

1.3 Kinetic particle theory and heating curves

When a substance is heated, its particles gain energy. In a single state, the average kinetic energy and temperature increase. At the melting or boiling point, energy is used to overcome attractive forces and separate particles rather than increase their average kinetic energy; therefore the temperature stays constant until the change is complete. During cooling, the reverse occurs and energy is released to the surroundings.

Exam distinction: Boiling occurs throughout a liquid at its boiling point. Evaporation occurs only at the surface and can happen below the boiling point. Evaporation causes cooling because the higher-energy particles escape, lowering the average energy of those left behind.

1.4 Temperature and pressure effects on gas volume

Temperature: at constant pressure, heating a fixed amount of gas increases its volume. Gas particles move faster and collide more frequently/forcefully; the gas expands until its pressure balances the external pressure. Cooling reduces the volume.

Pressure: at constant temperature, increasing external pressure compresses a gas and decreases its volume. Decreasing pressure allows the gas to expand. Gases are compressible because there is substantial empty space between their particles.

1.5 Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration due to their random motion, until the particles become more evenly distributed. It occurs in gases and liquids and is generally faster in gases because gas particles move rapidly and are far apart.

Effect of relative molecular mass (Supplement): at the same temperature, lighter gas molecules generally diffuse faster than heavier molecules because their average speeds are higher. For example, ammonia (Mᵣ = 17) diffuses faster than hydrogen chloride (Mᵣ ≈ 36.5). In a tube, the white ammonium chloride ring forms closer to the HCl end because NH₃ travels farther in the same time.

Worked example

Question: A sealed flexible balloon is warmed while the external pressure remains approximately constant. Explain why its volume increases.

Solution: Heating transfers energy to the gas particles. Their average kinetic energy and speed increase. They collide more frequently and forcefully with the balloon walls. The balloon expands, increasing its volume, until the internal pressure again balances the external pressure.

1.6 Evaporation: factors that affect its rate

Evaporation happens when particles at the surface of a liquid have enough energy to escape into the gas state. It can occur at temperatures below the boiling point. The rate increases when:

  • Temperature increases: a greater proportion of particles have enough kinetic energy to escape.
  • Surface area increases: more particles are exposed at the surface at the same time.
  • Air movement increases: moving air carries vapour away from the surface, reducing the amount of vapour close to the liquid.
  • Humidity decreases: drier air can accept more water vapour before becoming saturated.
Application: Wet clothes dry faster on a warm, windy day than on a cool, still, humid day. Drying is evaporation, not boiling.

1.7 Gas pressure explained by collisions

Gas particles move randomly and collide with the walls of their container. Each collision changes the particles' momentum and exerts a force on the wall. The combined force per unit area is the gas pressure. At a fixed temperature, compressing a gas into a smaller volume makes wall collisions more frequent, so pressure increases.

1.8 Diffusion investigation: ammonia and hydrogen chloride

When cotton wool soaked in concentrated aqueous ammonia and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a long glass tube, the gases diffuse towards each other. A white ring of solid ammonium chloride forms where they meet.

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

Practical safety: Concentrated ammonia and hydrochloric acid are corrosive/irritant and release harmful vapours. This demonstration must be performed by a trained teacher in a fume cupboard, using appropriate eye protection and laboratory controls. Learners should use a teacher demonstration or approved simulation, not attempt it unsupervised.

Common misconceptions to avoid

  • Particles themselves do not expand significantly when heated; the average separation changes.
  • Particles in a solid are not stationary; they vibrate about fixed positions.
  • During melting or boiling of a pure substance at fixed pressure, temperature does not rise until the change is complete.
  • Diffusion is not the same as bulk movement caused by stirring or wind; it results from random particle motion.

2.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures

An element is a substance consisting of only one type of atom. A compound contains two or more different elements chemically bonded together in fixed proportions. A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically bonded and can be present in any proportion.

2.2 Inside the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons. Negatively charged electrons occupy shells (energy levels) around the nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

2.3 Proton number and nucleon number

Proton number (atomic number, Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus. It identifies the element. Nucleon number (mass number, A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Number of neutrons = A − Z

For a neutral atom: number of electrons = number of protons. For an ion, the proton number remains unchanged but the number of electrons differs.

Worked example: sodium-23Sodium has proton number 11 and nucleon number 23. Protons = 11; neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12; electrons in a neutral atom = 11. Its electronic configuration is 2,8,1.

2.4 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same proton number and different nucleon numbers. Neutral isotopes have the same number of electrons and therefore very similar chemical properties, because chemical reactions depend mainly on electron arrangement. Their physical properties, such as mass, can differ.

2.5 Electronic configuration and the Periodic Table

Electrons occupy shells around the nucleus. For the first 20 elements at this level, the shell capacities used in simple configurations are 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second, 8 in the third, then 2 in the fourth. Electrons fill the lower-energy shells first. The number of occupied shells indicates the period; for main-group elements, the number of outer-shell electrons helps identify the group.

2.6 Ions and ionic bonding

An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons. Metals usually lose electrons to form positive ions (cations); non-metals usually gain electrons to form negative ions (anions). The nucleus does not change during ordinary ion formation.

Ionic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It commonly forms when electrons transfer from a metal atom to a non-metal atom. The resulting ionic compound is electrically neutral overall.

Common ion charges: Group I metals form 1+ ions; Group II metals form 2+ ions; Group III metals commonly form 3+ ions; Group VI non-metals commonly form 2− ions; Group VII non-metals form 1− ions. Learn the ions specified by your syllabus and practise balancing total charge.

Worked formula example: magnesium chloride. Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine forms Cl⁻. Two chloride ions are needed to balance one magnesium ion: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0. The formula is MgCl₂.

2.7 Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points: strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in a giant ionic lattice require much energy to overcome.
  • Conduct electricity when molten or in aqueous solution: ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Do not conduct when solid: ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move through the lattice.
  • Often brittle: shifting layers can bring like charges next to each other; repulsion causes the crystal to split.

2.8 Covalent bonding

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. It forms mainly between non-metal atoms. Each atom contributes one electron to each shared pair in a simple covalent bond. Atoms share electrons to achieve a more stable outer-shell arrangement.

Simple molecular substances: molecules contain strong covalent bonds within each molecule, but the forces between molecules are usually much weaker. Therefore many simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points and do not conduct electricity because they have no mobile ions or delocalised electrons.

Worked example: methane, CH₄. Carbon has four outer-shell electrons. It shares one pair with each of four hydrogen atoms, forming four single covalent bonds. Each hydrogen achieves two electrons in its first shell; carbon achieves eight in its outer shell.

2.9 Giant covalent structures

In giant covalent structures, many atoms are joined by a continuous network of covalent bonds. A large amount of energy is needed to break these bonds, so they generally have very high melting points.

StructureBonding and arrangementProperties and uses
DiamondEach carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds in a rigid three-dimensional network.Very hard; very high melting point; does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile charged particles. Used in cutting tools and jewellery.

2.10 Metallic bonding

Metals consist of a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons. Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

Metals usually conduct electricity because delocalised electrons can move through the structure and carry charge. They conduct heat because mobile electrons transfer energy. They are malleable and ductile because layers of ions can slide while attraction to the electron sea maintains the metallic bond.

2.12 Exam technique: how to earn explanation marks

  • For ionic bonding, use the words electron transfer, oppositely charged ions and electrostatic attraction.
  • For covalent bonding, state that atoms share pairs of electrons.
  • For metallic bonding, mention positive ions, delocalised electrons and electrostatic attraction.
  • When explaining melting points, identify the structure and the forces/bonds that must be overcome.
  • When explaining conductivity, identify the mobile charged particles (ions or electrons).

Worked examples

Example 1: particle numbers in an ion An aluminium ion is Al³⁺. Aluminium has proton number 13 and nucleon number 27. Protons = 13; neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14; electrons = 13 − 3 = 10. The positive charge means three electrons have been lost.
Example 2: formula from ionic charges Calcium forms Ca²⁺ and oxide forms O²⁻. A 1:1 ratio balances the charges: (+2) + (−2) = 0. The formula is CaO. Always simplify to the smallest whole-number ratio.
Example 3: explaining graphite conductivity Graphite conducts electricity because each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, leaving one electron per carbon delocalised. These electrons are free to move along the layers and carry charge.

Common misconceptions

  • Isotopes are not different elements: they have the same proton number.
  • When an atom forms an ion, electrons change; the number of protons does not.
  • Ionic compounds are not made of separate molecules; they form giant ionic lattices.
  • Strong covalent bonds within molecules are not the same as the weaker forces between simple molecules.
  • Graphite conducts because of delocalised electrons, not because its layers slide.
  • Diamond does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile charged particles.

3. Stoichiometry

Learning outcomes: write and interpret chemical formulae; construct and balance symbol equations; calculate relative formula masses; use mole ratios to calculate reacting masses and gas volumes; determine empirical formulae; and calculate solution concentrations.

3.1 Chemical formulae and balancing equations

A chemical formula shows the elements present and the simplest whole-number ratio of their atoms (or ions). In a balanced chemical equation, the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides. This follows the law of conservation of mass: atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.

Example: magnesium reacting with oxygen Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. Unbalanced: Mg + O₂ → MgO. Balanced: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. There are two Mg atoms and two O atoms on each side.

Balancing method: write correct formulae first; count each type of atom; add whole-number coefficients in front of formulae; recount; reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio. Never change subscripts to balance an equation because that changes the substance.

3.2 Relative atomic mass and relative formula mass

Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) is the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) or relative formula mass is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms shown in a formula. It has no unit.

Mᵣ = sum of (Aᵣ × number of atoms)
Worked example 1: calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂
Aᵣ: Ca = 40, O = 16, H = 1.
Mᵣ = 40 + 2(16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74.
The bracket applies to both O and H, so there are two O atoms and two H atoms.
Worked example 2: sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄
Aᵣ: H = 1, S = 32, O = 16.
Mᵣ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98.

3.3 The mole and Avogadro constant

The mole (mol) is the amount of substance containing the Avogadro number of specified particles. The Avogadro constant is approximately 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. The particles may be atoms, molecules, ions or formula units; identify which particles are being counted.

n = m / Mᵣ

For a pure substance, amount in moles (n) = mass in grams (m) ÷ relative mass in g mol⁻¹. Rearranging gives m = n × Mᵣ. For elements, use Aᵣ in place of Mᵣ.

Worked example 3: moles in 12 g of carbon
Aᵣ(C) = 12. n = 12 ÷ 12 = 1.00 mol of carbon atoms.
Worked example 4: mass of 0.25 mol of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃
Mᵣ = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 106. Mass = n × Mᵣ = 0.25 × 106 = 26.5 g.

3.4 Using a balanced equation: mole ratios

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the reacting mole ratio. Convert the known quantity into moles, use the coefficient ratio to find the required moles, then convert to the requested unit.

Worked example 5: reacting mass
What mass of magnesium oxide forms when 4.8 g of magnesium burns completely in excess oxygen?
Equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Aᵣ(Mg) = 24; Mᵣ(MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40.
Step 1: moles Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol.
Step 2: Mg : MgO ratio = 2 : 2, so moles MgO = 0.20 mol.
Step 3: mass MgO = 0.20 × 40 = 8.0 g.
Limiting reactant: when reactants are supplied in stated amounts, the limiting reactant is used up first and determines the maximum amount of product. Calculate the possible product from each reactant; the smaller product amount identifies the limiting reactant. The other reactant is in excess.

3.5 Empirical and molecular formulae

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound. To find it from percentage composition or masses: (1) divide each mass or percentage by its Aᵣ; (2) divide all results by the smallest; (3) multiply to obtain whole numbers if necessary.

Worked example 6: empirical formula
A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen. Assume 100 g: C = 40.0/12 = 3.33 mol; H = 6.7/1 = 6.7 mol; O = 53.3/16 = 3.33 mol. Divide by 3.33: C : H : O ≈ 1 : 2 : 1. Empirical formula = CH₂O.

If the molecular mass is known, molecular formula = empirical formula × factor, where factor = molecular mass ÷ empirical formula mass. The factor must be a whole number.

Worked example 7: molecular formula
Empirical formula CH₂O has mass 12 + 2 + 16 = 30. If molecular mass = 180, factor = 180 ÷ 30 = 6. Molecular formula = C₆H₁₂O₆.

3.6 Gas volumes and the mole

At room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.), one mole of any gas occupies approximately 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³). Use the molar gas volume stated in the question or required syllabus convention. Always convert units consistently.

moles of gas = gas volume (dm³) / 24 dm³ mol⁻¹
Worked example 8: gas volume
Calculate the volume at r.t.p. of 0.15 mol of carbon dioxide. Volume = 0.15 × 24 = 3.6 dm³ (3600 cm³).

For gases reacting together, use the balanced equation's mole ratio. At the same temperature and pressure, gas volume ratios equal mole ratios.

3.7 Solution concentration

Concentration describes the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution. Molar concentration is measured in mol dm⁻³.

concentration (mol dm⁻³) = moles (mol) / volume (dm³)

Rearrangements: n = c × V and V = n ÷ c. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. Convert dm³ to cm³ by multiplying by 1000.

Worked example 9: concentration
0.020 mol of sodium hydroxide is dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution. Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³. c = 0.020 ÷ 0.250 = 0.080 mol dm⁻³.
Worked example 10: titration mole ratio
25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide exactly neutralises 20.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid. Equation: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. Ratio HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1, so moles HCl = 0.00250 mol. Concentration HCl = 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = 0.125 mol dm⁻³.

UNIT 4 • CORE + SUPPLEMENT

4. Electrochemistry

Learning outcomes: explain electrolysis in terms of mobile ions; identify anode, cathode and electrolyte; predict products for selected molten and aqueous electrolytes; write ionic half-equations; describe electroplating; and explain the operation of a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

4.1 What is electrolysis?

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of direct electric current. The liquid or solution that conducts because it contains mobile ions is the electrolyte. Electrolysis requires ions that are free to move: ions in a solid ionic lattice are fixed in position, so solid sodium chloride does not conduct by ion movement.

  • Cathode (negative electrode): positive ions (cations) move towards it and gain electrons. Reduction occurs at the cathode.
  • Anode (positive electrode): negative ions (anions) move towards it and lose electrons. Oxidation occurs at the anode.
  • Electrons move through the external wires and power supply; ions carry charge through the electrolyte.
Memory aid: Cations → cathode; anions → anode. Reduction is gain of electrons; oxidation is loss of electrons. The electrode signs described here apply to an electrolytic cell connected to a d.c. power supply.

4.2 Electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide

Molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂, contains mobile Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ ions. At the cathode, lead(II) ions gain electrons to form lead. At the anode, bromide ions lose electrons to form bromine gas.

4.3 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

An aqueous electrolyte contains ions from the dissolved compound and from water. At the cathode, a metal less reactive than hydrogen may be deposited; otherwise hydrogen is often produced. At the anode, halide ions may form halogens when present in sufficiently concentrated solution; otherwise oxygen is often produced. Always apply the syllabus rules and conditions stated in a question.

Using inert graphite or platinum electrodes, aqueous copper(II) sulfate contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so Cu²⁺ ions are discharged at the cathode. Oxygen is produced at the anode from hydroxide ions / water.

4.6 Writing and checking half-equations

A half-equation shows the electron transfer at one electrode. Check that both atoms and total charge are balanced. Electrons appear on the right when they are lost (oxidation) and on the left when they are gained (reduction).

Example A: aluminium ion reduced
Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Charge: +3 − 3 = 0 on the left; zero on the right.
Example B: chloride ion oxidised
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Atoms: two Cl on each side. Charge: −2 on each side.
Example C: copper deposited
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. One Cu atom on each side and total charge zero on both sides.

4.7 Electroplating

Electroplating coats an object with a thin layer of another metal using electrolysis. The object to be plated is the cathode (negative electrode); the plating metal is commonly the anode (positive electrode); and the electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal.

  1. Clean the object thoroughly so the coating adheres evenly.
  2. Connect the object to the negative terminal and the plating metal to the positive terminal.
  3. Immerse both in a suitable solution containing ions of the plating metal.
  4. Pass direct current. Metal ions gain electrons at the object and form a metal coating.

Uses include corrosion protection, improving appearance and giving a cheaper object a surface with the properties of a more expensive metal. The object must be clean; poor cleaning can produce an uneven or poorly attached coating.

4.8 Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells

A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy. Hydrogen is supplied to one electrode and oxygen to the other. The gases react to form water, while electrons flow through the external circuit and provide an electric current. A fuel cell continues operating while reactants are supplied; it is not simply a rechargeable battery.

Overall reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. The only chemical product of the ideal hydrogen–oxygen cell is water. In practice, the environmental impact depends on how hydrogen is produced, compressed, transported and stored.

5. Chemical energetics

Learning outcomes: distinguish exothermic and endothermic reactions; describe energy transfer between a reacting system and its surroundings; interpret reaction profile diagrams; identify activation energy and the enthalpy change; explain the energy changes involved in bond breaking and bond formation; and analyse simple temperature-change experiments.

5.1 Energy changes during chemical reactions

Chemical reactions involve changes in the arrangement of atoms and in the chemical bonds between them. Energy is transferred during these changes. In a typical school experiment, the reaction mixture and its surroundings may become warmer or cooler.

  • Exothermic reaction: transfers energy to the surroundings. The surroundings usually become warmer and the measured temperature rises. The products have less chemical energy than the reactants.
  • Endothermic reaction: takes in energy from the surroundings. The surroundings usually become cooler and the measured temperature falls. The products have more chemical energy than the reactants.
Do not confuse: exothermic does not mean “fast”, and endothermic does not mean “slow”. The terms describe the direction of energy transfer, not the reaction rate.
FeatureExothermicEndothermic
Energy transferFrom reacting system to surroundingsFrom surroundings to reacting system
Temperature of surroundingsUsually risesUsually falls
Relative energy levelsProducts lower than reactantsProducts higher than reactants
Enthalpy change, ΔHNegativePositive
ExamplesCombustion; neutralisation; many oxidation reactionsThermal decomposition; some dissolving processes; photosynthesis overall

5.2 Reaction profile diagrams

A reaction profile shows how the energy of a reacting system changes as reactants are converted into products. The vertical axis is energy; the horizontal axis is the reaction pathway (not necessarily time).

Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum energy that reacting particles must have for a successful reaction. On a reaction profile, it is the energy difference between the reactants and the top of the energy barrier. The enthalpy change (ΔH) is the energy difference between products and reactants: ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants.

5.3 Bond breaking and bond formation

Breaking a chemical bond requires energy: it is an endothermic process. Forming a chemical bond releases energy: it is an exothermic process. The overall energy change depends on the balance between energy absorbed to break bonds in the reactants and energy released when new bonds form in the products.

Rule to remember:
Energy change = energy absorbed to break bonds − energy released when bonds form.

If more energy is released by bond formation than is absorbed by bond breaking, the reaction is exothermic. If more energy is absorbed to break bonds than is released by forming new bonds, the reaction is endothermic.

Worked example 1 – bond-energy calculation: In a simplified reaction, 500 kJ mol⁻¹ is absorbed breaking bonds and 650 kJ mol⁻¹ is released forming bonds. ΔH = 500 − 650 = −150 kJ mol⁻¹. The negative value means the reaction is exothermic.

Worked example 2 – interpret the sign: A reaction has ΔH = +85 kJ mol⁻¹. The positive sign means the products have 85 kJ mol⁻¹ more energy than the reactants; the reaction is endothermic and takes in energy.

5.4 Catalysts and activation energy

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. It is not used up overall. A catalyst does not change the energy of the reactants or products and therefore does not change the overall enthalpy change, ΔH.

5.5 Investigating temperature changes

A simple method for comparing energy changes is to measure the temperature of a reaction mixture before and after a reaction. A temperature rise suggests an exothermic process; a temperature fall suggests an endothermic process.

  1. Measure a known volume of solution using a measuring cylinder or pipette.
  2. Record the initial temperature with a thermometer or temperature probe.
  3. Mix the reactants, stir consistently and record the highest or lowest temperature reached.
  4. Calculate the temperature change: ΔT = final temperature − initial temperature.
  5. Repeat the experiment and compare results, keeping volumes, concentrations and starting temperatures consistent.

Worked example 3: A mixture warms from 21.5 °C to 29.0 °C. ΔT = 29.0 − 21.5 = +7.5 °C. The temperature rises, so the reaction is exothermic.

Worked example 4: A solution cools from 24.0 °C to 18.5 °C. ΔT = 18.5 − 24.0 = −5.5 °C. The temperature falls, so the process is endothermic.

5.6 Improving reliability and evaluating results

  • Use an insulated polystyrene cup and a lid to reduce energy transfer to or from the surroundings.
  • Use a thermometer or data logger with suitable resolution; stir gently so the temperature is uniform.
  • Keep the mass or volume, concentration and initial temperature of reactants constant.
  • Record temperature at short intervals to avoid missing the maximum or minimum.
  • Repeat measurements and calculate a mean; identify anomalous results rather than automatically including them.
  • State that heat exchange with surroundings and heat absorbed by the container make the measured temperature change less than the ideal value.
Common examination errors: do not say that breaking bonds releases energy; do not label activation energy as the same as ΔH; do not say a catalyst changes ΔH; and always include the sign and unit when reporting an enthalpy change.

6. Chemical reactions

6.1 Physical changes and chemical changes

A physical change changes the state, shape or arrangement of a substance without producing a new substance. Examples include melting ice, boiling water, dissolving sugar and cutting a metal. Many physical changes can be reversed by a physical process.

A chemical change produces one or more new substances with different chemical properties. Examples include burning magnesium, rusting iron, reacting an acid with a carbonate and decomposing hydrogen peroxide. Chemical changes often involve energy transfer and may show colour change, gas formation or precipitate formation, but no single observation proves a chemical reaction on its own.

Recognising reaction patterns helps you predict products and write balanced equations. Learn the general pattern, then check the conditions and reactivity of the substances involved.

Worked example 1: Magnesium reacts with oxygen. The word equation is magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. The balanced symbol equation is 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Two magnesium atoms and two oxygen atoms are present on each side.

6.3 Rate of reaction

The rate of reaction measures how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. A reaction is faster when a measurable change occurs in a shorter time. Rate can be followed by measuring gas volume, mass loss, concentration, colour intensity or the time taken for a visible change.

For a simple average-rate calculation:

Average rate = change in measured quantity ÷ time taken
For example, if 60 cm³ of gas is produced in 30 s, average rate = 60 ÷ 30 = 2.0 cm³ s⁻¹.

When a graph shows product volume against time, the gradient represents rate. A steeper gradient means a faster reaction. The curve becomes horizontal when no more product is being formed, so the reaction has stopped or reached completion under the conditions used.

6.4 Collision theory

For a reaction to occur, reacting particles must collide. A collision only leads to reaction if particles collide with sufficient energy (at least the activation energy) and with a suitable orientation. Increasing the number of successful collisions per second increases the reaction rate.

6.6 Investigating reaction rates

Method A – gas collection: React a measured mass of marble chips (calcium carbonate) with a measured volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask connected to a gas syringe. Record the carbon dioxide volume at regular time intervals. Keep the mass and size of chips, acid volume, acid concentration and temperature controlled when testing another variable.

Method B – loss of mass: Place a flask containing acid on a balance, add marble chips and record the mass at regular intervals. Carbon dioxide escapes, so the measured mass decreases. Use a cotton-wool plug to reduce splashing while allowing gas to escape; do not seal a gas-producing flask.

Method C – disappearing cross: Mix sodium thiosulfate solution with dilute hydrochloric acid over a marked cross. Sulfur forms and makes the mixture cloudy. Record the time until the cross can no longer be seen. Use the same viewing conditions and volumes. This method gives a comparative rate, often estimated as 1 ÷ time.

Fair test: change only the independent variable; keep other relevant variables constant. Repeat trials, calculate a mean, and identify anomalous results. Wear eye protection and follow the laboratory risk assessment, especially when handling acids or producing gases.

6.7 Reversible reactions

A reversible reaction can proceed in both directions. The forward reaction converts reactants into products; the reverse reaction converts products back into reactants. The symbol ⇌ is used to show that a reaction is reversible.

Example: hydrated copper(II) sulfate can be dehydrated by heating to form white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Adding water to the anhydrous solid reforms the blue hydrated compound:

CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) ⇌ CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)
Blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate ⇌ white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate + water.

Another familiar example is the hydration of cobalt(II) chloride, which changes colour as water is removed or added. Colour observations depend on the particular compound and conditions.

6.8 Dynamic equilibrium

In a closed system, a reversible reaction may reach dynamic equilibrium. At equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates. The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant, although the reactions have not stopped. The amounts of reactants and products are not necessarily equal.

6.9 Factors that affect equilibrium position

For reversible reactions where equilibrium shifts are considered, use the following qualitative rules (Le Chatelier's principle): a system responds to a change by shifting in the direction that opposes that change.

  • Concentration: adding a reactant tends to shift equilibrium towards products; removing a product also tends to shift towards products.
  • Pressure (gaseous systems): increasing pressure tends to favour the side with fewer moles of gas; decreasing pressure tends to favour the side with more moles of gas. If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, pressure has no effect on the position.
  • Temperature: increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction; decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction.
  • Catalyst: speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally and helps equilibrium be reached sooner; it does not change the equilibrium position.

Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) is exothermic in the forward direction. Higher pressure favours ammonia because there are fewer gas molecules on the product side (2 rather than 4). Lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction, although it also makes the reaction slower.

6.10 Oxidation and reduction

Oxidation and reduction occur together in redox reactions. In terms of electrons, oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain of electrons. The memory aid is OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

In reactions involving oxygen, oxidation can also be described as gain of oxygen and reduction as loss of oxygen. In terms of hydrogen, oxidation can involve loss of hydrogen and reduction can involve gain of hydrogen. Use the definition most appropriate to the question.

7.1 Acids, bases and alkalis

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), when dissolved in water. An alkali is a soluble base that produces hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq), in water. A base neutralises an acid; bases include metal oxides, metal hydroxides and some carbonates. Not all bases are soluble, so not all bases are alkalis.

7.3 pH scale and indicators

The pH scale is used to describe how acidic or alkaline an aqueous solution is. At about 25 °C, pH 7 is neutral; values below 7 are acidic and values above 7 are alkaline. 7.4 Neutralisation and ionic equations

Neutralisation occurs when hydrogen ions from an acid react with hydroxide ions from an alkali to form water. The net ionic equation is:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

For hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide:

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Chloride and sodium ions are spectator ions; they remain unchanged in solution. A complete ionic equation is H⁺ + Cl⁻ + Na⁺ + OH⁻ → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + H₂O. Removing spectator ions gives the net ionic equation above.

Worked example 1: Sulfuric acid reacts with potassium hydroxide. The balanced equation is H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. Two moles of KOH are required for each mole of H₂SO₄ because sulfuric acid can supply two hydrogen ions per formula unit.

7.5 Naming salts

The name of a salt depends on the metal (or ammonium) ion and the acid used:

  • Hydrochloric acid forms chlorides.
  • Nitric acid forms nitrates.
  • Sulfuric acid forms sulfates.

Examples: magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride; copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid → copper(II) sulfate; sodium hydroxide + nitric acid → sodium nitrate.

7.6 Preparing a soluble salt from an insoluble base

To prepare a soluble salt such as copper(II) sulfate from an insoluble base such as copper(II) oxide:

  1. Warm a measured volume of dilute sulfuric acid gently in a beaker. Do not boil it.
  2. Add copper(II) oxide in small portions while stirring until some solid remains unreacted. This ensures the acid has been used up.
  3. Filter the mixture to remove excess unreacted copper(II) oxide.
  4. Heat the filtrate gently to concentrate it, stopping before it becomes dry.
  5. Allow the solution to cool so crystals form. If needed, leave it in a warm place to evaporate slowly.
  6. Filter the crystals and dry them between filter papers or in a suitable warm place.

Why add excess base? It ensures all the acid reacts. The excess solid can then be removed by filtration, leaving a salt solution without excess acid.

7.7 Preparing a soluble salt by titration

If both reactants are soluble, such as hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, excess reactant cannot be removed by filtration. Titration is used to find the exact volumes that neutralise one another. The accurate neutralisation is first carried out with an indicator. The same measured volumes are then mixed again without indicator to obtain a purer salt solution for crystallisation.

  1. Rinse the burette with the solution it will contain, then fill it and remove any air bubble from the jet.
  2. Use a pipette to transfer a fixed volume of alkali into a conical flask; add a few drops of a suitable indicator.
  3. Record the initial burette reading at eye level.
  4. Add acid while swirling the flask. Near the end-point, add it drop by drop until the indicator just changes colour permanently.
  5. Record the final burette reading. Titre = final reading − initial reading.
  6. Repeat until concordant titres are obtained (commonly within 0.10–0.20 cm³, according to the practical instructions), then calculate a suitable mean, excluding a rough titre and any justified anomalous result.

Worked example 2: Initial burette reading = 1.20 cm³; final reading = 24.65 cm³. Titre = 24.65 − 1.20 = 23.45 cm³.

End-point and equivalence point: the end-point is the observed indicator colour change. The equivalence point is when reactants have combined in the exact stoichiometric ratio. A suitable indicator is chosen so its colour-change range is close to the equivalence point.

7.8 Preparing an insoluble salt by precipitation

An insoluble salt can be prepared by mixing two soluble solutions that provide the required ions. The insoluble product forms as a precipitate and is separated by filtration.

Example – barium sulfate: mix aqueous barium chloride with aqueous sodium sulfate. A white precipitate of barium sulfate forms.

BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

Net ionic equation: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).

  1. Prepare separate solutions of two suitable soluble salts.
  2. Mix the solutions and stir; the insoluble salt forms as a precipitate.
  3. Filter the mixture to collect the solid on filter paper.
  4. Wash the precipitate with distilled water to remove soluble impurities.
  5. Dry the solid between filter papers or in a suitable drying oven.

8. The Periodic Table

Learning outcomes: explain how elements are arranged by proton number; interpret groups and periods using electronic configurations; describe the properties and trends of Group I, Group VII and noble gases; explain halogen displacement reactions; and recognise characteristic properties of transition elements.

8.1 Arrangement of the Periodic Table

The modern Periodic Table arranges elements in order of increasing proton (atomic) number. The horizontal rows are called periods; the vertical columns are called groups. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell (for the main-group elements).

  • Period number: tells you the number of occupied electron shells in a simple atom.
  • Group number: for Groups I–VII, tells you the number of outer-shell electrons. Group 0 (also called Group 18) contains noble gases with full outer shells.
  • Across a period: proton number increases by one from one element to the next. The outer shell being filled remains the same shell.
  • Down a group: atoms have an additional occupied electron shell at each step; atomic size generally increases.
Worked example: An atom has electron configuration 2,8,7. It has three occupied shells, so it is in Period 3. It has seven outer-shell electrons, so it is in Group VII (modern Group 17). It is a halogen.

8.2 Group I – the alkali metals

Lithium, sodium and potassium are soft, reactive metals. They have one electron in the outer shell and form +1 ions by losing that electron. They are stored under oil to prevent contact with oxygen and water. Their reactions with water become more vigorous down the group.

ElementObservation with cold waterTrend / explanation
LithiumFloats, moves slowly, fizzes gentlyLeast vigorous of these three
SodiumMoves rapidly, melts into a silvery ball, fizzes stronglyMore vigorous than lithium
PotassiumVery vigorous; may ignite with a lilac flameMost vigorous of these three

General equation: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂, where M represents an alkali metal. For example: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. The solution becomes alkaline because a metal hydroxide forms, and hydrogen gas is released.

Why does reactivity increase down Group I? The outer electron is in a shell farther from the nucleus and is more shielded by inner electrons. The attraction between the nucleus and this outer electron becomes weaker, so it is lost more easily.

8.3 Group VII – the halogens

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are non-metals. They exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂), have seven outer-shell electrons, and gain one electron to form halide ions with a −1 charge. Their colours become darker down the group and their physical states change from gases to liquid to solid at room temperature.

Trend in reactivity: halogen reactivity decreases down Group VII. A halogen atom gains an electron during reaction. Down the group, the outer shell is farther from the nucleus and shielding increases, so the attraction for an incoming electron is weaker.

8.4 Halogen displacement reactions

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide ions; bromine displaces iodide ions; iodine does not displace chloride or bromide ions.

8.5 Group 0 / Group 18 – noble gases

Helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon are colourless, monatomic gases with very low chemical reactivity. Their outer electron shells are full (helium has a full first shell of two electrons; the others have eight outer-shell electrons). They do not normally form ions or compounds in the conditions studied at this level.

Trend down the group: noble gases have increasing boiling points down the group. Their atoms become larger and have more electrons, so temporary induced dipole attractions (London dispersion forces) become stronger. More energy is needed to separate the atoms.

8.6 Transition elements

Transition elements are metals found in the central block of the Periodic Table. Compared with Group I metals, they are generally harder, stronger and denser, have higher melting points, and are less reactive. Many form coloured compounds, show variable oxidation states, and act as catalysts.

  • Variable oxidation states: an element can form ions with different charges, for example iron(II), Fe²⁺, and iron(III), Fe³⁺.
  • Coloured compounds: many transition-metal compounds are coloured; for example, copper(II) compounds are often blue or blue-green.
  • Catalysts: iron is used in the Haber process; nickel is used in hydrogenation; transition-metal compounds can catalyse other reactions.
  • Physical properties: transition metals are typically strong, dense and have high melting points, although there are exceptions.

  • Do not say Group I reactivity decreases down the group: it increases.

9. Metals

9.1 Physical properties and uses of metals

Most metals are good conductors of heat and electricity, are strong, malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires). Many have high melting points and high densities, although there are exceptions such as mercury, which is liquid at room temperature, and some low-density metals.

9.2 The reactivity series

The reactivity series ranks metals by how readily they take part in reactions. A useful school-level order is:

Most reactive → least reactive: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon (reference), zinc, iron, hydrogen (reference), copper, silver, gold. Carbon and hydrogen are included as reference points, not as metals.

A metal higher in the series can displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. A metal above hydrogen can usually react with dilute hydrochloric acid or dilute sulfuric acid to produce hydrogen gas; metals below hydrogen generally do not. The exact reaction rate also depends on conditions and the metal's surface.

9.4 Displacement reactions

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution containing its ions. For example, zinc displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate solution:

Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Ionic equation: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). Blue copper(II) sulfate solution becomes paler as Cu²⁺ ions are removed, and a reddish-brown copper deposit forms on the zinc. Copper cannot displace zinc from zinc sulfate because copper is less reactive.

Worked example: Magnesium is placed in iron(II) sulfate solution. Magnesium is more reactive than iron, so it displaces iron: Mg + FeSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Fe. The ionic equation is Mg + Fe²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Fe.

9.5 Extraction of metals from ores

Most metals occur naturally in compounds called ores. The method used to extract a metal depends on its position in the reactivity series and the stability of its compounds.

  • Very reactive metals such as aluminium are extracted by electrolysis of molten compounds. Carbon is not a sufficiently strong reducing agent for extracting aluminium from aluminium oxide.
  • Less reactive metals such as iron can be extracted by reduction of their oxides using carbon or carbon monoxide in a furnace.
  • Very unreactive metals such as gold may occur native (uncombined) in the Earth's crust.

9.6 Extraction of iron in the blast furnace

Iron is extracted from haematite, mainly iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃. A blast furnace is charged with iron ore, coke (carbon) and limestone (calcium carbonate). A blast of hot air enters near the bottom.

  1. Coke burns in oxygen and releases heat: C + O₂ → CO₂.
  2. Carbon dioxide reacts with hot coke to form carbon monoxide: CO₂ + C → 2CO.
  3. Carbon monoxide reduces iron(III) oxide to iron: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
  4. Limestone thermally decomposes: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.
  5. Calcium oxide reacts with acidic silica impurities to form slag: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃.

9.7 Extraction of aluminium by electrolysis

Aluminium is extracted from purified aluminium oxide (alumina), Al₂O₃, dissolved in molten cryolite. Cryolite lowers the operating temperature and helps the mixture conduct electricity. The cell uses carbon electrodes; the carbon anodes are gradually consumed because oxygen produced at the anode reacts with hot carbon.

  • Cathode (negative): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Molten aluminium forms and collects at the bottom.
  • Anode (positive): 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻. Oxygen reacts with carbon: C + O₂ → CO₂ (some CO may also form).

Overall simplified equation: 2Al₂O₃(l) → 4Al(l) + 3O₂(g). The carbon anodes must be replaced periodically. Large amounts of electrical energy are required, which makes aluminium extraction expensive.

9.8 Recycling metals

Recycling metals conserves finite mineral resources, reduces the need for mining, often uses less energy than extraction from ores, and reduces waste and environmental damage. However, collection, sorting, transport and purification also require energy and money. Aluminium recycling is especially valuable because primary aluminium extraction uses large amounts of electricity.

9.9 Corrosion and rusting

Corrosion is the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reactions with substances in its environment. Rusting is the corrosion of iron. Iron rusts when both oxygen and water are present. Salt solutions speed up rusting because they increase electrical conductivity and help the electrochemical processes occur.

Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, often written as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. Rust is porous and flakes away, exposing fresh iron to further corrosion.

9.12 Common exam errors

  • Do not state that all metals react with cold water or dilute acids. Compare the metal with hydrogen and use the specified conditions.

  • An alloy is a mixture, not a pure compound with a fixed chemical formula.

10.3 Complete and incomplete combustion

When a hydrocarbon burns in a plentiful supply of oxygen, complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water. When oxygen is limited, incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot), as well as water.

Complete combustion: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

Incomplete combustion to carbon monoxide: 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O.

Incomplete combustion to carbon: CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O.

Safety: Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless. Never rely on smell to detect it. Fuel-burning appliances need adequate ventilation and appropriate maintenance and carbon-monoxide alarms where required.

10.4 Acid rain

Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolve in atmospheric water and undergo reactions that produce acids. These acidic substances can return to the Earth's surface in rain, snow, fog or dry deposition. Acid deposition can lower the pH of lakes and soils, harm aquatic organisms and plants, and accelerate the corrosion of limestone buildings and some metals.

Representative equations include:

  • Sulfur dioxide dissolves and can be oxidised to sulfuric acid: 2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.
  • Nitrogen dioxide can form nitric acid in the presence of oxygen and water: 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃.
  • Acids react with calcium carbonate in limestone: CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O.

10.5 The greenhouse effect and climate change

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth's surface warmer than it would otherwise be. Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The surface emits infrared radiation; greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of this outgoing infrared energy. Human activities increase concentrations of greenhouse gases, strengthening this effect and contributing to global warming and climate change.

Possible consequences of climate change include rising average temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, more frequent or intense heat extremes in many regions, melting glaciers and ice sheets, sea-level rise and impacts on ecosystems, agriculture, water availability and human health. The exact effects vary by region.

Exam distinction: The greenhouse effect is a natural process. The enhanced greenhouse effect is the additional warming caused by increased greenhouse-gas concentrations from human activities. Do not say that the ozone hole is the cause of global warming; ozone depletion and climate change are different environmental issues.

10.7 Eutrophication

Eutrophication occurs when excess nutrients, especially nitrates and phosphates, enter a water body. These nutrients promote rapid algal growth. When algae die, decomposer microorganisms break down the organic matter and use dissolved oxygen. Oxygen levels may fall so low that fish and other aquatic organisms die.

10.8 Water treatment and purification

Water treatment makes water suitable for its intended use. A typical drinking-water treatment plant may use several stages. The exact sequence varies according to the quality of the source water and local regulations.

  1. Screening: removes large debris such as leaves and rubbish.
  2. Sedimentation: allows heavier suspended particles to settle; a coagulant such as aluminium sulfate may be added to help fine particles clump together.
  3. Filtration: water passes through sand, gravel or other filter media to remove remaining suspended particles.
  4. Disinfection: chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet treatment is used to reduce harmful microorganisms. A controlled chlorine residual may protect water in distribution systems.
  5. Storage and testing: treated water is checked against safety standards before supply.

Important: Boiling kills many disease-causing microorganisms but does not remove dissolved salts, heavy metals or many chemical pollutants. Filtration alone does not guarantee that water is microbiologically safe. Safe treatment requires methods appropriate to the contaminants present.

10.10 Practical investigation: measuring water quality

Water quality can be investigated using simple measurements, but no single test proves that water is safe to drink.

  • pH: use a calibrated pH meter or suitable indicator to test acidity or alkalinity.
  • Turbidity: compare clarity using a turbidity tube or meter. Cloudiness suggests suspended particles but does not identify the contaminant.
  • Dissolved oxygen: use a dissolved-oxygen probe or approved test kit. Low dissolved oxygen can indicate organic pollution or eutrophication.
  • Nitrate / phosphate: use suitable test kits to detect nutrient enrichment.
  • Microbiological testing: trained personnel use approved methods to test for indicator organisms such as E. coli. Do not culture unknown environmental samples at home or taste test water.
Worked data example: A lake sample has dissolved oxygen of 3 mg dm⁻³, while an upstream sample has 8 mg dm⁻³. The downstream value is lower by 5 mg dm⁻³. This may indicate increased oxygen demand, for example from decomposing organic matter. Further tests are needed before identifying the cause.

10.11 Interpreting environmental data

In exam questions, identify the trend, quote supporting data and then explain a chemical or biological mechanism. For example, if a graph shows rising carbon dioxide concentration and rising average temperature over time, state that both increase, quote values or dates, and explain that carbon dioxide absorbs outgoing infrared radiation. A correlation alone does not prove that one measured variable is the only cause of the other.

10.12 Common misconceptions and exam technique

  • Air is a mixture, not a compound. Nitrogen is the largest component of dry air, not oxygen.
  • Carbon monoxide is formed by incomplete combustion and is poisonous; carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas.
  • Acid rain is mainly linked to sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, not directly to carbon dioxide.
  • The natural greenhouse effect is necessary for a habitable Earth; the enhanced effect results from increased greenhouse-gas concentrations.
  • Ozone depletion and climate change are separate issues, although some substances can affect both.
  • Eutrophication begins with nutrient enrichment; oxygen depletion occurs when decomposers break down excess organic matter.
  • Water that looks clear is not necessarily safe to drink. Microorganisms and dissolved chemicals may be invisible.
  • When evaluating a control method, mention both its benefit and a possible limitation, cost or energy requirement.

11. Organic chemistry

Learning outcomes: describe crude oil and fractional distillation; identify homologous series and functional groups; compare alkanes and alkenes; explain combustion and addition reactions; describe ethanol production, carboxylic acids, esters and addition polymers.

11.1 Organic compounds and hydrocarbons

Organic chemistry studies carbon compounds. A hydrocarbon contains carbon and hydrogen only. Carbon forms four covalent bonds and can make chains, branches and rings. A homologous series is a family with the same functional group and general formula, similar chemical properties, and a gradual change in physical properties. Successive members differ by –CH₂–.

AlkanesSaturated hydrocarbons with C–C single bonds. Open-chain general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
AlkenesUnsaturated hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond. One double bond in an open chain: CₙH₂ₙ.
AlcoholsContain the hydroxyl functional group, –OH. Example: ethanol, C₂H₅OH.

11.2 Crude oil and fractional distillation

Crude oil is a finite, naturally occurring mixture of hydrocarbons. It is separated into fractions by fractional distillation. The crude oil is heated and most vaporises. Vapours enter a column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Each fraction condenses over a range of temperatures at a different height because the hydrocarbons have different boiling-point ranges. Fractions are mixtures, not pure substances.

FractionTypical useGeneral trend
Refinery gasesCooking and heatingVery low boiling points; very volatile
PetrolCar fuelLow boiling range; flows easily
KeroseneJet fuel and heatingHigher boiling range than petrol
DieselDiesel enginesLonger chains; more viscous
Fuel oilShips and industrial heatingHigh boiling range
Residue / bitumenRoad surfacing and roofingVery high boiling point; very viscous

As chain length increases, boiling point and viscosity generally increase, while volatility and ease of ignition generally decrease.

11.3 Alkanes and alkenes

Alkanes are saturated because they contain only carbon–carbon single bonds. They are relatively unreactive. Alkenes are unsaturated because they contain a C=C double bond and undergo addition reactions. Prefixes meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent- and hex- indicate 1–6 carbon atoms.

11.4 Combustion and substitution

Complete combustion in excess oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water. Example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. In limited oxygen, incomplete combustion may produce poisonous carbon monoxide and/or carbon soot. Alkanes can undergo substitution with chlorine in ultraviolet light: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl.

11.5 Addition reactions and bromine-water test

Alkenes undergo addition across the C=C bond. Ethene reacts with hydrogen to form ethane, with steam to form ethanol under suitable industrial conditions, and with bromine to form 1,2-dibromoethane: C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂. To test for an alkene, shake with orange/brown bromine water. It turns colourless. An alkane does not decolourise bromine water under ordinary conditions without UV light.

Exam wording: State the observation and inference: “orange bromine water turns colourless, showing the presence of a carbon–carbon double bond / unsaturation.”

11.6 Ethanol

Ethanol is made by fermentation of glucose using yeast in warm, anaerobic conditions: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂. A suitable warm temperature allows yeast enzymes to work; excessive heat denatures enzymes and low temperature slows fermentation. Ethanol is separated by distillation. Industrial hydration of ethene with steam is an alternative. Fermentation uses renewable sugars but is relatively slow and produces dilute ethanol; hydration is continuous and faster but uses ethene, usually derived from crude oil.

11.7 Carboxylic acids and esters

Carboxylic acids contain –COOH. Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, is found in vinegar and is a weak acid. It reacts with reactive metals to produce a salt and hydrogen, and with carbonates to produce a salt, water and carbon dioxide. Ethanoic acid reacts with ethanol in the presence of an acid catalyst to form ethyl ethanoate and water: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O. Esters often have fruity smells and are used in flavourings and fragrances.

11.8 Addition polymers

Many alkene molecules join when their C=C double bonds open and new C–C single bonds form. Ethene forms poly(ethene), with repeating unit –CH₂–CH₂–. The polymer is represented as [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ. Plastics are durable and useful but many persist in the environment, fragment into microplastics and can harm wildlife. Reduce unnecessary use, reuse, sort recyclable materials and dispose of waste responsibly.

Combustion: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.

12.3 Paper chromatography and Rf

Place a small sample spot on a pencil baseline above the solvent level. The solvent rises through the paper and carries dissolved substances. Different substances travel different distances because of their different attractions to the stationary paper and mobile solvent. Pencil is used because graphite does not dissolve; ink may separate into dyes. A pure substance usually gives one spot in a suitable solvent; a mixture may give several.

Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent front

Worked example: A dye travels 4.2 cm and the solvent front travels 7.0 cm. Rf = 4.2 ÷ 7.0 = 0.60. Rf has no unit and is normally between 0 and 1. Compare values only when solvent and conditions are the same.

12.4 Acid–base titration

Use a pipette and filler to transfer a fixed volume of one solution into a conical flask. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator. Fill a burette with the other solution and record the initial reading. Add solution while swirling; near the end-point add dropwise. Record the final reading. Titre = final reading − initial reading. Repeat until concordant titres are obtained and calculate their mean.

Accuracy: rinse the burette with the solution it will contain and the pipette with the solution it will transfer; remove air bubbles from the jet; remove the funnel before readings; add dropwise near the end-point. Choose an indicator suitable for the acid–alkali combination.

Worked calculation: 25.0 cm³ NaOH is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1:1). Moles HCl = 0.100 × 20.0/1000 = 0.00200 mol. Moles NaOH = 0.00200 mol. Concentration NaOH = 0.00200 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0800 mol dm⁻³.
