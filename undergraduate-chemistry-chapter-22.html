Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Quantum chemistry replaces classical trajectories with wavefunctions and probability amplitudes. It explains discrete atomic spectra, orbital structure, chemical bonding and molecular response to light. Predictions depend on the Hamiltonian and approximations used; quantum theory does not imply that electrons are tiny planets moving on fixed paths.

The Born interpretation gives probability density |ψ|². A normalised bound-state wavefunction satisfies ∫|ψ|²dτ=1. The wavefunction may be complex, but probability density is real and non-negative. Nodes are regions where ψ=0 and therefore the probability of detecting the particle is zero.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is Ĥψ=Eψ. The Hamiltonian includes kinetic and potential-energy operators. Boundary conditions restrict acceptable solutions and produce quantised eigenvalues. Exact analytic solutions are limited to simple systems such as hydrogen-like atoms and idealised model potentials.

Observables are represented by Hermitian operators. Their eigenvalues are real and correspond to possible measurement outcomes. The expectation value ⟨A⟩=∫ψ*Âψdτ is the statistical mean for an ensemble of identically prepared systems. Non-commuting operators encode limits on simultaneous precision.

For a one-dimensional infinite well, ψn=(2/L)^1/2 sin(nπx/L) and En=n²h²/(8mL²). Energy rises with n² and falls as the box length squared. The ground state has non-zero zero-point energy because the wavefunction must satisfy zero boundary values at both walls.

Hydrogen-like orbitals are described by n, l and ml; spin is ms. n controls energy and radial extent, l controls angular momentum and orbital type, and ml specifies orientation. Allowed values are 0≤l≤n−1 and −l≤ml≤+l.

The radial node count is n−l−1 and angular node count is l, giving n−1 total nodes. s orbitals have spherical angular distributions; p orbitals have a nodal plane; d and f orbitals have more complex angular patterns. Orbital pictures are probability-density or wavefunction visualisations, not hard boundaries.

In many-electron atoms, electron-electron repulsion and shielding make orbital energies depend on occupancy. Effective nuclear charge is the net attraction experienced by an electron. Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules provide a useful filling scheme, but near-degenerate subshells produce exceptions.

The variational principle states that the expectation energy of a valid normalised trial function is not below the exact ground-state energy. Optimising adjustable parameters improves the upper bound. Perturbation theory adds corrections when the perturbing interaction is sufficiently small relative to the reference problem.

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation separates electronic and nuclear motion because nuclei are much heavier. Solve the electronic problem at fixed nuclear geometry to obtain a potential-energy surface; then treat nuclear vibration and rotation on that surface. Strong non-adiabatic coupling near crossings can invalidate the approximation.

Atomic orbitals combine into molecular orbitals when symmetry and overlap allow. In LCAO, ψMO=cAψA+cBψB. In-phase combination creates a bonding orbital with increased electron density between nuclei; out-of-phase combination creates an antibonding orbital with a node between nuclei.

Bond order in simple MO theory is (Nb−Na)/2. Greater bond order often corresponds to shorter, stronger bonds, but the relationship is approximate. Electron filling of antibonding orbitals can lower bond order and explain differences in stability and magnetic behaviour.

The HOMO is the highest occupied molecular orbital and the LUMO the lowest unoccupied. Frontier-orbital interactions help rationalise nucleophilic/electrophilic roles, donor–acceptor complexes and pericyclic selection rules. Symmetry and overlap are essential; energy gap alone is not a full reactivity predictor.

Absorption occurs when hν matches an allowed energy gap. Transition probability depends on the transition dipole moment and symmetry selection rules. Electronic spectra may show vibronic structure because electronic transitions occur alongside changes in vibrational state.

Computational methods range from semi-empirical and density-functional approaches to correlated wavefunction methods. Report the functional or method, basis set, charge, spin multiplicity, solvent model and convergence criteria. A computed minimum is not automatically a kinetically accessible product or a validated experimental structure.
