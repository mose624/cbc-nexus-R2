Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Quantum chemistry replaces classical trajectories with wavefunctions and probability amplitudes. It explains discrete atomic spectra, orbital structure, chemical bonding and molecular response to light. Predictions depend on the Hamiltonian and approximations used; quantum theory does not imply that electrons are tiny planets moving on fixed paths.

The Born interpretation gives probability density |ψ|². A normalised bound-state wavefunction satisfies ∫|ψ|²dτ=1. The wavefunction may be complex, but probability density is real and non-negative. Nodes are regions where ψ=0 and therefore the probability of detecting the particle is zero.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is Ĥψ=Eψ. The Hamiltonian includes kinetic and potential-energy operators. Boundary conditions restrict acceptable solutions and produce quantised eigenvalues. Exact analytic solutions are limited to simple systems such as hydrogen-like atoms and idealised model potentials.

Observables are represented by Hermitian operators. Their eigenvalues are real and correspond to possible measurement outcomes. The expectation value ⟨A⟩=∫ψ*Âψdτ is the statistical mean for an ensemble of identically prepared systems. Non-commuting operators encode limits on simultaneous precision.

For a one-dimensional infinite well, ψn=(2/L)^1/2 sin(nπx/L) and En=n²h²/(8mL²). Energy rises with n² and falls as the box length squared. The ground state has non-zero zero-point energy because the wavefunction must satisfy zero boundary values at both walls.

Hydrogen-like orbitals are described by n, l and ml; spin is ms. n controls energy and radial extent, l controls angular momentum and orbital type, and ml specifies orientation. Allowed values are 0≤l≤n−1 and −l≤ml≤+l.

The radial node count is n−l−1 and angular node count is l, giving n−1 total nodes. s orbitals have spherical angular distributions; p orbitals have a nodal plane; d and f orbitals have more complex angular patterns. Orbital pictures are probability-density or wavefunction visualisations, not hard boundaries.

In many-electron atoms, electron-electron repulsion and shielding make orbital energies depend on occupancy. Effective nuclear charge is the net attraction experienced by an electron. Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules provide a useful filling scheme, but near-degenerate subshells produce exceptions.

The variational principle states that the expectation energy of a valid normalised trial function is not below the exact ground-state energy. Optimising adjustable parameters improves the upper bound. Perturbation theory adds corrections when the perturbing interaction is sufficiently small relative to the reference problem.

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation separates electronic and nuclear motion because nuclei are much heavier. Solve the electronic problem at fixed nuclear geometry to obtain a potential-energy surface; then treat nuclear vibration and rotation on that surface. Strong non-adiabatic coupling near crossings can invalidate the approximation.

Atomic orbitals combine into molecular orbitals when symmetry and overlap allow. In LCAO, ψMO=cAψA+cBψB. In-phase combination creates a bonding orbital with increased electron density between nuclei; out-of-phase combination creates an antibonding orbital with a node between nuclei.

Bond order in simple MO theory is (Nb−Na)/2. Greater bond order often corresponds to shorter, stronger bonds, but the relationship is approximate. Electron filling of antibonding orbitals can lower bond order and explain differences in stability and magnetic behaviour.

The HOMO is the highest occupied molecular orbital and the LUMO the lowest unoccupied. Frontier-orbital interactions help rationalise nucleophilic/electrophilic roles, donor–acceptor complexes and pericyclic selection rules. Symmetry and overlap are essential; energy gap alone is not a full reactivity predictor.

Absorption occurs when hν matches an allowed energy gap. Transition probability depends on the transition dipole moment and symmetry selection rules. Electronic spectra may show vibronic structure because electronic transitions occur alongside changes in vibrational state.

Computational methods range from semi-empirical and density-functional approaches to correlated wavefunction methods. Report the functional or method, basis set, charge, spin multiplicity, solvent model and convergence criteria. A computed minimum is not automatically a kinetically accessible product or a validated experimental structure.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Quantum chemistry: mathematical foundations and derivations

1. Normalisation and probability

The probability of finding a particle in a volume element dτ is |ψ|²dτ. A bound-state wavefunction is normalised by ∫all space |ψ|²dτ = 1. If ψ is not normalised, write ψN = Nψ and choose N so that N²∫|ψ|²dτ = 1. The wavefunction itself may be complex; probability density is real and non-negative.

2. Particle in a one-dimensional box

Inside a box from x=0 to L, potential energy is zero; outside, it is infinite, so ψ(0)=ψ(L)=0. The Schrödinger equation becomes −(ħ²/2m)d²ψ/dx²=Eψ. Its acceptable solutions are ψn=(2/L)½sin(nπx/L), with n=1,2,3… and En=n²h²/(8mL²). The non-zero ground-state energy is zero-point energy; confinement forbids a stationary state with zero kinetic energy.

3. Variational principle

For a normalised trial function ψ, Etrial = ⟨ψ|Ĥ|ψ⟩ is greater than or equal to the exact ground-state energy. Select a physically sensible trial function containing adjustable parameters, evaluate the expectation value, and minimise with respect to those parameters. The resulting energy is an upper bound, not necessarily the exact energy.

4. Molecular orbital bond order

In a simple MO model, electrons fill orbitals according to Aufbau, Pauli and Hund principles. Bond order = (Nb−Na)/2. Constructive overlap produces bonding orbitals; destructive overlap produces antibonding orbitals. The O2 ground state has two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals, explaining its paramagnetism where a simple Lewis structure does not.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Particle in a box energy scaling

Problem: An electron is confined to a box of length 0.200 nm. Calculate E1 and E2.

Solution: E1=h²/(8mL²). Substituting h=6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s, m=9.109×10⁻³¹ kg and L=2.00×10⁻¹⁰ m gives E1=1.51×10⁻¹⁸ J. E2=4E1=6.04×10⁻¹⁸ J; ΔE21=3E1=4.53×10⁻¹⁸ J.

Worked numerical problem 2: Photon wavelength from energy gap

Problem: A transition has ΔE=3.20×10⁻¹⁹ J. Find its wavelength.

Solution: λ=hc/ΔE=(6.626×10⁻³⁴×2.998×10⁸)/(3.20×10⁻¹⁹)=6.20×10⁻⁷ m=620 nm.

Worked numerical problem 3: MO bond order and magnetism

Problem: A diatomic species has 10 bonding and 6 antibonding electrons, with two unpaired electrons in the highest occupied orbitals.

Solution: Bond order=(10−6)/2=2. The unpaired electrons predict paramagnetism. Bond order suggests a net double-bond character within the simple MO model.

Worked numerical problem 4: Normalisation of a constant wavefunction

Problem: A particle has ψ=A over a one-dimensional interval of length L and zero elsewhere. Find A.

Solution: Normalisation gives ∫0L |A|²dx=|A|²L=1, so A=1/√L, choosing a real positive phase.

Worked numerical problem 5: Energy gap and frequency

Problem: An electronic transition has energy 4.00×10⁻¹⁹ J. Calculate frequency.

Solution: ν=ΔE/h=(4.00×10⁻¹⁹)/(6.626×10⁻³⁴)=6.04×10¹⁴ Hz. This corresponds to visible light.

Worked numerical problem 6: Radial nodes

Problem: Find radial and angular nodes for a 3p orbital.

Solution: For n=3 and l=1, radial nodes=n−l−1=1; angular nodes=l=1; total nodes=n−1=2.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Why is the wavefunction not itself a probability?

Answer: The wavefunction is a probability amplitude and may be complex or negative. Its modulus squared, |ψ|², gives the measurable probability density.

Model answer 2

Question: Explain zero-point energy in a particle-in-a-box model.

Answer: Boundary conditions allow only standing waves with n≥1. The lowest allowed state has E1>0, so confinement prevents zero kinetic energy.

Model answer 3

Question: State the physical meaning of an orbital node.

Answer: A node is a surface or region where the wavefunction and therefore probability density are zero. Nodes arise from the mathematical form of the eigenfunction.

Model answer 4

Question: Why does O2 exhibit paramagnetism in MO theory?

Answer: Its two highest-energy electrons occupy separate degenerate π* antibonding orbitals with parallel spins, leaving two unpaired electrons.

Model answer 5

Question: What does the variational principle guarantee?

Answer: For a valid normalised trial function, the calculated expectation energy cannot lie below the exact ground-state energy. Optimisation improves the upper-bound estimate.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: Why are atomic orbitals not classical planetary orbits?

Answer: An orbital is a stationary quantum state described by a wavefunction and probability distribution. It does not specify a definite classical trajectory or simultaneous exact position and momentum.

Model answer 7

Question: Calculate the number of nodes in a 4d orbital.

Answer: For n=4 and l=2, angular nodes=2, radial nodes=4−2−1=1 and total nodes=3.

Model answer 8

Question: Why is a Hermitian operator important?

Answer: Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues, properties required for representing measurable observables in quantum mechanics.

Model answer 9

Question: Explain the Born–Oppenheimer approximation and one limitation.

Answer: It separates electronic and nuclear motion by treating nuclei as fixed while solving the electronic problem. It can fail near conical intersections or closely spaced electronic states where nuclear and electronic motions are strongly coupled.

Model answer 10

Question: What information does a HOMO–LUMO interaction provide?

Answer: It helps rationalise donor–acceptor interactions and possible reaction pathways. Orbital energies, symmetry and overlap matter; a small gap alone does not prove a particular reaction will occur.
