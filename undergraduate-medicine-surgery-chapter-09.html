Core framework and conceptual foundations

Clinical skills combine respectful communication, systematic data collection, examination technique, interpretation and professional conduct. Begin by confirming identity, introducing yourself and your role, explaining the purpose of the encounter, obtaining consent and checking communication needs. Use open questions initially, then focused questions to clarify chronology, severity, associated symptoms, relevant risks and the patient's ideas, concerns and expectations. A symptom history should distinguish onset, site, character, radiation, associated features, timing, exacerbating and relieving factors, and severity, while avoiding leading questions.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

A clinical examination should be hypothesis-informed but not prematurely narrowed. Explain each step, obtain permission, offer a chaperone where appropriate, maintain dignity and stop if the patient withdraws consent. General assessment includes appearance, work of breathing, perfusion, hydration, mental status and vital signs. Interpret observations as a pattern and consider measurement error, baseline variation and clinical context. Documentation should be contemporaneous, legible, factual and distinguish patient-reported symptoms from observed signs and clinical interpretation. A problem representation is a concise summary using discriminating features; a differential diagnosis should be prioritised by likelihood, severity and time sensitivity. Diagnostic tests are interpreted using pre-test probability, sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and the consequences of false positives and false negatives. Handover should communicate the clinical problem, relevant background, assessment, actions and explicit contingency plan using a structured format such as SBAR.

Integrated clinical case

A patient presents with shortness of breath. Demonstrate how to establish immediate stability, obtain a focused respiratory history, assess vital signs and work of breathing, explain the examination, and communicate urgent concerns to a supervising clinician. State what information is missing before a differential can be safely prioritised.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.
