Unit overview

Experimental physics connects mathematical models to observable quantities. A reliable experiment does more than produce a number: it defines the measurand, establishes a traceable measurement procedure, quantifies uncertainty, tests assumptions and reports enough information for another investigator to reproduce the result.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Learning outcomes

  • Define the central physical quantities and use SI units consistently.
  • Derive and apply governing equations while stating assumptions and validity limits.
  • Interpret diagrams, graphs, mathematical models and measurable consequences.
  • Solve multi-step numerical and conceptual problems with clear working.
  • Evaluate uncertainty, approximations, boundary conditions and experimental limitations where relevant.
  • Communicate conclusions using correct notation, units and physical reasoning.

Core textbook content

Experimental physics connects mathematical models to observable quantities. A reliable experiment does more than produce a number: it defines the measurand, establishes a traceable measurement procedure, quantifies uncertainty, tests assumptions and reports enough information for another investigator to reproduce the result. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Measurement, measurands and SI units

A measurand is the quantity intended to be measured, stated with enough detail to distinguish it from related quantities. Before using an instrument, define the physical quantity, operating conditions, range and measurement model. SI base units provide a coherent foundation: metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela. Derived units follow algebraically, such as the newton, joule, pascal and volt. A result without a unit is incomplete, while a unit attached to an ill-defined measurand does not make the result meaningful. Distinguish resolution, sensitivity, range, repeatability, reproducibility and accuracy. Resolution is the smallest displayed or detectable increment; it is not automatically the uncertainty. Sensitivity is the change in indication per change in input. Repeatability concerns measurements under the same conditions, whereas reproducibility includes changed operators, instruments or laboratories.

Worked example / application. A digital balance reads 125.34 g with a display increment of 0.01 g. The resolution is 0.01 g, but uncertainty must also consider calibration, repeatability, drift and sample handling. Reporting 125.34 g alone would imply more certainty than the display can justify.

2. Instrument characteristics and selection

Choose an instrument by matching its range, resolution, accuracy class, response time, loading effect and environmental tolerance to the measurand. A voltmeter with finite input resistance draws current and may alter a high-resistance circuit. An ammeter has a small but non-zero series resistance and can change the current being measured. A thermometer has thermal capacity and requires time to approach equilibrium. Sensor calibration converts indication into a physical value through a documented relationship. Check zero offset, linearity, hysteresis, drift, dead band and repeatability. A calibration certificate states the reference, conditions, corrections and uncertainty; it does not guarantee that the instrument remains calibrated indefinitely.

Worked example / application. A 0–10 V voltmeter with input resistance 1 MΩ is connected across a 100 kΩ resistor in series with 10 V and 100 kΩ. The meter loads the measured branch. The parallel resistance is (100 kΩ × 1 MΩ)/(1.1 MΩ) = 90.9 kΩ, so the measured voltage is 10 × 90.9/(100 + 90.9) = 4.76 V rather than the unloaded 5.00 V.

3. Random, systematic and gross effects

Random effects cause scatter and are treated statistically through repeated observations. Systematic effects shift results in a consistent direction and require calibration, correction or a revised model. Gross errors include recording the wrong range, transposing digits or using an incorrect unit; they should be prevented by procedures and checked against raw records rather than silently removed. Repetition reduces the standard error of a mean when independent random effects dominate, but repetition does not remove a common zero offset. A good laboratory report separates observed scatter from known corrections and residual limitations.

Worked example / application. Five independent time measurements are 2.01, 1.99, 2.03, 2.00 and 1.97 s. Their mean is 2.00 s. A stopwatch that runs 0.08 s fast still biases every reading; averaging cannot correct that systematic offset.

4. Describing data: mean, spread and distributions

For observations x_i, the arithmetic mean is x̄ = Σx_i/n. The sample variance s² = Σ(x_i−x̄)²/(n−1) estimates population variance when observations are independent and identically distributed. The sample standard deviation s describes scatter of individual observations; the standard uncertainty of the mean is s/√n under suitable assumptions. A histogram reveals shape, outliers and possible multiple populations. Median and interquartile range can be useful for skewed distributions. Never report only the mean when spread or distribution shape is scientifically relevant. Preserve raw data, units, significant digits and the conditions under which observations were collected.

Worked example / application. For 4.8, 5.0, 5.1, 4.9 and 5.2 cm, x̄ = 5.00 cm. Deviations are −0.2, 0, 0.1, −0.1, 0.2 cm; sum of squares = 0.10 cm². Thus s = √(0.10/4) = 0.158 cm and standard uncertainty of the mean = 0.071 cm.

5. Uncertainty and significant reporting

Measurement uncertainty quantifies the dispersion of values reasonably attributable to the measurand. Type A evaluation uses statistical analysis of observations; Type B uses other information such as calibration certificates, manufacturer specifications, resolution or prior knowledge. Standard uncertainty is expressed like a standard deviation. Expanded uncertainty U = k u_c uses a coverage factor k, often approximately 2 when a roughly 95% coverage interpretation is justified. State the coverage factor and basis rather than treating k = 2 as universally exact. Round uncertainty to one or two significant figures and round the measured value to the same decimal place.

Worked example / application. A length is 24.63 cm with combined standard uncertainty 0.08 cm. With k = 2, U = 0.16 cm. Report 24.63 ± 0.16 cm (k = 2), subject to the stated coverage assumptions.

6. Propagation of uncertainty

If y = f(x_1,…,x_n), and input uncertainties are small, the first-order law of propagation uses sensitivity coefficients: u_c²(y) = Σ(c_i²u²(x_i)) + covariance terms, where c_i = ∂f/∂x_i. For independent inputs the covariance terms vanish. For y = x^a, relative standard uncertainty is approximately |a|u(x)/x. For products and quotients, independent relative uncertainties combine in quadrature. For sums and differences, absolute uncertainties combine in quadrature. These approximations rely on a locally linear model and suitable probability assumptions; nonlinear or bounded problems may need Monte Carlo propagation.

Worked example / application. A resistance R = V/I with V = 12.0 ± 0.1 V and I = 2.00 ± 0.03 A. R = 6.00 Ω. Relative uncertainty = √[(0.1/12)²+(0.03/2)²] = 0.0176, so u(R) ≈ 0.106 Ω. Report approximately 6.00 ± 0.11 Ω as standard uncertainty.

7. Calibration curves and regression

A calibration curve relates instrument indication to a reference value. For a linear model y = a + bx, least-squares fitting estimates slope and intercept by minimising the sum of squared vertical residuals, when the assumptions of the model are appropriate. Inspect residuals for curvature, changing variance, drift and outliers. A high coefficient of determination alone does not prove a valid model. If both axes have significant uncertainty, ordinary least squares may be inappropriate; consider orthogonal-distance methods or a physically justified model. Do not extrapolate beyond the calibrated range without evidence.

Worked example / application. A sensor gives output 0.42, 0.81 and 1.19 V at reference temperatures 10, 20 and 30 °C. The approximate slope is (1.19−0.42)/(30−10)=0.0385 V °C⁻¹. A full calibration should fit all points, estimate uncertainty and inspect residuals.

8. Graphing and linearisation

A graph should communicate the tested physical relationship. Put the independent variable on the horizontal axis and dependent variable on the vertical axis unless the model dictates otherwise. Label axes with quantity and unit, select readable scales, show uncertainty bars when meaningful, and distinguish data from fitted models. Linearisation can reveal parameters: for exponential decay y = y₀e^(−t/τ), plotting ln y against t gives slope −1/τ. Transformations also transform uncertainty and may alter error distributions, so use them thoughtfully. A straight line is not proof of causation or of a correct physical model.

Worked example / application. For ln A versus time, a fitted gradient of −0.25 s⁻¹ implies τ = −1/slope = 4.0 s. If the gradient uncertainty is 0.02 s⁻¹, first-order propagation gives u(τ) ≈ 0.32 s.

9. Experimental design and control variables

A defensible experiment begins with a question, a testable model and a defined dependent variable. Identify independent variables, control variables, confounders and a feasible measurement range. Use repeats to estimate variability and choose enough measurement points to reveal departures from a model. Randomise order where drift or environmental changes could bias the sequence. Include blanks, controls or reference standards when appropriate. Design for sensitivity: the expected signal should be distinguishable from noise and instrument resolution. A pilot experiment can expose practical limitations before the full data collection.

Worked example / application. To test pendulum period against length, vary length over a broad safe range, measure several oscillations per timing run, repeat each length, keep amplitude small and approximately constant, and record temperature and timing method. Measuring one swing only magnifies reaction-time error.

10. Data acquisition, sampling and aliasing

A digital acquisition system samples a continuous signal at discrete times. The sampling frequency must be high enough to represent the highest relevant signal frequency; the Nyquist criterion requires sampling above twice the highest frequency for ideal band-limited signals, while practical systems need anti-alias filtering and margin. Quantisation maps continuous amplitude to finite digital steps. Sensor bandwidth, ADC resolution, trigger settings, grounding and electromagnetic interference all affect data quality. Record sampling rate, bit depth, filter settings, sensor range and time base so that the measurement can be interpreted later.

Worked example / application. A vibration signal contains frequencies up to 800 Hz. Sampling at 1 kHz is inadequate because components above 500 Hz can alias. A rate above 1.6 kHz is the theoretical minimum for a perfectly band-limited signal; a higher practical rate and suitable anti-alias filter are preferable.

11. Laboratory safety, ethics and reproducibility

Risk assessment must precede work involving electrical supplies, lasers, hot surfaces, vacuum systems, moving apparatus, chemicals or radiation. Isolate power before rewiring, use appropriate eye protection, never look into a laser beam, secure rotating parts and allow hot equipment to cool. Follow local rules for waste and hazardous materials. Scientific integrity requires retaining original data, documenting exclusions, distinguishing measured from calculated values and reporting inconvenient results. Reproducibility depends on apparatus diagrams, instrument models, calibration status, procedure, environmental conditions, analysis choices and uncertainty statements.

Worked example / application. If one reading appears anomalous, retain it in the raw data, investigate the apparatus and procedure, and document any justified exclusion using a pre-stated criterion. Removing it solely because it weakens the preferred conclusion is not acceptable.

12. Integrated experiment: determine g with a pendulum

For a simple pendulum at small angular amplitude, T = 2π√(L/g), so T² = (4π²/g)L. Plot T² against L; the gradient m = 4π²/g and g = 4π²/m. Measure length from pivot to the bob's centre of mass, not to its top. Time multiple oscillations, repeat measurements, keep amplitude small, and use several lengths. Evaluate uncertainty in length and timing, inspect linearity, and discuss air resistance, pivot friction, finite amplitude and human reaction time. The experiment tests a model and estimates a physical constant; it does not merely demonstrate a formula.

Worked example / application. If the fitted gradient is 4.03 s² m⁻¹, g = 4π²/4.03 = 9.80 m s⁻². If u(m)=0.05 s² m⁻¹, then u(g)/g ≈ u(m)/m = 0.0124, giving u(g)≈0.12 m s⁻².

Summary equations and principles

Model → assumptions → governing equation → boundary / initial data → solution → dimensional and physical checks.

Use the equations introduced in each section as a connected framework rather than as isolated formulae. Define every symbol, retain units through calculations and identify whether quantities are vectors, scalars, fields, rates or integrated totals. Check special cases and conservation statements before accepting a numerical answer.

Independent study and further reading

Use a recognised university-level physics textbook and laboratory manual alongside these notes. Compare notation and sign conventions across sources, but do not combine formulae until their conventions and assumptions have been reconciled. For deeper study, locate peer-reviewed papers or university lecture material on the specific physical model, then reproduce at least one derivation or numerical result independently.

For revision, prepare a one-page concept map linking definitions, governing equations, experimental observables, limiting cases and common failure modes. Rework the examples with altered numerical values and explain why the qualitative outcome does or does not change.
