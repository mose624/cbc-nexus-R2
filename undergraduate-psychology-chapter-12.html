CBE NEXUS · UNDERGRADUATE PSYCHOLOGY · CHAPTER 12

Clinical Psychology and Evidence-Based Interventions

Undergraduate textbook notes · Clinical assessment · Formulation · Psychological therapies · Professional ethics

Learning outcomes: Explain the scope of clinical psychology; conduct a structured conceptual assessment; distinguish diagnosis from formulation; critically appraise evidence; compare psychological interventions; and apply ethical, culturally responsive and safety-conscious principles.

Introduction

Clinical psychology applies psychological science to the assessment and treatment of distress, mental and behavioural difficulties, and problems in adaptation. It requires more than memorising diagnostic criteria or therapy techniques. Effective practice combines careful listening, scientific reasoning, collaborative formulation, ethical judgement and attention to the social conditions in which people live. This chapter introduces the major models, assessment processes, interventions and professional responsibilities that guide contemporary clinical work. It is an academic resource, not a substitute for individual assessment or emergency care by qualified local professionals.

1. Definition, scope and aims of clinical psychology

Clinical psychology is the scientific and professional field concerned with understanding, assessing, preventing and reducing psychological distress, mental disorder and difficulties in adaptation. It integrates psychological science with clinical practice across the lifespan. Its work may include assessment, psychological formulation, psychotherapy, consultation, prevention, programme evaluation, research, teaching and supervision. Clinical psychologists work in hospitals, community clinics, schools, universities, rehabilitation services, forensic settings, private practice and humanitarian programmes. The scope is broader than diagnosis: it includes strengths, relationships, social conditions, functioning, quality of life and recovery. A person is not reducible to a diagnostic label, and a clinical formulation should explain how difficulties developed and what may maintain or relieve them.

Clinical work is guided by scientific reasoning and ethical responsibility. The practitioner gathers information systematically, considers alternative explanations, communicates uncertainty and chooses interventions proportionate to the person's needs and preferences. Outcomes may include symptom reduction, improved functioning, safer behaviour, stronger relationships, increased autonomy and meaningful participation in education, work or community life. Prevention and early intervention matter because distress can be intensified by poverty, discrimination, violence, isolation, illness and barriers to care. Clinical psychologists therefore consider individual processes alongside family, institutional and societal contexts.

2. Historical development and changing models of care

Clinical psychology developed through the interaction of experimental psychology, psychometrics, psychiatry, education and public health. Early psychological testing and laboratory research helped establish systematic assessment, while clinical services expanded in response to social need, war-related trauma and the recognition that psychological interventions could be evaluated. Behavioural approaches emphasised observable learning and measurable change; cognitive approaches examined interpretation, attention, memory and beliefs; humanistic and psychodynamic traditions highlighted subjective experience, relationships and meaning. Later developments integrated these perspectives and placed greater emphasis on controlled research, cultural context, patient rights and collaborative care.

The history also contains serious harms: coercive institutions, discriminatory diagnoses, unequal access, cultural misinterpretation and interventions delivered without adequate consent. Contemporary practice must examine this history rather than assume that professional authority guarantees benefit. Modern services increasingly emphasise community-based support, least-restrictive care, shared decisions, trauma-informed practice, disability rights and recovery. The movement towards evidence-based practice does not mean applying one manual mechanically. It means evaluating research critically while attending to the individual, the therapeutic relationship, clinical judgement and local realities.

3. Professional roles and related disciplines

Clinical psychologists are trained in psychological assessment, formulation, intervention, research and professional ethics. Psychiatrists are medical doctors who specialise in mental health and may diagnose medical conditions and prescribe medication within their scope. Counsellors and psychotherapists may provide structured psychological support or therapy, with training and regulation varying across jurisdictions. Social workers often address psychosocial needs, safeguarding, family systems, resources and social justice. Psychiatric nurses contribute assessment, care coordination, medication support and therapeutic engagement. These roles overlap, but they are not interchangeable; competence depends on training, registration, supervision and the complexity of the task.

Interprofessional care is especially important when a person has physical illness, substance use, trauma, risk, disability or complex social needs. A psychologist should explain their role, avoid making claims beyond competence and refer or consult when another discipline is needed. Collaboration requires clear consent about information sharing, defined responsibilities and respectful communication. In educational or workplace settings, clinical psychologists may consult with teachers, managers and families, but confidential clinical information should not be disclosed merely because another professional requests it. The person's rights and agreed care goals remain central.

4. Biopsychosocial and integrative formulation

The biopsychosocial model considers interacting biological, psychological and social influences. Biological factors may include inherited vulnerability, sleep disruption, pain, medication effects, neurological conditions and physiological stress. Psychological factors include learning history, appraisals, attention, emotion regulation, coping, memory and beliefs about the self or world. Social factors include relationships, housing, income, safety, culture, discrimination, work demands and access to care. These are not separate boxes: sleep loss can intensify threat sensitivity, which may lead to withdrawal, conflict and further sleep disruption. Formulation maps such reciprocal influences rather than searching for one simplistic cause.

A useful formulation distinguishes predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors. Predisposing factors increase vulnerability; precipitating factors help explain why difficulties emerged at a particular time; perpetuating factors maintain the current pattern; protective factors support coping and recovery. The framework is a working hypothesis, not a proven causal account. It should be revised when new information emerges and discussed with the client in understandable language. A formulation should identify practical intervention points, such as improving sleep, reducing avoidance, strengthening social support or addressing unsafe living conditions.

5. Developmental, cultural and systemic perspectives

Clinical assessment must consider the person's developmental stage, language, culture, family and social systems. A behaviour may have different meanings at different ages or in different cultural settings. Developmental history can clarify whether a difficulty is longstanding, acquired, episodic or associated with a transition. Family and systemic approaches examine patterns of communication, roles, boundaries, conflict, support and power. Cultural humility requires curiosity about the client's own explanations, values, spiritual practices and preferred forms of help. The clinician should not assume that a cultural identity predicts beliefs or behaviour.

Systemic formulation also considers institutions and structural conditions. A student experiencing concentration problems may be affected by trauma, overcrowded housing, hunger, inaccessible teaching or untreated illness rather than a single individual deficit. A worker's distress may relate to unsafe conditions or discrimination. Culturally responsive care uses qualified interpreters when needed, checks the meaning of translated concepts, avoids using relatives as interpreters for sensitive assessment and adapts examples without weakening the intervention's core principles. The client should be invited to correct misunderstandings.

6. Referral, engagement and initial clinical interview

A referral should clarify who is requesting help, the reason for referral, urgency, relevant background and the questions the assessment is expected to address. At first contact, the clinician explains their role, confidentiality and its limits, record keeping, fees or service arrangements, expected duration and how decisions will be made. Engagement is not a formality: fear, stigma, previous harmful experiences, language barriers and uncertainty can affect disclosure. Open questions such as “What has been most difficult recently?” allow the person to describe priorities in their own terms before the clinician narrows the inquiry.

The interview explores onset, course, frequency, intensity, context and impact of the presenting concern. It asks what makes the problem better or worse, what the person has tried, what matters most to them and what outcome they hope for. The clinician should distinguish the client's words from professional interpretation and avoid leading questions. Sensitive questions are introduced respectfully and with explanation. Where the person has limited capacity, communication needs or a support person, the clinician adapts the process while preserving autonomy and seeking consent in an accessible way.

7. Clinical history and mental status examination

A clinical history may cover current symptoms, previous episodes, physical health, medication, sleep, substance use, developmental history, education, work, relationships, family history, trauma exposure and previous treatment. The clinician asks only for information relevant to the assessment and avoids unnecessary repetition of traumatic events. The mental status examination is a structured description of current presentation: appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. It is a snapshot, not a complete diagnosis, and observations should be described objectively rather than using pejorative labels.

For example, “speech was rapid and difficult to interrupt” is more informative than “the client was difficult.” Mood is the person's reported emotional state; affect is the clinician's observation of emotional expression. Thought form concerns the organisation and flow of ideas, while thought content concerns themes such as guilt, suspiciousness or hopelessness. Perceptual experiences should be explored sensitively, including the person's interpretation and distress. Cognitive screening may be indicated, but results require context and should not be treated as a definitive measure of intelligence or capacity.

8. Risk assessment, safeguarding and safety planning

Risk assessment is a structured, ongoing clinical process, not a prediction that can identify with certainty who will act. It considers current thoughts, intent, plans, access to means, previous behaviour, recent changes, intoxication, agitation, hopelessness, protective relationships and the person's ability to use support. Questions about suicide and self-harm should be direct, calm and non-judgemental. The clinician also considers risk from others, abuse, neglect, exploitation, violence and inability to meet essential needs. Risk formulation should identify immediate concerns, foreseeable scenarios and actions that can reduce danger.

If there is immediate danger, the person should be connected to local emergency services, an emergency department or an appropriately qualified crisis service; the clinician follows local law and service protocols. A collaborative safety plan may identify warning signs, internal coping strategies, safe places, trusted contacts, professional services and steps to reduce access to lethal means. It is not a substitute for urgent care when danger is imminent. Safeguarding decisions should be proportionate, documented and explained as far as safely possible. Confidentiality may have limits where disclosure is legally or ethically necessary to protect someone.

9. Diagnostic classification and its limitations

DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 provide recognised systems for describing mental and behavioural disorders. Classification can support communication, research, service planning and access to treatment. Diagnosis should follow careful assessment of symptom pattern, duration, severity, impairment, developmental context, physical health, substance effects and alternative explanations. The clinician should explain what a diagnosis does and does not mean. It is a clinical description, not a complete account of identity, character, future or personal worth. Diagnostic criteria should be applied with cultural and developmental sensitivity.

Categorical systems place people into diagnostic groups, whereas dimensional approaches describe symptoms and traits along continua. Both approaches have strengths and limitations. Comorbidity is common because symptoms overlap and conditions can co-occur; it should not automatically be interpreted as multiple independent causes. Diagnostic uncertainty should be documented honestly. Labels may help some people understand experiences and access services, but may also contribute to stigma or overshadow physical and social explanations. Clinicians should review diagnoses when new evidence appears and avoid diagnosing solely from a screening score.

10. Evidence-based practice and critical appraisal

Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research evidence, the clinician's expertise and the client's values, preferences, culture and circumstances. No single source is sufficient. A randomised controlled trial may estimate the effect of an intervention under defined conditions, but its sample may exclude people with complex needs. Qualitative research can illuminate acceptability, meaning and barriers. Cohort studies can examine longer-term outcomes, while case studies may generate hypotheses but cannot establish general effectiveness. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses synthesise studies, yet their conclusions depend on the quality and comparability of the included evidence.

Critical appraisal asks whether the research question is clear, the design fits the question, participants are representative, measures are valid, missing data are handled appropriately and the analysis is transparent. It considers effect size, confidence intervals, clinical significance, harms, bias and applicability. Statistical significance alone does not show that a treatment produces meaningful change. Publication bias, small samples, selective reporting and conflicts of interest can distort the evidence base. Clinicians should communicate uncertainty and avoid presenting a promising intervention as guaranteed.

11. Therapeutic alliance, consent and shared decisions

The therapeutic alliance includes agreement on goals, agreement on tasks and a relationship characterised by trust and collaboration. Empathy involves trying to understand the client's perspective and communicating that understanding without claiming to know exactly how the person feels. The clinician explains options, likely benefits, possible burdens, alternatives and uncertainties. Informed consent is an ongoing process rather than a signature on a form. The client should be able to ask questions, decline an intervention, revisit a decision and understand what information will be recorded or shared.

Shared decision-making recognises that the clinician contributes technical knowledge while the client contributes lived experience, priorities and knowledge of their own circumstances. Goals should be meaningful, specific and reviewable. For one person, progress may mean returning to work; for another, it may mean sleeping more consistently, reducing panic or reconnecting with family. Ruptures in the therapeutic relationship should be acknowledged and repaired where possible. Power differences, cultural misunderstandings and inaccessible communication can weaken collaboration unless actively addressed.

12. Cognitive behavioural therapy and behavioural activation

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) examines relationships among situations, thoughts, emotions, bodily responses and behaviour. It does not assume that distress is “just thinking”; it investigates how interpretations and learned responses interact with real circumstances. A collaborative formulation identifies patterns that can be tested through observation and practice. Cognitive restructuring helps a person examine evidence, identify thinking habits and develop balanced alternatives. Behavioural experiments test predictions in everyday life. Homework or between-session practice supports generalisation, but tasks should be negotiated and adapted to the client's resources and abilities.

Behavioural activation is commonly used for depression. It maps how low mood, withdrawal and reduced access to rewarding or meaningful activity can reinforce one another. The clinician and client plan manageable activities linked to values, routine, mastery and connection, then review what happened rather than treating completion as a moral test. Activity scheduling may begin with small steps and include problem-solving around fatigue, cost, transport or caregiving. CBT and behavioural activation require competent training, appropriate supervision and adaptation for trauma, neurodiversity, disability and co-occurring conditions.

13. Exposure, third-wave and skills-based therapies

Exposure-based interventions are used for conditions in which avoidance maintains fear or distress, including some anxiety disorders, OCD and PTSD. Exposure is planned collaboratively, based on a formulation and paced according to the person's needs. It allows new learning about feared cues, uncertainty and coping; it is not punishment, coercion or reckless confrontation. In OCD, exposure and response prevention involves approaching triggers while reducing compulsive responses, under appropriately trained clinical guidance. The clinician monitors distress, consent and safety, and distinguishes therapeutic exposure from genuine danger.

Third-wave approaches include acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) and mindfulness-based interventions. ACT develops psychological flexibility through acceptance, defusion, present-moment awareness, values and committed action. DBT combines acceptance and change strategies and teaches skills in mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Mindfulness-based programmes train attention and a different relationship to thoughts and sensations. These approaches are not interchangeable; selection depends on the clinical question, evidence, client preference and therapist competence.

14. Psychodynamic, humanistic, interpersonal and systemic therapies

Psychodynamic therapies explore recurring relational patterns, emotional conflicts, defensive processes and the influence of earlier experiences on present functioning. Contemporary forms vary in length and structure, and should be delivered by appropriately trained practitioners. Humanistic approaches emphasise empathic understanding, authenticity, respect and the client's capacity for growth. Interpersonal psychotherapy focuses on current relationship contexts such as grief, role transitions, disputes and interpersonal deficits, often using a time-limited structure. Family and systemic therapies examine interaction patterns and shared meanings rather than locating all difficulty within one individual.

Therapy selection should not be based on fashion or the clinician's preferred school alone. The clinician explains the rationale, expected process, evidence and limitations of the proposed approach. Some clients benefit from an integrative plan, but integration should be coherent rather than an unstructured mixture of techniques. Family involvement requires careful consent and attention to safety, power and confidentiality. In situations involving coercive control or abuse, joint sessions may be unsafe or inappropriate. The clinician should assess whether the proposed format protects the person and supports their goals.

15. Trauma-focused care and motivational interviewing

Trauma-focused psychological interventions address the effects of traumatic experiences while prioritising safety, choice and pacing. Depending on the presentation and evidence, approaches may include trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure or eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing delivered by trained clinicians. Trauma-informed practice recognises that distress may be linked to experiences of threat and loss of control. It avoids unnecessary repetition of traumatic details, explains procedures, offers choices and does not assume that every symptom is caused by trauma.

Motivational interviewing is a collaborative communication method for exploring ambivalence and strengthening a person's own reasons for change. It uses open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries. The practitioner avoids argument and does not impose goals. For substance use or health behaviour, the conversation may explore perceived benefits, costs, confidence and readiness. Motivational interviewing is not a technique for manipulating someone into compliance; it respects autonomy and can be combined with other evidence-informed support when appropriate.

16. Depression and anxiety-related conditions

Psychological care for depression may include CBT, behavioural activation, interpersonal psychotherapy and other evidence-supported approaches. Assessment considers severity, duration, functioning, previous episodes, physical health, substance use, grief, trauma, social adversity and suicide risk. Anxiety conditions require differentiation among generalised anxiety, panic, social anxiety, specific phobia and other presentations. Treatment may include psychoeducation, CBT, exposure, skills for tolerating uncertainty and interventions addressing avoidance. The clinician should avoid assuming that all worry is irrational; some concerns reflect genuine danger or hardship requiring practical action.

Treatment plans should be collaborative and monitored. For severe symptoms, psychotic features, significant risk, complex comorbidity or poor response, coordinated specialist assessment may be necessary. Medication decisions belong to qualified prescribers, and psychologists should coordinate care with consent. Improvement is not always linear. Setbacks can be reviewed as information about triggers, skills and support rather than evidence of failure. Relapse prevention identifies early warning signs, helpful responses and routes back to care.

17. OCD, PTSD, bipolar disorder and psychosis

Obsessive-compulsive disorder involves obsessions, compulsions or both, with distress or impairment. CBT incorporating exposure and response prevention is an established psychological approach. PTSD assessment considers exposure to traumatic events, intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, changes in mood and cognition, arousal, duration and functional impact. Trauma-focused interventions may be indicated after careful assessment. Bipolar disorders involve episodes of mood disturbance; psychological care may support psychoeducation, routine stability, sleep regularity, relapse-signature recognition, family communication and adherence to a jointly agreed care plan.

Psychosis may involve hallucinations, delusions, disorganisation and changes in functioning, but experiences vary widely and require careful assessment of medical, substance-related, cultural and psychological factors. CBT for psychosis can help some people examine distressing experiences and develop coping strategies; family interventions may improve communication and support. Psychological treatment is generally part of coordinated care, not a substitute for medical assessment where needed. Clinicians should not argue aggressively about beliefs or reinforce harmful certainty; they should explore meaning, distress, safety and practical coping respectfully.

18. Substance use, eating disorders, personality and neurodevelopment

Substance-use assessment explores patterns, context, consequences, dependence, withdrawal risk, physical health, co-occurring conditions and the person's goals. Motivational interviewing, CBT, relapse-prevention approaches and coordinated medical or social support may be useful. Eating disorders require careful assessment of physical risk and specialist multidisciplinary care; psychological interventions should be delivered within appropriate medical monitoring. Personality-related difficulties are best understood through enduring patterns of emotion, identity, relationships and coping, not moral judgement. Structured therapies may support emotion regulation, interpersonal functioning and safety.

Neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism and ADHD require developmental history and attention to strengths, sensory needs, communication, executive functioning and environmental fit. Support should not aim to erase harmless differences or force masking; it should reduce distress and barriers while respecting identity and autonomy. Adaptations may include predictable structure, written information, sensory adjustments, flexible pacing and clear communication. Co-occurring anxiety, depression, sleep problems or learning difficulties should be assessed without assuming that every difficulty is part of the neurodevelopmental diagnosis.

19. Sleep, chronic pain and physical-health interfaces

Sleep difficulties can both contribute to and result from psychological distress. Assessment examines routines, timing, environmental conditions, medication, substance use, physical illness, circadian factors and beliefs about sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is a structured intervention delivered by trained practitioners; sleep hygiene alone is often insufficient for persistent insomnia. Chronic pain requires a biopsychosocial approach that validates pain while examining activity patterns, fear, mood, sleep, social support and functional goals. Psychological care does not imply that pain is imaginary or that the person is responsible for being unwell.

Clinical psychologists may work alongside physicians, nurses, physiotherapists and other specialists to support adjustment, pacing, coping, communication and quality of life. Goals may focus on valued activity and function rather than promising complete symptom elimination. The clinician should be alert to medical red flags and refer for medical assessment when symptoms suggest a physical cause requiring investigation. Psychological treatment complements, rather than replaces, appropriate medical care.

20. Crisis intervention, safeguarding and referral pathways

Crisis intervention begins by establishing immediate safety, understanding the precipitating event and identifying urgent practical needs. The clinician uses calm, direct communication, reduces unnecessary stimulation and involves trusted supports when appropriate and consented to. The person may need emergency medical care, specialist psychiatric review, protection from violence or urgent social support. A crisis plan records warning signs, preferred supports, contact details, practical steps and what to do if the situation escalates. Plans should be accessible, realistic and reviewed with the person rather than stored unread in a file.

Safeguarding requires knowledge of applicable local laws and organisational procedures. Concerns may involve children, older people, adults at risk, domestic abuse, exploitation or neglect. The clinician documents facts, distinguishes observation from inference and consults the designated safeguarding lead or relevant authority according to policy. Information sharing should be necessary, proportionate and secure. Clinicians must not promise absolute secrecy. They should explain limits before sensitive disclosure wherever possible and involve the person in decisions unless doing so would increase danger or conflict with legal duties.

21. Cultural humility, disability, equity and social determinants

Culturally responsive practice recognises that distress, help-seeking and healing are shaped by language, history, faith, family, migration, community and social power. Cultural humility means ongoing self-reflection, openness to correction and willingness to examine professional assumptions. It is not achieved by memorising stereotypes about groups. The clinician asks what the person's experience means to them, whether community or spiritual supports are important and what forms of care feel acceptable. Qualified interpreters should be used when needed, with attention to confidentiality and dialect.

Disability-inclusive care provides accessible environments, communication formats and reasonable adjustments. Clinicians should distinguish impairment from disabling barriers and avoid assuming incapacity. Social determinants such as income insecurity, housing, education, violence, discrimination and unemployment may be central treatment targets or referral needs. A purely individual therapy plan may be inadequate when distress is maintained by unsafe or unjust conditions. Advocacy, coordinated referral and practical problem-solving can be legitimate parts of psychological care within professional boundaries.

22. Telepsychology and digital interventions

Telepsychology can improve access for people who face distance, mobility, time or service barriers. Before remote work begins, the clinician assesses privacy, technology access, suitability, emergency location, identity, communication needs and a plan for disconnection or crisis. Informed consent covers platform limitations, data security, recording policies and the risks of being overheard. Some presentations or circumstances may require in-person assessment or a hybrid approach. Digital access should not be assumed: devices, data costs, electricity, literacy and private space are unevenly distributed.

Digital interventions range from guided self-help to clinician-supported programmes and automated tools. Their quality, evidence, privacy practices and accessibility vary. Clinicians should check whether a tool has credible evidence for the intended population and whether data are stored or shared appropriately. An app is not automatically a medical device or a substitute for clinical care. AI-generated advice can be inaccurate, biased or unsafe. Professional responsibility remains with the clinician, who should explain limitations and provide human review and escalation pathways.

23. Outcome monitoring, stepped care and relapse prevention

Measurement-based care uses repeated, appropriate measures alongside clinical discussion to monitor symptoms, functioning, wellbeing and progress towards personal goals. Measures can reveal improvement, deterioration or lack of response, but scores must be interpreted in context. A questionnaire is not a diagnosis by itself. The clinician asks whether the measure captures what matters to the person and whether language, disability or cultural factors affect its meaning. Treatment fidelity refers to delivering the intervention's essential components while allowing justified adaptation.

Stepped care matches intensity to need and response. A person may begin with lower-intensity support and move to more specialised treatment if risk, severity or non-response warrants it. Stepped care must not become a barrier that delays necessary specialist help. Relapse prevention identifies individual warning signs, high-risk situations, protective routines, coping strategies and support contacts. Discharge should be planned collaboratively, with clear routes for re-entry if difficulties return. Recovery is often a continuing process rather than a simple end point.

24. Research, implementation and common factors

Efficacy asks whether an intervention can work under controlled conditions; effectiveness asks how it performs in routine practice. Randomised trials can strengthen causal inference, while systematic reviews and meta-analyses estimate patterns across studies. However, results depend on participant selection, comparison conditions, outcome measures, follow-up and risk of bias. Implementation science examines how evidence-based interventions are adopted, delivered, sustained and adapted in real services. It considers workforce capacity, leadership, resources, local culture, accessibility and whether an intervention reaches the people who need it.

Common factors such as empathy, collaboration, hope, credible rationale and a strong therapeutic alliance contribute to many forms of therapy, but they do not mean that all treatments are equivalent. Specific techniques may be important for particular problems. Researchers and clinicians should distinguish correlation from causation, statistical from clinical significance and average effects from individual response. Evidence gaps should be stated openly. Services should evaluate outcomes and unintended harms, including unequal access and differential dropout.

25. Ethics, boundaries, supervision and professional development

Ethical practice includes respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, fidelity and professional integrity. Clinicians work within competence, seek supervision, maintain accurate records and protect confidential information. Boundaries clarify the purpose and limits of the professional relationship. Dual relationships, conflicts of interest, gifts, social media contact and financial arrangements require careful consideration under local standards. Confidentiality is fundamental but not absolute; exceptions may arise through consent, legal duties or serious safety concerns. Clinicians should explain these limits clearly and document decisions.

Supervision supports reflection, safe decision-making and continuing competence; it does not transfer the clinician's responsibility to the supervisor. Practitioners should recognise fatigue, impairment and personal reactions that may affect care. Continuing professional development includes reading current research, reviewing outcomes, learning from complaints and adapting to changing standards. Records should be factual, relevant, secure and respectful because clients may have rights to access them. Professional accountability includes acknowledging errors, responding to concerns and taking corrective action.

26. Applied formulation and integrated clinical case

Consider an adult referred for persistent low mood, worry, poor sleep and withdrawal after losing employment. A narrow symptom list may suggest depression and anxiety, but formulation asks about the timing of the job loss, financial pressure, family responsibilities, physical health, previous episodes, social support and the person's interpretation of the event. Predisposing factors might include earlier episodes or limited support; precipitating factors include job loss; perpetuating factors may include disrupted sleep, avoidance and reduced daily structure; protective factors may include a supportive sibling, practical skills and willingness to seek help.

The plan is negotiated rather than imposed. It may include risk assessment, medical review where indicated, practical employment or financial referral, behavioural activation, sleep-focused support and CBT strategies for worry. Progress is monitored using both the person's goals and suitable measures. If symptoms worsen or risk increases, the plan is reviewed and care escalated. The formulation remains provisional: if new information suggests trauma, substance effects, bipolar-spectrum symptoms or a physical condition, the clinician reassesses. The case illustrates how diagnosis, formulation and treatment planning answer related but different questions.

Structured university examination questions and model answers

Use these questions to test conceptual understanding, application, comparison and critical evaluation. In longer answers, define terms, explain mechanisms, apply a relevant example and evaluate limitations.

1. Differentiate clinical psychology from psychiatry.

Model answer: Clinical psychology is grounded in psychological science and commonly focuses on assessment, formulation, psychological interventions and research. Psychiatry is a medical specialty that includes medical diagnosis and prescribing. Their roles overlap and collaborative care is often appropriate.

2. Explain the biopsychosocial model using an example.

Model answer: It examines interacting biological, psychological and social influences. For insomnia, pain or illness may disrupt sleep biologically, worry may increase arousal psychologically, and shift work or overcrowding may maintain the problem socially.

3. Define predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors.

Model answer: Predisposing factors increase vulnerability; precipitating factors contribute to onset; perpetuating factors maintain difficulties; protective factors reduce impact or support recovery. They organise hypotheses rather than prove causation.

4. What is a mental status examination?

Model answer: A structured description of current appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, affect, thought, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. It complements history and should use objective, respectful descriptions.

5. Why is suicide risk assessment not a precise prediction?

Model answer: Risk changes over time and available indicators cannot determine with certainty who will act. Assessment identifies current concerns, foreseeable scenarios, protective factors and practical safety actions; it must be repeated as circumstances change.

6. State the role and limitations of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11.

Model answer: They provide shared classification systems for communication, research and service planning. They do not fully explain an individual's experience, establish a single cause or replace formulation, cultural judgement and assessment.

7. Explain categorical and dimensional diagnosis.

Model answer: Categorical approaches assign a person to a diagnostic group when criteria are met. Dimensional approaches describe severity or traits along continua. Each captures some aspects of experience and misses others.

8. Define evidence-based practice in clinical psychology.

Model answer: It integrates best available research, clinical expertise and the client's values, preferences, culture and circumstances. It is a process of informed judgement, not mechanical use of a manual.

9. Why can statistical significance be misleading?

Model answer: A small effect can be statistically significant in a large sample yet have little practical value. Interpretation requires effect size, uncertainty, harms, clinical importance and relevance to the client.

10. Describe the three components of a therapeutic alliance.

Model answer: Agreement on goals, agreement on therapeutic tasks and a collaborative bond characterised by trust, empathy and respect.

11. Explain behavioural activation.

Model answer: It identifies withdrawal and reduced meaningful activity that may maintain depression, then collaboratively schedules manageable valued activities and reviews their effects and barriers.

12. What is exposure and response prevention?

Model answer: A CBT approach for OCD in which a person approaches relevant triggers while reducing compulsive responses, allowing new learning. It requires consent, formulation and trained clinical delivery.

13. Outline the core processes of ACT.

Model answer: Acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, values clarification and committed action support psychological flexibility.

14. How does DBT combine acceptance and change?

Model answer: It validates the person's current experience while teaching skills and strategies to change behaviours that cause harm or interfere with a life worth living.

15. What is motivational interviewing?

Model answer: A collaborative, autonomy-supportive method using reflective listening and evocation to explore ambivalence and strengthen the person's own reasons for change.

16. Why is psychological care for bipolar disorder usually collaborative?

Model answer: Mood episodes may require medical assessment and treatment. Psychological care can support psychoeducation, routines, sleep, early warning signs, family communication and relapse planning alongside appropriate medical care.

17. Give two principles of culturally responsive assessment.

Model answer: Ask the person what their experiences mean within their context, and use accessible language or qualified interpreters when needed. Avoid stereotypes and check assumptions collaboratively.

18. What should a telepsychology consent discussion include?

Model answer: Privacy, platform and data limitations, emergency location, disconnection plans, recording policy, communication arrangements, suitability and alternatives to remote care.

19. Distinguish efficacy from effectiveness.

Model answer: Efficacy concerns whether an intervention works under controlled conditions; effectiveness concerns outcomes in routine practice with real-world complexity and constraints.

20. What is measurement-based care?

Model answer: Repeated use of suitable outcome measures alongside clinical discussion to track symptoms, functioning and personally meaningful goals and to inform treatment adjustments.

21. Explain stepped care and one possible limitation.

Model answer: Stepped care matches intervention intensity to need and response. Poorly designed systems may delay specialist treatment or impose low-intensity steps despite high severity or risk.

22. What is the purpose of clinical supervision?

Model answer: It supports safe practice, reflective learning, ethical decision-making and competence. It does not remove the practitioner's accountability for their own decisions.

23. When may confidentiality need to be limited?

Model answer: With valid consent for sharing, or where applicable law or serious safety and safeguarding duties require proportionate disclosure. The clinician should explain limits and document the rationale.

24. How should a clinician respond to diagnostic uncertainty?

Model answer: State what is known and unknown, consider alternative explanations, gather relevant information, avoid premature labels and review the working diagnosis as evidence develops.

25. Why should a formulation be discussed with the client?

Model answer: It checks whether the account fits the person's experience, supports shared understanding and identifies realistic intervention points. It should remain open to correction.

26. Identify two ethical concerns in digital mental-health tools.

Model answer: Privacy and security of sensitive data, and inaccurate or biased automated advice. Clinicians should evaluate evidence, suitability, accessibility and escalation pathways.

27. What is relapse prevention?

Model answer: A collaborative plan identifying early warning signs, triggers, helpful coping responses, support contacts and steps for timely re-entry to care.

28. How can social determinants influence clinical outcomes?

Model answer: Poverty, unsafe housing, discrimination, violence and limited service access can cause or maintain distress and restrict treatment participation; care may need practical referral and advocacy as well as therapy.

Chapter synthesis and revision framework

Clinical psychology is best understood as an evidence-informed, relationship-based and ethically accountable discipline. Assessment describes the person's experience and context; formulation proposes how relevant factors interact; diagnosis communicates a recognised pattern where appropriate; and treatment planning translates shared goals into proportionate action. None of these stages should be treated as infallible. Each is open to revision as the person's circumstances and the available evidence change.

When evaluating an intervention, ask what problem it targets, what mechanism it proposes, what evidence supports it, for whom it has been studied, what harms or burdens may arise, and whether it fits the client's values and context. Monitor outcomes, seek supervision, protect confidentiality and recognise the limits of professional competence. The strongest clinical plans are scientifically informed without being rigid, compassionate without being uncritical, and individualised without ignoring structural barriers.

Revision sequence: scope and professional roles → formulation → assessment and risk → diagnosis and evidence → therapeutic relationship → intervention models → condition-specific care → culture and access → outcome monitoring → ethics and professional development.
