Use these worked examples, planning tables, instrument guides, analysis exercises and quality checklists to turn research knowledge into a feasible, ethical and defensible academic project.

1. Research proposal development template

A research proposal should persuade an academic reader that a meaningful question exists, that the proposed investigation is appropriate, that the researcher understands the relevant scholarship and that the project can be completed ethically with available resources. The proposal is a plan, not a report of results that have not yet been collected.

1.1 Title and abstract

Use a concise title that identifies the central phenomenon and, where appropriate, the population, location or period. Avoid a title that is so broad that it could describe hundreds of unrelated projects. Avoid causal language if the design is descriptive or exploratory. A proposal abstract commonly summarises the context, problem, aim, design, data sources, analysis and expected contribution. Do not present anticipated findings as if they have already been observed.

1.3 Aim, objectives and questions alignment

Write one overall aim. Then write three to five objectives that represent achievable stages of the investigation. Each objective should be linked to a research question and a form of evidence. For a project examining access to laboratory learning, an objective might be to describe the availability of specified equipment. The related question asks which equipment is available and functional. The evidence may come from a structured inventory completed using a defined checklist. A separate objective about learner experience would require a different source and instrument.

Alignment test: For every objective, ask: What evidence would answer it? Where will that evidence come from? How will it be collected? How will it be analysed? If any answer is missing, the proposal is not yet complete.

1.4 Justification, scope and definitions

The justification explains who may benefit and what kind of contribution is realistic. Distinguish academic contribution from practical benefit. A study may help a department understand a local problem without claiming that it will solve a national challenge. Scope defines population, setting, period and concepts. Delimitations are boundaries chosen by the researcher. Operational definitions explain how key concepts are used in the study.

2. Worked problem statement and research alignment example

The following is a teaching example. It is not a report of real institutional findings and should not be copied as evidence. Replace the bracketed details with verified information from the researcher's own context.

2.1 Example problem statement

Practical learning is an important component of science education because it can connect theoretical concepts with observation, measurement and experimental reasoning. In [defined institutions or region], science departments are expected to provide practical learning opportunities aligned with the programme requirements. However, [verified institutional audit, official report or preliminary evidence] indicates that access to laboratory facilities and the frequency of practical sessions vary across the selected setting. This may affect opportunities for learners to develop experimental skills, although the relationship cannot be assumed without evidence.

Previous studies have examined laboratory access, practical teaching and science achievement in different contexts. Their findings may not fully explain the situation in [specific context], where equipment availability, class size, teacher workload and timetable arrangements may differ. The available evidence identified for this proposal provides limited information about how these factors operate together in the selected population. The proposed study will therefore examine laboratory access, practical-session frequency and learner performance in [defined institutions] during [period].

2.2 What makes the example defensible?

  • It identifies the broad educational context without claiming that every school has the same problem.
  • It requires evidence from a real audit or credible source rather than invented percentages.
  • It distinguishes a possible influence from a proven causal effect.
  • It names the context and signals the need to review previous research.
  • It identifies a study response that can be investigated with defined evidence.

3. Research instruments: questionnaire, interview and observation examples

3.1 Questionnaire design checklist

Map each question to an objective. Use language appropriate to the participant group. Ask one thing at a time. Avoid leading statements, double-barrelled questions, unexplained abbreviations and response options that overlap. Provide a “not applicable” or “prefer not to say” option where appropriate. Avoid collecting identifiers unless they are necessary and approved.

Example neutral items for a study of learning resources:

  1. During the previous four weeks, how many supervised practical sessions did you attend? Provide defined response categories or a numeric response.
  2. How often was the equipment required for your practical lesson available and functional? Use a clearly labelled frequency scale.
  3. How confident are you in carrying out [named procedure]? Use a balanced confidence scale.
  4. What barriers, if any, made it difficult to participate in practical sessions? Allow more than one response and an optional explanation.
  5. What change would most improve your access to practical learning? Use an open response.

Do not assume that self-reported session frequency equals the official timetable. Where possible and permitted, compare self-report with attendance or departmental records. Explain differences rather than silently choosing the preferred source.

3.2 Semi-structured interview guide

Opening: explain the study, confirm consent and ask whether the participant has questions. Core questions may include: “Can you describe a recent experience of using the laboratory?” “What usually happens when equipment is unavailable?” “How do you prepare before a practical session?” “What makes a procedure easier or more difficult to carry out?” “What changes would improve the experience?” Neutral probes include “Can you give an example?”, “What happened next?” and “How did you interpret that?”

Avoid questions that presume an answer, such as “Why does the school fail to provide enough equipment?” A neutral alternative is “How would you describe the availability of equipment in your experience?” Interviewers should allow participants to disagree, decline a question or end the interview according to the approved protocol.

3.3 Observation schedule

An observation schedule should define the event, setting, time period and behaviours to be recorded. For a practical lesson, categories might include lesson start and end times, equipment availability, number of learners per workstation, teacher demonstration, learner participation, safety briefing and interruptions. Define each category so that different observers interpret it consistently. Observation should not be used to secretly assess individual learners or staff. Obtain required permissions and follow the ethics protocol.

3.4 Pilot testing

A pilot checks whether questions are understood, response options work, the instrument takes an acceptable time and procedures are feasible. Ask pilot participants which terms were unclear and whether any important response was missing. Record revisions and explain them. If pilot participants are excluded from the main study, state why. If pilot data are retained, the protocol and analysis should justify that decision.

4. Data analysis practice and worked interpretation

4.1 Descriptive analysis exercise

Hypothetical data: 80 learners answer whether they attended at least one supervised practical session during a defined month. Fifty-six answer yes and 24 answer no. The proportion answering yes is 56 divided by 80, equal to 0.70 or 70%. The proportion answering no is 24 divided by 80, equal to 0.30 or 30%. These figures describe only the hypothetical sample. Population estimates require a defensible sampling design and consideration of nonresponse and uncertainty.

When reporting a percentage, provide the numerator and denominator where useful. State the timeframe and exact wording of the question. A table should identify the sample size and missing responses. Do not imply that a percentage from a convenience sample is a national estimate.

4.2 Comparing two groups

Suppose a hypothetical study compares average practical assessment scores between two groups. Group A has 30 learners and a mean score of 64; Group B has 30 learners and a mean score of 70. The observed mean difference is 6 points. Before interpreting it, ask how the groups were formed, whether baseline achievement differed, whether assessment conditions were comparable, whether the score measure is reliable and whether observations are independent. A difference does not prove that the teaching method caused the outcome. A suitable estimate and uncertainty interval should be reported using a method consistent with the design and assumptions.

4.3 Qualitative coding exercise

Hypothetical interview extract: “I understand the teacher's written comments, but I do not always know which part to change first. Sometimes I ask a classmate to explain the marking guide.” Possible initial codes include uncertainty about prioritisation, peer clarification and interpretation of assessment criteria. A possible candidate theme is “translating feedback into an action plan”. The researcher must examine the full dataset, compare other accounts and search for contrary examples before deciding whether the theme is supported.

4.4 Interpreting a null or unexpected finding

A non-significant result does not prove that no relationship exists. It may reflect a small sample, imprecise measurement, limited variation, a genuinely small association or model assumptions. Report the estimate and uncertainty rather than saying “there is no effect” solely because a threshold was not crossed. An unexpected qualitative finding should be examined with the same care as an expected one. Do not remove it because it complicates the argument.

5. Research ethics and data management checklist

Before collecting evidence, confirm the following with the relevant institution or ethics committee:

  • The study has the required ethical approval and site permissions.
  • Participant information is clear, accurate and available in an appropriate language.
  • Consent is voluntary and free from coercion or undue influence.
  • Risks, benefits, withdrawal arrangements and confidentiality limits are explained.
  • Recruitment protects people in dependent relationships, including students and employees.
  • Procedures for minors or participants with reduced capacity follow applicable rules.
  • Only necessary personal information is collected.
  • Data are coded, access-controlled and stored using approved systems.
  • Identity keys are stored separately from research responses.
  • Retention, deletion, sharing and cross-border storage follow policy and law.
  • Audio, photographs, quotations and sensitive records are covered by consent and approval.
  • Unexpected incidents and protocol deviations have a reporting route.

For research conducted in Kenya, review the Data Protection Act and guidance from the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner in addition to institutional requirements. For research in another country, identify the applicable national data protection and research governance framework. This checklist is educational and does not replace legal advice or formal ethics review.

6.1 Weekly supervision record

After each meeting, record the date, topics discussed, decisions, action items, responsible person and due date. Distinguish agreed decisions from suggestions that still require confirmation. Send a concise follow-up to the supervisor where appropriate. Keep versions of drafts and a response log showing how feedback was addressed or why a suggestion was not adopted.

6.2 Final submission checklist

  • The title, abstract and final chapters describe the same study.
  • Every objective is answered in the findings or analysis.
  • The discussion interprets evidence rather than repeating tables.
  • Every major claim is supported by evidence or an appropriate citation.
  • All citations match the reference list and all listed sources are cited.
  • Tables, figures, units, captions and appendices are labelled consistently.
  • Ethics approval and declarations follow institutional requirements.
  • Limitations and recommendations are specific and proportionate.
  • AI assistance complies with the institution's current policy.
  • The final file meets the required format and submission deadline.

7. Oral defence and examiner questions

Prepare to explain the research as a coherent chain rather than memorising isolated paragraphs. A clear defence usually begins with the problem and contribution, then explains the literature and framework, the methodological decisions, the principal findings, the limitations and the implications.

  1. Why did you choose this topic, and who needs the answer?
  2. What exactly is the research problem?
  3. How did you establish that the gap exists?
  4. Which theory or concepts guide the study?
  5. Why is your design appropriate for the question?
  6. What alternative designs did you consider?
  7. How did you select participants, cases or sources?
  8. How did you determine sample adequacy?
  9. How did you establish the quality of your instruments?
  10. What ethical risks arose and how were they managed?
  11. Which assumptions underpin your analysis?
  12. What was the most unexpected finding?
  13. How do your findings compare with earlier scholarship?
  14. What are the most important limitations?
  15. What is original or useful about the contribution?
  16. What would you change if you repeated the project?

7.1 A structured way to answer

Use a four-part response: answer the question directly; explain the methodological or theoretical reason; refer to evidence from your study; acknowledge the limitation or alternative interpretation. If you do not know, say so and explain what evidence or guidance would be needed. Do not invent a result or cite a source you have not checked.
