Leadership and management in nursing

Leadership influences direction, motivation, culture and improvement; management coordinates resources, people, processes and operational responsibilities. Nurses may lead without holding a formal management title by advocating for patients, communicating risks, supporting colleagues and modelling professional standards.

Effective leadership is situational and responsive to team capability, patient acuity, resources and organisational context. Accountability remains with each practitioner for their own actions. Leaders should promote psychological safety, fairness, learning and respectful challenge when care may be unsafe.

Leadership theories and styles

Commonly discussed approaches include transformational, transactional, situational, servant, authentic and distributed leadership. Transformational leadership emphasises shared purpose and development; transactional approaches use clear expectations and performance feedback; situational approaches adapt support and direction to circumstances.

No single style is ideal in every setting. Emergencies may require clear direction, while service redesign benefits from participation and shared problem-solving. Reflect on how power, culture, workload and communication affect team behaviour and patient outcomes.

Delegation, supervision and accountability

Delegation assigns a task to another competent person while the delegating practitioner retains responsibilities defined by law, policy and professional standards. Safe delegation considers the task, circumstances, person's condition, worker competence, instructions, supervision and evaluation.

Do not delegate activities outside the recipient's competence or legal scope. The person receiving a task must clarify expectations and raise concerns if unable to perform it safely. Rules differ by jurisdiction and employer; use the current regulator standards and local delegation policy.

Workforce planning and resource management

Nursing workforce management considers patient acuity, staffing numbers and skill mix, workload, competencies, leave, supervision, supplies and service demand. Staffing decisions influence safety, continuity, staff wellbeing and quality. Budgets should support appropriate care rather than encourage unsafe shortcuts.

Leaders monitor workload, skill gaps, fatigue, absence, turnover, incidents and patient outcomes. Resource allocation should be transparent and equitable. Escalate unsafe staffing through formal channels, document concerns and use contingency plans approved by the organisation.

Professional ethics, values and codes

Nursing ethics commonly draws on autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, fidelity, veracity, dignity and confidentiality. Ethical practice requires balancing duties when values or interests conflict. Professional codes set standards for conduct, competence, accountability and relationships.

Ethical reasoning identifies the facts, stakeholders, relevant duties, options, likely consequences and applicable law or policy. Seek supervision, ethics consultation or multidisciplinary discussion when needed. Respect the patient's informed choices while recognising legal limits and duties to protect others.

Consent, capacity, confidentiality and advocacy

Valid consent requires appropriate information, understanding, voluntariness and decision-making capacity as defined by applicable law. Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate. Nurses should support communication and reasonable adjustments rather than assume incapacity because of disability, age, diagnosis or disagreement.

Confidential information should be shared only with appropriate authority and a legitimate purpose, subject to legal and safety exceptions. Explain confidentiality limits. Advocacy supports the person's voice, access, rights and informed decisions, especially when power imbalances or communication barriers exist.

Professional regulation, scope and accountability

Professional regulation protects the public through registration, standards, education requirements, conduct processes and practice oversight. Scope of practice is shaped by education, demonstrated competence, law, professional standards, employer policy and the circumstances of the individual patient.

Practitioners must maintain competence, recognise limits, seek help, keep accurate records and report unsafe practice through appropriate channels. Students practise under required supervision. Requirements differ between countries; verify current guidance from the relevant nursing and midwifery regulator.
