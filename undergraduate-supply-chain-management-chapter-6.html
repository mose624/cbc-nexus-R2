CBE Nexus · Undergraduate Supply Chain Management · Chapter 6

Warehousing, Distribution and Logistics Management

Warehouse strategy, facility design, material handling, transport planning, distribution networks, order fulfilment and logistics performance.

Course guide

Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, learners should be able to explain the main concepts and frameworks, analyse operational decisions, assess cost and service trade-offs, identify risks and controls, interpret performance information and recommend evidence-based improvements in an integrated supply chain environment.

Study approach: Use the chapter to connect theory with practice. Define terms accurately, state assumptions, apply frameworks to a realistic organisation and justify recommendations using customer, financial, process, people, risk and sustainability perspectives.

1. Role of warehousing in supply chain strategy

Role of warehousing in supply chain strategy is a core management concern because it shapes the way materials, information, capacity, money and service commitments move through a supply network. Managers should understand the purpose of the activity, the decisions it supports and the consequences of poor execution. The correct approach depends on product mix, customer promise, operating scale, regulatory context and exposure to uncertainty.

A sound analysis begins by defining the process boundary and the outcome that matters. The team identifies inputs, outputs, owners, decision points, constraints, information requirements and interfaces with suppliers, internal functions and customers. It then establishes a baseline using reliable operational and financial data. Definitions must be consistent across departments.

In practice, the manager compares alternative methods rather than assuming that one technique is universally superior. Options are evaluated against total lifecycle cost, service performance, quality, flexibility, safety, sustainability and risk. A lower direct cost can create hidden expenditure elsewhere through rework, delays, emergency action or customer dissatisfaction.

Implementation requires documented procedures, competent people, suitable technology and clear accountability. Responsibilities should be assigned for routine execution, exception approval, record maintenance and independent review. Staff need practical training using realistic cases, while suppliers and customers should understand changed interfaces or commitments.

Performance should be monitored through balanced leading and lagging indicators. Measures may include service attainment, cycle time, cost per transaction, quality, utilisation, variability, exception frequency and recovery time. Each indicator needs a precise formula, owner, source, reporting frequency and target.

A recurring challenge is the gap between a written process and actual behaviour. Workarounds, spreadsheet records, delayed transactions and informal approvals may appear convenient but weaken control and visibility. Leaders should investigate why the workaround exists, remove unnecessary friction and preserve essential safeguards.

For examination and professional practice, connect the concept to a realistic scenario. State assumptions, identify stakeholders, explain trade-offs and justify the recommendation with evidence. Where calculations are required, show the formula and units, test operational feasibility and interpret the result.

Applied learning task: Map this activity in an organisation you know. Identify one source of delay or waste, one control weakness, one data requirement and one performance measure. Propose a practical improvement, explain who must approve it and state how you would verify sustained benefit.

A distribution centre loses operating capacity

Situation

A regional distribution centre experiences a major equipment failure during a seasonal peak. Orders are accumulating, customers have fixed delivery windows and alternative facilities have limited spare capacity.

Analysis

The management team establishes a control room, identifies priority orders, checks inventory and labour availability at nearby sites, and maps transport capacity. It separates immediate service recovery from the investigation of the failure and tests data before reallocating stock.

Management response

The organisation activates a pre-agreed continuity route, redirects selected orders, communicates revised delivery commitments and protects safety and traceability. It uses temporary manual controls only with numbered records and reconciliation after systems recover.

Evaluation

Recovery is assessed through order backlog, time to restore service, cost per order, missed delivery windows and customer complaints. The continuity plan is revised to address single points of failure and the exercise is repeated.

A key supplier fails unexpectedly

Situation

A critical supplier announces that it cannot fulfil orders for several weeks. The buyer has limited stock, uncertain demand and no immediately approved replacement.

Analysis

Procurement, operations, quality and finance assess material criticality, available stock, open orders, substitute specifications, supplier solvency indicators and qualification time. They estimate the time before service failure and identify customers or production lines at greatest risk.

Management response

The business allocates remaining stock by transparent priority rules, expedites a qualified alternative where safe, negotiates recovery milestones and communicates realistic lead times. It does not bypass technical or regulatory approval merely to obtain a faster source.

Evaluation

The organisation measures recovery time, service impact, premium freight and supplier concentration. It introduces early-warning indicators, alternative-source qualification and periodic continuity tests.

A quality defect triggers a product recall

Situation

A batch of supplied components is found to have a defect that may affect products already distributed to customers. Records are incomplete across two warehouses and one contract manufacturer.

Analysis

The response team establishes product scope, lot genealogy, supplier records, production consumption, shipment history and customer exposure. It places affected stock on hold, preserves evidence and confirms legal, safety and notification requirements with qualified specialists.

Management response

The organisation activates traceability and recall procedures, stops further distribution, informs relevant parties through approved channels and arranges controlled return, inspection or replacement. It distinguishes confirmed affected lots from unaffected stock using evidence.

Evaluation

The review evaluates detection time, traceability completeness, containment speed, customer impact and corrective-action effectiveness. Supplier controls, incoming inspection and data integration are improved.

A cyber incident disrupts supply chain systems

Situation

A ransomware incident affects the organisation's warehouse and transport platforms. Inventory visibility is reduced and dispatch teams cannot rely on normal system transactions.

Analysis

IT and operations determine the scope of compromised systems, isolate affected assets, verify backups and identify safe manual operating procedures. They assess the integrity of inventory, order, carrier and supplier data before restoring interfaces.

Management response

The organisation prioritises critical services, activates incident response and continuity arrangements, uses controlled offline records where necessary and communicates with suppliers and customers. Recovery is staged with security validation and transaction reconciliation.

Evaluation

The organisation tests restoration time, data loss, order accuracy and control effectiveness. It strengthens access management, backups, supplier connectivity standards and incident exercises.

Chapter summary and revision guidance

Warehousing, Distribution and Logistics Management is not an isolated technical activity. It forms part of a connected supply network in which decisions affect customers, suppliers, operations, finance and risk. Effective managers establish clear objectives, use reliable data, coordinate stakeholders and evaluate options using total-system outcomes.

For revision, prepare a process map, define key terms, practise applying the frameworks to an organisation, identify common implementation risks and explain how performance can be measured. In examination responses, avoid unsupported claims and show how the recommendation would be implemented, controlled and reviewed.
