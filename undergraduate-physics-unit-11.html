Unit overview

Thermodynamics is the macroscopic science of energy, heat, work, entropy and equilibrium. This undergraduate unit develops the laws of thermodynamics, derives process relations, introduces thermodynamic potentials and statistical interpretation, and applies theory to engines, refrigerators, phase changes, real gases and laboratory measurements.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: calculus, mechanics, kinetic theory and introductory physics. Study approach: reproduce derivations, draw process diagrams, practise property calculations and check results against energy conservation and the second law.

Learning outcomes

  • Define systems, properties, equilibrium, heat, work and internal energy.
  • Apply all four laws of thermodynamics.
  • Derive and use ideal-gas process equations, heat capacities and entropy changes.
  • Analyse engines, refrigerators, power cycles and phase equilibria.
  • Use thermodynamic potentials, Maxwell relations and statistical ideas.
  • Evaluate measurements, uncertainty and irreversibility.

1. Systems, boundaries and state variables

Thermodynamics describes macroscopic systems through measurable properties and energy transfers. A system may be a fixed mass, a flowing control volume or an isolated idealisation. State variables include pressure, temperature, volume, density, internal energy and entropy. Intensive properties do not scale with size; extensive properties do.

Use a piston-cylinder as a closed system when its mass is fixed, and a turbine as a control volume when fluid crosses its boundary. A good boundary choice simplifies the mass and energy balances.

In a quantitative treatment, begin by defining the state variables and the physical constraint. Write the governing conservation or equilibrium relation before inserting numerical values. This separates the physical model from the arithmetic and makes the assumptions visible to a reader or examiner.

Check the result dimensionally and examine limiting cases. For example, a reversible limit should not predict more work than the corresponding ideal bound, and an isolated system should not show a decrease in total entropy. A numerical answer that violates a conservation law requires the model or sign convention to be revisited.

In laboratory work, identify the instrument range, calibration, resolution, response time and likely heat exchange with the surroundings. Repeat measurements where possible, retain raw data, plot residuals and propagate uncertainties through the final equation. Distinguish random scatter from systematic bias.

Applications include power generation, refrigeration, materials processing, chemical engineering, climate science, cryogenics, transport and biological energy conversion. The same principles apply across scales, but the appropriate equation of state, transport model and boundary conditions depend on the material and operating regime.

2. Temperature and zeroth law

Temperature determines the direction of spontaneous heat transfer. The zeroth law states that if A is in thermal equilibrium with C and B is in equilibrium with C, then A and B are in equilibrium. This transitive property makes thermometry possible.

Thermometers use reproducible properties such as liquid expansion, electrical resistance, thermoelectric emf or radiation intensity. Calibration connects the sensor response to a recognised temperature scale and traceable reference.

3. Thermal expansion and calorimetry

For small temperature intervals, linear expansion is ΔL=αL₀ΔT and volume expansion is ΔV=βV₀ΔT. For isotropic solids β is approximately 3α over a suitable range. Calorimetry uses energy balance and measured temperature changes to determine heat capacities or latent heats.

Specific heat capacity is energy needed to raise one kilogram by one kelvin under a stated constraint. Latent heat is energy absorbed or released during a phase change without a temperature change at equilibrium.

4. Heat, work and internal energy

Heat and work are modes of energy transfer across a boundary; neither is a property stored inside a system. Internal energy is a state function associated with microscopic energy. Heat transfer occurs because of a temperature difference; work transfer occurs through organised interactions.

Adopt Q positive into the system and W positive when work is done by it. For a closed stationary system, ΔU=Q−W. For quasi-static pressure-volume work, W=∫p dV; expansion gives positive work output.

5. First law for closed systems

The first law is conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems. Total energy includes internal, kinetic and gravitational potential contributions. For a closed system, ΔU+ΔKE+ΔPE=Q−W, with additional energy forms included where significant.

For a stationary system with negligible kinetic and potential changes, ΔU=Q−W. At constant volume boundary work vanishes; for an adiabatic boundary Q=0. A cycle returns to its initial state, so net heat input equals net work output.

6. Specific heats and ideal-gas energy

For an ideal gas, pV=nRT and internal energy depends only on temperature. The constant-volume heat capacity is Cv=(∂u/∂T)v and Cp=(∂h/∂T)p. Mayer's relation is Cp−Cv=R on a molar basis.

The ratio γ=Cp/Cv controls reversible adiabatic relations. A monatomic ideal gas has γ=5/3 in the classical regime; a diatomic gas near ordinary temperatures has γ near 7/5 because rotational degrees of freedom contribute.

7. Quasi-static, reversible and irreversible processes

A quasi-static process proceeds slowly enough for the system to remain close to equilibrium. A reversible process can be reversed by infinitesimal changes without net effects on system and surroundings. Quasi-static does not automatically mean reversible.

Friction, viscous dissipation, mixing, unrestrained expansion, electrical resistance and heat transfer across finite temperature differences are irreversible. They generate entropy and reduce useful work potential.

8. Isothermal and isochoric processes

An isothermal process occurs at constant temperature. For an ideal gas ΔU=0 and reversible work is W=nRT ln(V₂/V₁). An isochoric process occurs at fixed volume, so boundary work is zero and Q=ΔU=nCvΔT for constant Cv.

A rigid sealed vessel approximates an isochoric process. A piston in effective thermal contact with a large reservoir can approximate isothermal behaviour if heat transfer is sufficiently rapid relative to the process.

9. Isobaric and adiabatic processes

An isobaric process occurs at constant pressure and, for a closed system with only pressure-volume work, Q=ΔH. An adiabatic process has no heat transfer; compression generally raises gas temperature while expansion generally lowers it.

For a reversible adiabatic ideal gas with constant heat capacities, pVγ=constant, TV^(γ−1)=constant and Tγp^(1−γ)=constant. These relations do not apply to arbitrary irreversible adiabatic changes.

10. Polytropic processes and p-V diagrams

A polytropic process follows pVⁿ=constant. For n≠1, boundary work is W=(p₂V₂−p₁V₁)/(1−n); for n=1 the result is W=p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁). Special cases include constant pressure, isothermal and reversible adiabatic behaviour.

A p-V diagram shows work as signed area under the process curve. Clockwise cycles deliver net work under the chosen convention; counter-clockwise cycles require net work input.

11. Second law and heat-engine limits

The Kelvin–Planck statement says no cyclic device can absorb heat from a single reservoir and convert it entirely into work without another effect. The Clausius statement says heat cannot by itself flow from cold to hot.

A heat engine must reject some energy to a colder sink. A refrigerator can move heat uphill only with work input. The second law adds directionality that is absent from energy conservation alone.

12. Carnot cycle and efficiency

The Carnot cycle is an ideal reversible cycle between hot reservoir TH and cold reservoir TC, with two isotherms and two adiabatic processes. It provides the maximum possible efficiency for an engine between those temperatures.

For a reversible cycle QH/TH=QC/TC, so η=1−TC/TH. A reversible refrigerator has COPR=TC/(TH−TC), and a heat pump has COPHP=TH/(TH−TC). Use kelvin.

13. Entropy and Clausius inequality

For reversible heat transfer, dS=δQrev/T. Entropy is a state property although heat is path-dependent. The Clausius inequality gives ∮δQ/T≤0, with equality for a reversible cycle and strict inequality for an irreversible cycle.

For an isolated system ΔS≥0. Entropy generation is non-negative and occurs in friction, mixing, throttling, viscous flow and heat transfer across finite temperature differences.

14. Entropy balances

For a closed system, ΔS=∫δQ/Tboundary+Sgen. For a control volume, entropy also enters and leaves with mass flow. The boundary temperature is the local temperature where heat crosses the boundary.

For heat Q flowing from TH to TC, total entropy change is −Q/TH+Q/TC, positive when TC is lower. A negative calculated entropy generation usually indicates a sign error or omitted flow term.

15. Thermodynamic potentials

Enthalpy H=U+pV is useful for constant-pressure processes and flowing systems. Helmholtz free energy F=U−TS is useful at fixed temperature and volume. Gibbs free energy G=H−TS is useful at fixed temperature and pressure.

Their differentials reveal natural variables: dF=−S dT−p dV and dG=−S dT+V dp for a simple fixed-composition system. At constant T,p, spontaneous change reduces G until equilibrium.

16. Maxwell relations

Maxwell relations follow from equality of mixed second derivatives of thermodynamic potentials. From dG=−S dT+V dp, one obtains (∂S/∂p)T=−(∂V/∂T)p.

These relations convert entropy derivatives into measurable pressure-volume-temperature derivatives. Subscripts identify the held-fixed variables and must never be omitted in a derivation.

17. Phase transitions and Clapeyron equation

At phase equilibrium, molar Gibbs free energies of the phases are equal. Differentiating along a coexistence curve gives dp/dT=L/[T(v₂−v₁)], where L is latent heat per mole and v₂−v₁ is the molar volume change.

The slope of a phase boundary depends on latent heat and volume change. Water's solid-liquid boundary has an unusual negative slope near ordinary pressure because ice has a larger specific volume than liquid water.

18. Real gases and equations of state

Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour because molecules have finite size and intermolecular forces. Compressibility factor Z=pv/(RT) measures deviation; Z=1 for an ideal gas.

The van der Waals equation introduces molecular-volume and attractive-pressure corrections. Virial and cubic equations of state are used in dense-fluid engineering, with model choice depending on pressure, temperature and composition.

19. Kinetic theory of gases

Kinetic theory relates pressure and temperature to molecular motion. For a dilute ideal gas, pV=(1/3)Nm⟨c²⟩. Comparing with pV=NkBT gives mean translational kinetic energy (1/2)m⟨c²⟩=(3/2)kBT.

The rms speed is crms=√(3RT/M). Equipartition assigns kBT/2 to each independent quadratic degree of freedom in the classical regime; quantum effects freeze some modes at low temperature.

20. Molecular speed and transport

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution describes a range of molecular speeds. Most probable, mean and rms speeds differ. Diffusion transports mass, viscosity transports momentum and thermal conductivity transports energy.

For hard spheres of diameter d and number density n, mean free path is approximately λ=1/(√2πd²n). Rarefied-gas behaviour occurs when mean free path is not small relative to apparatus dimensions.

21. Heat engines and refrigerators

A heat engine absorbs QH, rejects QC and delivers W=QH−QC, with η=W/QH. A refrigerator removes heat from a cold space and uses work input; COPR=QC/W. A heat pump delivers heat to a warm space, COPHP=QH/W.

A vapour-compression refrigerator contains an evaporator, compressor, condenser and expansion valve. Real performance is reduced by pressure drops, compressor inefficiency, heat leaks and auxiliary power.

Worked numerical examples

Each solution states the physical model, applies the relevant law and interprets the result. Repeat the calculations independently and check dimensions and limiting cases.

Worked example 1: First-law balance

A closed system receives 650 kJ heat and delivers 240 kJ work. ΔU=Q−W=650−240=410 kJ. Internal energy increases by 410 kJ.

Worked example 2: Isothermal expansion

Two moles of ideal gas expand at 300 K from 0.010 to 0.025 m³. W=nRT ln(V₂/V₁)=4.57 kJ. Since ΔU=0, Q=W.

Worked example 3: Constant-volume heating

A 0.50 kg gas with Cv=718 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ warms from 290 to 410 K in a rigid vessel. Q=ΔU=mCvΔT=43.1 kJ.

Worked example 4: Adiabatic compression

Air with γ=1.40 is compressed from 100 to 800 kPa, initially at 300 K. T₂/T₁=8^[(γ−1)/γ]=1.811, so T₂≈543 K.

Worked example 5: Carnot efficiency

An ideal engine operates between 900 K and 300 K. η=1−300/900=0.667. For 2.4 MJ heat input, work is 1.6 MJ and rejection is 0.8 MJ.

Worked example 6: Entropy change

One mole of ideal gas warms from 300 to 450 K at constant volume. With Cv,m=3R/2, ΔS=1.5R ln(1.5)=5.06 J/K.

Worked example 7: Refrigerator COP

A refrigerator removes 1.8 kW while consuming 0.60 kW. COPR=3.0 and condenser heat rejection is 2.4 kW.

Worked example 8: Thermal expansion

A 2.0 m steel bar with α=12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ warms by 65 K. ΔL=αLΔT=1.56 mm.

Worked example 9: Polytropic exponent

A gas expands from 500 kPa to 150 kPa while volume triples. n=ln(500/150)/ln(3)=1.095. Use W=(p₂V₂−p₁V₁)/(1−n).

Worked example 10: Joule-Thomson estimate

For μJT=0.25 K/bar and pressure drop −4 bar, ΔT≈μJTΔp=−1.0 K, assuming the coefficient remains nearly constant.

Worked example 11: Mayer relation

If Cv,m=20.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, Cp,m=Cv,m+R=29.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and γ≈1.40.

Worked example 12: Entropy generation

500 kJ passes from 500 K to 300 K. ΔStotal=−500/500+500/300=0.667 kJ/K.

Worked example 13: Steady-flow heater

Air flows at 0.8 kg/s and enthalpy rises 45 kJ/kg. With negligible work and kinetic and potential changes, heat input is 36 kW.

Worked example 14: Latent heat

A 0.25 kg sample melts with L=334 kJ/kg. Q=mL=83.5 kJ; temperature remains approximately constant during equilibrium melting.

Worked example 15: RMS molecular speed

For nitrogen at 300 K with M=0.0280 kg/mol, crms=√(3RT/M)=517 m/s.

University examination questions and model answers

These original revision questions test definitions, derivations, conceptual understanding and applications. They are practice materials, not official past papers.

Question 1. State the zeroth law and explain thermometry.

Model answer: If A is in thermal equilibrium with C and B is in equilibrium with C, then A and B are in equilibrium. A calibrated thermometer acts as C and assigns a common temperature.

Question 2. Distinguish intensive and extensive properties.

Model answer: Temperature, pressure, density and specific volume are intensive. Mass, total volume, internal energy and entropy are extensive and scale with system size.

Question 3. Why is heat not a state property?

Model answer: Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference and depends on the path. Internal energy is a state property determined by the endpoints.

Question 4. Derive the closed-system first law.

Model answer: Conservation gives ΔU+ΔKE+ΔPE=Q−W. If kinetic and potential changes are negligible, ΔU=Q−W, with heat inward and work outward positive.

Question 5. A system receives 900 J heat and has 350 J work done on it. Find ΔU.

Model answer: Work done on the system is W=−350 J. Thus ΔU=900−(−350)=1250 J.

Question 6. Derive isothermal ideal-gas work.

Model answer: For constant T, p=nRT/V. Therefore W=∫p dV=nRT ln(V₂/V₁). Expansion gives positive work output.

Question 7. Why is Cp greater than Cv?

Model answer: At constant volume no boundary work occurs. At constant pressure the gas expands and performs work while heated, so more heat is required for the same temperature rise.

Question 8. Derive reversible adiabatic pVγ relation.

Model answer: From δQ=0, Cv dT=−p dV. Use pV=nRT and integrate dT/T=−(R/Cv)dV/V to obtain TV^(γ−1)=constant and pVγ=constant.

Question 9. Differentiate adiabatic and isentropic.

Model answer: Adiabatic means no heat transfer. Isentropic means constant entropy. Only a reversible adiabatic process is necessarily isentropic.

Question 10. State Kelvin–Planck statement.

Model answer: No cyclic engine can convert all heat absorbed from a single reservoir into work without another effect. A real engine must reject heat.

Question 11. Derive Carnot efficiency.

Model answer: For a reversible cycle QH/TH=QC/TC. Hence η=(QH−QC)/QH=1−TC/TH, using kelvin.

Question 12. Carnot engine between 750 K and 300 K: efficiency?

Model answer: η=1−300/750=0.60, so the theoretical upper limit is 60%.

Question 13. Define entropy and Clausius inequality.

Model answer: For reversible heat transfer dS=δQrev/T. For a cycle ∮δQ/T≤0; equality is reversible and strict inequality is irreversible.

Question 14. Explain entropy generation in heat transfer.

Model answer: For Q flowing from TH to TC, total entropy change is −Q/TH+Q/TC, positive when TC is lower.

Question 15. Write ideal-gas entropy change.

Model answer: For constant heat capacities, Δs=Cv ln(T₂/T₁)+R ln(v₂/v₁). It assumes ideal-gas behaviour and kelvin temperatures.

Question 16. Define enthalpy.

Model answer: H=U+pV. Flowing mass carries enthalpy across a control-volume boundary, simplifying turbine, compressor and heater energy balances.

Question 17. Explain Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies.

Model answer: F=U−TS is useful at fixed T,V; G=H−TS at fixed T,p. Under the corresponding constraints, spontaneous change reduces the appropriate potential.

Question 18. Derive a Maxwell relation from Gibbs energy.

Model answer: For dG=−S dT+V dp, equality of mixed derivatives gives (∂S/∂p)T=−(∂V/∂T)p.

Question 19. State Clapeyron equation.

Model answer: Along a coexistence curve dp/dT=L/[T(v₂−v₁)], with L latent heat per mole and v₂−v₁ the molar volume change.

Question 20. Define Joule-Thomson coefficient.

Model answer: μJT=(∂T/∂p)H. Its sign determines whether pressure reduction cools or warms a real gas.

Question 21. Why does ideal-gas throttling not change temperature?

Model answer: Ideal-gas enthalpy depends only on temperature. Throttling is approximately isenthalpic, so temperature remains unchanged.

Question 22. State kinetic-theory assumptions.

Model answer: Molecules move randomly, collide elastically, occupy negligible volume and exert negligible long-range forces in the dilute ideal-gas model.

Question 23. Derive rms molecular speed.

Model answer: (1/2)m⟨c²⟩=(3/2)kBT, so crms=√(3kBT/m)=√(3RT/M).

Question 24. Explain partition function.

Model answer: It sums statistical weights of accessible energy states, normalises probabilities and generates free energy, mean energy and heat capacity.

Question 25. Compare engine, refrigerator and heat pump.

Model answer: An engine converts part of absorbed heat to work. A refrigerator uses work to remove heat from a cold space. A heat pump delivers heat to a warm space.

Question 26. Why can refrigerator COP exceed one?

Model answer: COP is heat removed divided by work input. Rejected heat equals removed heat plus work, so the device moves environmental energy.

Question 27. Describe Rankine cycle.

Model answer: A pump raises liquid pressure, boiler adds heat, turbine produces work during expansion, and condenser rejects heat and returns vapour to liquid.

Question 28. Compare Otto and Diesel cycles.

Model answer: Otto heat addition is constant-volume; Diesel heat addition is constant-pressure. Their ideal cycles model spark-ignition and compression-ignition engines respectively.

Question 29. State the third law.

Model answer: Entropy of a perfect crystal with a unique ground state approaches zero as temperature approaches zero. Residual entropy may remain if disorder persists.

Question 30. Explain reversible and irreversible processes.

Model answer: A reversible process can be reversed by infinitesimal changes without net effects. Friction, mixing, finite temperature differences and unrestrained expansion are irreversible.

Question 31. A heat pump delivers 12 kW using 3 kW. Find COP.

Model answer: COPHP=12/3=4. Energy balance gives 9 kW extracted from the low-temperature source.

Question 32. A 1.5 kg sample with c=900 J/kgK warms 35 K. Find Q.

Model answer: Q=mcΔT=1.5×900×35=47,250 J=47.3 kJ.

Question 33. Why use kelvin in Carnot and entropy equations?

Model answer: Kelvin has an absolute zero, making temperature ratios and logarithmic entropy expressions physically meaningful.

Question 34. Discuss uncertainty in thermodynamic experiments.

Model answer: Calibration, resolution, heat loss, thermal lag and measured masses or pressures contribute uncertainty. Propagation and repeat trials quantify effects and expose bias.

Question 35. How can entropy generation guide design?

Model answer: It identifies irreversibility and lost work potential. Reducing friction, pressure drops and finite-temperature-difference heat transfer can improve performance.

Formula summary and revision checklist

  • Closed-system first law: ΔU+ΔKE+ΔPE=Q−W.
  • Boundary work: W=∫p dV.
  • Ideal gas: pV=nRT; pv=RT.
  • Enthalpy: H=U+pV.
  • Mayer relation: Cp−Cv=R; γ=Cp/Cv.
  • Isothermal work: W=nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  • Reversible adiabatic ideal gas: pVγ=constant.
  • Carnot efficiency: η=1−TC/TH.
  • Entropy: dS=δQrev/T.
  • Ideal-gas entropy change: Δs=Cv ln(T₂/T₁)+R ln(v₂/v₁).
  • Helmholtz: F=U−TS; Gibbs: G=H−TS.
  • Clapeyron: dp/dT=L/[T(v₂−v₁)].
  • RMS speed: crms=√(3RT/M).
  • Refrigerator COP=QC/W; heat-pump COP=QH/W.

Laboratory investigations

Specific heat capacity of a solid

Measure the mass of a metal sample and heat it in a controlled bath. Transfer it rapidly into an insulated calorimeter containing a measured mass of water. Stir to equilibrium and use an energy balance including calorimeter heat capacity. Repeat trials and quantify heat loss and transfer-time effects.

Pressure-temperature relation at constant volume

Use a pressure-rated gas thermometer with fixed volume and calibrated temperature sensor. Record pressure at stable temperatures without exceeding apparatus ratings. Plot absolute pressure against kelvin temperature. Curvature can reveal calibration offsets, dead volume and non-ideal behaviour.

Latent heat by electrical heating

Supply measured electrical energy to a sample undergoing phase change. Record voltage, current and time, and measure sample mass. Correct for heat losses using a blank or cooling-curve method, then calculate latent heat after accounting for sensible heating.

Engine efficiency and reversible limit

Measure fuel or electrical input, useful output and reservoir temperatures. Calculate measured efficiency and the Carnot upper bound. Identify losses through exhaust, friction, heat transfer, leakage and auxiliary systems. The Carnot limit is not the expected efficiency of a real machine.
