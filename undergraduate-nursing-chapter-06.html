Purpose and principles of health assessment

Health assessment is a systematic process of collecting, validating, organising and interpreting information about a person's health. It establishes a baseline, identifies strengths and needs, supports clinical decisions, monitors change and informs person-centred care. Assessment is continuous: information gathered during conversation, observation, examination, records and investigations should be integrated rather than treated as unrelated facts.

A complete assessment respects privacy, dignity, culture, language, disability, health literacy and the person's right to decline. Explain the purpose and sequence, obtain appropriate consent, use a chaperone when indicated and maintain professional boundaries. Students perform examinations only within their training and supervision arrangements.

Preparation, environment and patient identification

Prepare by reviewing the referral or reason for assessment, checking equipment, ensuring lighting and privacy, performing hand hygiene and reducing interruptions. Introduce yourself and your role, confirm identity with the approved identifiers, explain what will happen and ask permission before touching or exposing any body area. Offer a support person or chaperone according to preference and policy.

Position the person comfortably and expose only the area being examined, using drapes appropriately. Consider pain, fatigue, mobility, temperature, hearing, vision, interpreter needs and infection precautions. Clean shared equipment between patients and follow local protocols for equipment calibration and maintenance.

Health history and therapeutic interviewing

A health history commonly includes presenting concern, history of the current problem, past medical and surgical history, medicines, allergies and reactions, immunisation where relevant, family history, personal and social history, occupation, living conditions, functional status and health-related behaviours. Use open questions initially, then focused questions to clarify details.

For a symptom, explore onset, location, duration, character, aggravating and relieving factors, radiation, timing, severity and associated features. Ask what the symptom means to the person and how it affects daily life. Avoid leading questions and assumptions. Summarise key points and invite correction to validate the account.

Subjective and objective data

Subjective data are experiences reported by the person or an appropriate informant, such as pain, nausea, dizziness or fatigue. Objective data are observable or measurable findings, such as temperature, respiratory rate, swelling, skin appearance or laboratory values. Both are important; neither should be dismissed simply because it cannot be measured directly.

Distinguish direct observation from interpretation. For example, record 'respiratory rate 28 breaths/min, accessory muscle use observed' rather than 'patient is anxious' unless anxiety has been assessed and supported. Identify the source of information, time collected, measurement method and any limitations.

General survey and initial impression

The general survey begins as the nurse observes appearance, behaviour, level of consciousness, posture, mobility, speech, hygiene, nutritional appearance and signs of distress. It is not a diagnosis. It helps identify immediate safety concerns and guides the order and urgency of a more detailed assessment.

Assess whether the person appears acutely unwell, has difficulty breathing, shows altered responsiveness, severe bleeding or other emergency features. Follow local emergency response pathways rather than continuing a routine examination when immediate intervention is required.

Physical examination techniques

The four classic techniques are inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation. Inspection uses sight and sometimes smell to observe colour, symmetry, movement, lesions and effort. Palpation uses touch to assess temperature, texture, tenderness, pulses, masses and organ borders, using appropriate light or deeper pressure only when trained.

Percussion produces sounds by tapping the body surface and can help estimate underlying density or organ boundaries. Auscultation uses a stethoscope to hear internal sounds such as breath, heart and bowel sounds. The sequence varies by body system; for abdominal examination, auscultation is generally performed before palpation and percussion to avoid altering bowel activity.

Vital signs and pain assessment

Vital signs commonly include temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation; pain and level of consciousness are also routinely assessed in many settings. Record values with units, time, position, method, oxygen support and relevant context. Use equipment correctly and compare with baseline and trends.

Pulse assessment may include rate, rhythm, volume and symmetry. Respiratory assessment includes rate, depth, effort, pattern and associated symptoms. Blood pressure accuracy depends on rest, posture, cuff size and technique. Pulse oximetry is an estimate and can be affected by perfusion, motion, device quality and other factors. Use local early-warning thresholds and escalate concerns.

Head-to-toe and focused assessment

A head-to-toe assessment provides a structured review of major body systems, while a focused assessment concentrates on a specific concern. Typical components include neurological status, head and neck, eyes and ears, respiratory system, cardiovascular and peripheral vascular status, abdomen, musculoskeletal function, skin and relevant psychosocial needs.

Use a consistent sequence while adapting to clinical priority and the person's tolerance. Compare sides where appropriate, explain findings in plain language and avoid unnecessary repeated exposure. A normal finding does not exclude disease; interpret findings alongside history, risk factors and investigations.

System-based assessment and clinical reasoning

Clinical reasoning links history, examination findings, baseline status, risk factors and available investigations. Identify cues, group related findings, consider possible explanations, recognise uncertainty, prioritise urgent risks and decide what information or help is needed next. Reassess after interventions and when the person's condition changes.

A systematic approach reduces omission but does not replace judgement. Use validated tools and local pathways for neurological observations, respiratory distress, falls risk, pressure injury risk or deterioration. Never delay escalation of a concerning change while completing a checklist.

Documentation, handover and escalation

Record findings promptly, objectively and in the approved record. Include relevant positives and negatives, measurement methods, the person's account, consent where relevant, limitations, actions taken, response and communication with the clinical team. Correct errors transparently according to policy; do not backdate or falsify entries.

Use structured handover such as SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment and Recommendation. State the concern clearly, provide relevant trends and request a specific review or action. Urgent findings require direct communication using the local escalation system; an electronic note alone is not sufficient.

Integrated clinical-learning case

Scenario: During a supervised placement, a student encounters a patient whose condition or care needs require closer assessment. The patient asks questions and expresses preferences that may affect the proposed plan. The student must listen, gather relevant information, check identity and consent, assess immediate safety, communicate findings and seek supervision.

Discussion prompts: Which information is subjective and which is objective? What additional assessment would be appropriate within the student's competence? Which features require urgent escalation? How can dignity, privacy and shared decision-making be protected? What information should be included in a structured handover?

Learning point: Safe nursing integrates knowledge, observation, communication, ethical conduct and teamwork. Clinical decisions and interventions must follow approved local protocols and qualified supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For extended responses, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and connect the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Differentiate subjective from objective assessment data.

    Show answer guidance

    Subjective data are reported experiences; objective data are observed or measured findings. Both are needed and should be recorded with their source and context.

  2. Name the four classic physical examination techniques.

    Show answer guidance

    Inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation.

  3. Why is abdominal auscultation generally performed before palpation?

    Show answer guidance

    Palpation and percussion can alter bowel activity; listening first helps avoid influencing the sounds being assessed.

  4. What information belongs in a focused symptom history?

    Show answer guidance

    Onset, location, duration, character, aggravating and relieving factors, radiation, timing, severity and associated features.

  5. Explain the purpose of SBAR.

    Show answer guidance

    It structures urgent or routine communication into Situation, Background, Assessment and Recommendation.

  6. Why should findings be compared with baseline?

    Show answer guidance

    A change from the person's usual state may be more informative than an isolated value within a population reference range.

  7. State two limitations of pulse oximetry.

    Show answer guidance

    It estimates peripheral oxygen saturation and may be affected by perfusion, movement, device quality and other factors; it must be interpreted clinically.

  8. What should happen when an emergency sign is identified during a routine assessment?

    Show answer guidance

    Stop the routine sequence, call for help and follow the local emergency response pathway.

  9. Give two examples of objective data.

    Show answer guidance

    Measured blood pressure and observed swelling are examples.

  10. Why is consent required during physical examination?

    Show answer guidance

    It respects autonomy and bodily integrity and ensures the person understands and agrees to the proposed examination.

  11. What is the difference between a general and focused assessment?

    Show answer guidance

    A general assessment surveys broad health status; a focused assessment investigates a particular concern or system.

  12. State two features of high-quality clinical documentation.

    Show answer guidance

    It is timely, accurate, objective, complete, attributable and stored in the approved record.

  13. Why should the nurse validate information with the patient?

    Show answer guidance

    Validation checks accuracy, corrects misunderstandings and gives the person an opportunity to clarify their account.

  14. What is clinical reasoning in assessment?

    Show answer guidance

    It is the process of interpreting cues, grouping findings, prioritising risks, considering explanations and deciding what action or information is needed.

  15. Name three principles for a respectful examination.

    Show answer guidance

    Privacy, explanation, consent, appropriate draping, cultural sensitivity and attention to comfort.
