Principles of child health nursing

Paediatric nursing supports infants, children and adolescents across health promotion, acute illness, chronic conditions, disability, rehabilitation and end-of-life care. Children are not simply smaller adults: anatomy, physiology, communication, medicine handling, developmental needs and patterns of illness vary with age and stage of development. Assessment and care must therefore be age-appropriate and based on current paediatric guidance.

Family-centred care recognises the child and family as partners while keeping the child's best interests, rights, assent and evolving capacity central. Nurses should explain procedures in developmentally appropriate language, preserve privacy, involve caregivers with appropriate consent and respect the child's voice. Work within local law, professional scope and safeguarding policy.

Growth, development and developmental assessment

Growth refers to measurable physical change, such as weight, length or height and head circumference. Development includes progressive acquisition of motor, language, cognitive, social and emotional abilities. Growth and development are influenced by genetics, nutrition, health, environment, relationships, culture and opportunities for learning and play.

Use appropriate age- and sex-specific growth references and interpret measurements over time rather than relying on one point. Developmental surveillance involves listening to caregiver concerns, observing function and using validated screening tools where indicated. A screening result is not a diagnosis; concerns require appropriate assessment and referral.

Paediatric communication and family partnership

Communication should match the child's developmental stage, language, sensory needs and emotional state. Infants communicate through cues and behaviour; young children benefit from simple explanations and play; school-age children may understand sequences and choices; adolescents should be included directly and offered appropriate confidential discussion within legal limits.

Ask caregivers what is usual for the child and what has changed. Use interpreters rather than relying on children to translate sensitive clinical information. Prepare children before procedures, offer realistic choices, acknowledge fear and pain, and avoid false reassurance. Document who provided information and the child's response.

Paediatric history and physical assessment

A paediatric history includes presenting concern, pregnancy and birth history when relevant, previous illness, medicines and allergies, immunisation history, feeding, growth, development, family history, social context and exposure risks. The child's baseline behaviour, intake, urine output, activity and interaction can be valuable indicators of illness.

Assessment includes general appearance, work of breathing, circulation, hydration, responsiveness, temperature, pain and age-appropriate system examination. Observe before disturbing a distressed child where possible. Use correct equipment sizes and age-appropriate reference ranges. Deterioration may present subtly; escalate concern based on appearance and trends, not a single normal-looking number.

Nutrition, feeding and fluid balance in children

Nutritional needs change rapidly during infancy, childhood and adolescence. Assessment considers feeding pattern, dietary diversity, growth trajectory, swallowing, gastrointestinal symptoms, food security, cultural practices and caregiver capacity. Breastfeeding and complementary feeding advice should follow current national and WHO-aligned guidance, adapted to individual circumstances.

Children have age-dependent fluid requirements and can deteriorate quickly with dehydration. Monitor intake, output, weight trends, mucous membranes, tears, capillary refill, activity and clinical observations as appropriate. Fluid calculations and replacement are high-risk clinical tasks and must follow approved paediatric protocols and qualified supervision.

Immunisation and prevention of childhood illness

Immunisation programmes reduce morbidity and mortality from vaccine-preventable diseases. Schedules, products, eligibility, catch-up arrangements and contraindications vary by country and are updated over time. Nurses should use the current national immunisation schedule and approved product information rather than memorised schedules in static notes.

Prevention also includes nutrition support, injury prevention, oral health, hygiene, safe sleep education, screening and early access to care. Explain expected responses and when to seek help using current local guidance. Maintain cold-chain requirements, consent, accurate records and safe injection practices according to policy.

Common childhood infections and respiratory conditions

Children may present with upper respiratory infections, bronchiolitis, pneumonia, otitis media, gastroenteritis, urinary tract infection and other communicable illnesses. Age, immune status, hydration, respiratory effort and underlying conditions influence severity. Some conditions have nonspecific early symptoms, especially in infants.

Nursing care includes assessment, infection prevention, hydration and nutrition support, comfort measures within scope, caregiver education and monitoring for deterioration. Respiratory distress, apnoea, cyanosis, poor responsiveness, inability to feed, severe dehydration or signs of sepsis require urgent escalation under local pathways.
