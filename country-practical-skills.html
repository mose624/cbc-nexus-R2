Biology practical work involves observing living organisms and biological materials, using instruments to examine structures, sampling populations, carrying out investigations and recording evidence accurately.

2. Microscope skills and magnification

  1. Carry the microscope with two hands and place it on a stable bench.
  2. Clean lenses with lens tissue only.
  3. Place the prepared slide on the stage and secure it.
  4. Select the lowest-power objective and focus carefully.
  5. Centre the specimen before changing to higher power.
  6. Use fine adjustment at high power and adjust illumination.
  7. Draw what is actually visible, using clear single lines and labels.
Total magnification = Eyepiece magnification × Objective lens magnification Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

Image size and actual size must be in the same units before division. Convert millimetres to micrometres using 1 mm = 1000 µm.

Biological drawing rules: use a sharp pencil, large clear outline, no shading, straight label lines that do not cross, and a title. Include magnification or a scale bar when required. Draw relative sizes and proportions accurately.

3. Sampling organisms using quadrats and transects

A quadrat is placed randomly or systematically in a habitat. Count individuals or estimate percentage cover within each sample. Repeat at several locations and calculate a mean. Avoid choosing only places where the target organism is obvious.

Mean number per quadrat = Total individuals counted ÷ Number of quadrats Estimated population = Mean density per unit area × Total habitat area

For a belt transect, place a tape across the habitat and sample at regular intervals or continuously along the line. Record abiotic conditions such as light intensity, soil moisture or temperature when investigating distribution. State the sampling method and limitations.

4. Common biological investigations

  • Food tests: use appropriate reagents and controls to test for starch, reducing sugars, protein and lipids. Record the initial and final colour or appearance. Use the correct reagent procedure taught in the laboratory.
  • Osmosis: compare changes in mass or length of equal-sized plant tissue pieces placed in solutions of different concentrations. Keep tissue type, dimensions, time and temperature consistent; blot consistently before weighing.
  • Enzyme activity: investigate how temperature or pH affects reaction rate. Keep enzyme and substrate concentrations, volumes and timing consistent. Repeat each condition.
  • Photosynthesis: investigate the effect of light intensity or another limiting factor on photosynthetic rate. Control temperature and carbon dioxide availability where possible.
  • Transpiration: use a potometer to estimate water uptake, not a direct measurement of water loss from leaves. Reduce leaks, cut the shoot under water where instructed, and control environmental conditions.
  • Germination: investigate the conditions needed for germination while changing one factor at a time and using a suitable control group.

5. Biological observations and records

Separate qualitative observations (colour, shape, texture, presence of structures) from quantitative measurements (mass, length, number, time, temperature). Use a labelled table, consistent units and repeat samples. Distinguish observation from interpretation: “brown region present” is an observation; “the tissue is diseased” is an interpretation that needs supporting evidence.

6. Biology practical evaluation

Discuss sample size, random sampling, measurement resolution, biological variation, environmental changes, contamination and observer bias. Improvements should address the stated limitation: for example, increase the number of randomly selected quadrats, standardise tissue dimensions or use a calibrated balance.

Biology practical mastery: detailed methods

Learning outcomes: identify biological apparatus; prepare and observe specimens; use a microscope; carry out food, osmosis and enzyme investigations; sample organisms; present biological drawings and evaluate evidence.

8. Preparing temporary microscope slides

Use a clean slide and a thin specimen. For plant epidermis, peel a small transparent section with forceps, place it flat in a drop of water, add a suitable stain if instructed, then lower a cover slip gently at an angle using a mounted needle. Blot excess liquid without moving the cover slip. For cheek-cell preparations, use only the approved sterile swab method, dispose of the swab safely and follow the school’s infection-control procedure. Never share swabs or mouth-contact equipment.

What to observe: cell boundary, cytoplasm, nucleus and any structures visible at the selected magnification. Not every cell type shows every structure clearly. A plant cell wall is distinct from the cell membrane; chloroplasts are expected only in suitable photosynthetic tissues.

10. Osmosis in plant tissue: full investigation

Aim: investigate how solution concentration affects the mass of plant tissue.

  1. Prepare several solutions of known concentration and label them clearly.
  2. Cut equal-sized potato cylinders using the same cork borer. Trim them to equal lengths and gently blot them.
  3. Measure and record the initial mass of each piece.
  4. Place one piece in each labelled solution, ensuring it is fully covered.
  5. Leave all pieces for the same measured time at a controlled temperature.
  6. Remove each piece, blot using the same method and immediately measure its final mass.
  7. Calculate change in mass and percentage change in mass for each concentration.
  8. Plot percentage change in mass against solution concentration.
Change in mass = Final mass − Initial mass Percentage change = (Change in mass ÷ Initial mass) × 100%

A positive percentage change means the tissue gained mass; a negative value means it lost mass. The concentration at which the graph crosses zero is an estimate of the isotonic concentration under the conditions used. Explain movement of water across partially permeable cell membranes from higher water potential to lower water potential.

11. Enzyme investigations: planning and interpretation

Enzymes are biological catalysts. A valid investigation changes one factor (such as temperature, pH or substrate concentration) while controlling enzyme amount, substrate amount, total volume and reaction time. Choose a measurable endpoint, for example time for a standard colour change or gas volume produced in a fixed interval. Repeat each condition and calculate a mean. At high temperatures, enzyme structure may change and the active site may no longer fit the substrate; low temperatures generally slow molecular movement without necessarily denaturing the enzyme.

12. Ecological sampling and calculations

Random sampling reduces selection bias. Use random coordinates within a measured study area, place the quadrat at each coordinate and record abundance or percentage cover using a consistent rule. For a transect, record species and abiotic conditions at measured intervals. State quadrat size, number of samples, sampling pattern and study area.

Frequency (%) = (Number of quadrats containing the species ÷ Total quadrats) × 100 Density = Total individuals counted ÷ Total area sampled

When estimating a population for a larger habitat, justify why the sampled sites represent the whole habitat. Mobile organisms, clumped distributions and seasonal changes can make estimates uncertain.

13. Biological drawings and specimen descriptions

  • Use a sharp pencil and clear continuous outlines; avoid sketchy strokes and shading.
  • Make the drawing large enough to show detail and maintain correct proportions.
  • Use straight ruled label lines that touch the structure and do not cross.
  • Include a title and magnification or scale bar where requested.
  • For comparisons, use the same magnification and identify similarities and differences separately.
  • Describe visible features before explaining their function.

⚗ Online Chemistry Practical Notes

Chemistry practical work involves handling substances safely, measuring quantities, preparing solutions, separating mixtures, carrying out reactions and analysing experimental evidence.

1. Chemistry apparatus and uses

ApparatusUse and correct handling
BeakerHolds, mixes or heats liquids. Graduations are approximate and should not be used when high volume accuracy is required.
Measuring cylinderMeasures liquid volume more accurately than a beaker. Place on a level bench and read the meniscus at eye level.

Evaporating basinConcentrates a solution or evaporates solvent using controlled heating.

2. Measurement and preparation of solutions

Use a balance to measure solid mass. Transfer the solid to a beaker and dissolve it in a portion of solvent. Transfer the solution into a volumetric flask, rinsing the beaker and funnel into the flask to avoid loss. Add solvent close to the mark, then use a dropper to bring the bottom of the meniscus to the calibration line at eye level. Stopper and invert repeatedly to mix.

Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = Amount of substance (mol) ÷ Volume (dm³) Amount (mol) = Mass (g) ÷ Molar mass (g mol⁻¹)

Remember: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Convert volume into dm³ when using mol dm⁻³.

3. Titration technique

  1. Rinse the burette with the titrant and the pipette with the solution it will deliver.
  2. Use a pipette filler to transfer the fixed aliquot into a clean conical flask.
  3. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator if the method requires one.
  4. Record the initial burette reading at eye level.
  5. Add titrant while swirling the flask; add dropwise near the endpoint.
  6. Record the final reading and calculate titre = final reading − initial reading.
  7. Repeat until concordant titres are obtained, following the examination board's stated tolerance.
Accuracy tips: use a white tile to see the endpoint, rinse flask walls with distilled water, remove air bubbles from the burette tip, read the bottom of the meniscus and record readings to the burette's appropriate resolution. Do not copy a titre or force concordance.

4. Separation and purification methods

  • Filtration: separates an insoluble solid from a liquid; the solid retained is residue and the liquid collected is filtrate.
  • Crystallisation: concentrates a solution and allows crystals to form on cooling or slow evaporation. Avoid overheating substances that decompose.
  • Simple distillation: recovers a solvent from a solution or separates liquids with sufficiently different boiling points.
  • Fractional distillation: separates miscible liquids with closer boiling points using a fractionating column.
  • Chromatography: separates mixture components by different affinities for stationary and mobile phases. Keep the baseline above the solvent level.
  • Separating funnel: separates immiscible liquid layers; identify which layer is which rather than assuming the denser liquid is always the lower layer without checking.

5. Common chemical tests

Use only the reagents and procedures specified by the teacher or syllabus. Record the reagent, conditions and positive observation. Examples include tests for common gases, ions, acids and alkalis. Never identify a gas by smelling it directly. Use approved splint tests or other prescribed methods with appropriate supervision and small quantities.

6. Reaction-rate investigations

Change one independent variable, such as concentration, temperature or surface area, while controlling other relevant factors. Measure rate by a suitable method, for example gas volume produced per unit time or time taken for a visible change. Repeat measurements and use a consistent endpoint.

Average rate = Change in measured quantity ÷ Time taken

7. Chemistry practical safety

Wear eye protection and suitable protective clothing. Read hazard labels and safety data. Use a fume cupboard where instructed, keep flammable solvents away from flames, add acid to water when preparing dilute acid, never taste chemicals and never pipette by mouth. Dispose of chemical waste as directed. Never improvise gas tests or mix unknown chemicals.

Chemistry practical mastery: detailed methods

Learning outcomes: measure mass and volume accurately; prepare standard solutions; perform titrations; separate mixtures; identify selected gases and ions safely; investigate reaction rates; calculate results and evaluate uncertainty.

8. Preparing a solution of known concentration

  1. Calculate the required mass using the stated concentration, volume and molar mass.
  2. Weigh the solid accurately using a clean weighing boat or paper.
  3. Transfer it to a beaker and dissolve it in a small amount of distilled water.
  4. Transfer quantitatively to a volumetric flask using a funnel; rinse the beaker and funnel into the flask.
  5. Add distilled water close to the calibration mark, then use a dropper to bring the bottom of the meniscus to the mark at eye level.
  6. Stopper and invert several times to mix thoroughly. Label the solution and concentration.
Mass (g) = Concentration (mol dm⁻³) × Volume (dm³) × Molar mass (g mol⁻¹)

Use the correct volume conversion: 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³. For a hydrated salt, use the molar mass of the complete hydrated formula, not the anhydrous salt.

9. Titration: calculations and quality of results

A titration determines the volume of one solution needed to react completely with a measured volume of another. A rough titration locates the approximate endpoint. Subsequent accurate runs should add titrant quickly at first, then dropwise near the endpoint while swirling. Concordant titres are close together according to the tolerance specified by the examination board; do not select values merely because they produce a preferred answer.

Titre = Final burette reading − Initial burette reading

For a reaction aA + bB → products, use the mole ratio from the balanced equation:

Moles A ÷ a = Moles B ÷ b

Then use concentration = moles ÷ volume in dm³. Always show the balanced equation, mole ratio, conversion of volume, substitution and final unit. The shortcut C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ applies only to a 1:1 mole ratio.

13. Rates of reaction: fair test

Investigate one variable at a time, such as concentration, temperature, surface area or catalyst presence. Suitable measurements include gas volume collected at fixed time intervals, mass loss where a gas escapes, or time to a clearly defined visual endpoint. Keep total volume, apparatus, reagent quantities and endpoint definition consistent. Repeat trials and plot the measured response against time or the changed variable as appropriate.

Mean rate over an interval = Change in measured quantity ÷ Time interval

For a gas-volume graph, the gradient represents rate of gas production. A steeper initial gradient indicates a faster initial reaction under comparable conditions.

2. Measurement instruments and resolution

Choose an instrument whose range and resolution suit the quantity. A ruler may be adequate for a large length, while vernier calipers or a micrometer are more suitable for small diameters. Do not report more decimal places than the instrument supports. Check zero before and after measurements when appropriate.

Vernier reading: main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × least count). Apply the zero correction using the sign of the zero error.

Micrometer reading: sleeve reading + (thimble reading × least count), followed by the appropriate zero correction.

3. Core physics investigations

  • Density: measure mass with a balance and volume by dimensions for regular objects or displacement for irregular objects. Ensure units are consistent.
  • Hooke's law: add known loads, measure extension from the original length and plot force against extension. Do not exceed the elastic limit.
  • Moments: measure the perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action of force. Balance moments in equilibrium.
  • Pendulum: measure the time for 10–20 oscillations, repeat and divide by the number of oscillations to estimate the period.

4. Graphs in Physics and all sciences

Choose the graph to match the data. A line graph is used for continuous variables; a bar chart compares discrete categories; a histogram shows a continuous frequency distribution with touching bars; a scatter graph shows the relationship between two numerical variables; a pie chart shows proportions of a whole. A frequency polygon and cumulative frequency graph are used for grouped distributions.

Parts of a scientific graph

  1. Descriptive title.
  2. Horizontal x-axis and vertical y-axis.
  3. Correct variable names and units on both axes.
  4. Even, sensible scale using most of the graph area.
  5. Accurately plotted points, usually small crosses.
  6. Line or curve of best fit where appropriate.
  7. Key or legend when more than one data series is shown.
Axis convention: the independent variable is usually plotted on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. Label axes as Quantity (unit), for example Time (s), not just “Time”. Do not join scatter points dot-to-dot unless the task specifically requires it.
Gradient = Change in y ÷ Change in x = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

Use two widely separated points on the best-fit line. Include units in the gradient. The meaning of the gradient depends on the quantities on the axes: distance–time gives speed; velocity–time gives acceleration; current–potential difference gives conductance if current is on y and potential difference on x.

5. Data processing and uncertainty

Mean = Sum of repeated readings ÷ Number of readings Percentage error = (Absolute error ÷ Accepted value) × 100% Percentage uncertainty = (Absolute uncertainty ÷ Measured value) × 100%

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. Precision describes closeness of repeated readings to one another. Reliability concerns consistency on repetition. Validity concerns whether the method tests the intended relationship. A precise result can still be inaccurate if a systematic error is present.

6. Types and sources of experimental error

  • Systematic error: shifts readings consistently, for example a zero error or poor calibration. Check zero and calibrate equipment.
  • Random error: causes unpredictable variation, for example reaction time or fluctuating conditions. Repeat readings and calculate a mean.
  • Parallax error: occurs when a scale is viewed from an angle. Read at eye level.
  • Gross error: a major mistake such as recording the wrong unit or connecting a meter incorrectly. Check readings, labels and circuit diagrams.

8. Density of regular and irregular objects

Regular solid: measure the dimensions with an appropriate instrument, calculate volume using the correct geometrical formula, measure mass on a balance and divide mass by volume. For a rectangular block, V = length × width × height; for a cylinder, V = πr²h.

Irregular solid: measure its mass. Record the initial water level in a measuring cylinder, fully submerge the object without splashing and record the final level. The increase in water volume is the object's volume, provided it does not dissolve, absorb water or react.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Use consistent units. 1 cm³ = 1 mL; 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Avoid trapped air bubbles and ensure the object is fully submerged.

9. Hooke’s law and spring constant

Measure the spring’s original length with no added load. Add known masses in safe increments, allow oscillations to stop and record the new length. Calculate extension = loaded length − original length. Convert force to newtons using W = mg and extension to metres before calculating the spring constant.

F = kx  therefore  k = F ÷ x

Within the proportional region, force is directly proportional to extension. The gradient of a force (y) against extension (x) graph is k in N m⁻¹. Do not exceed the elastic limit. A spring that does not return to its original length may have been permanently deformed.

10. Simple pendulum investigation

  1. Set up a small dense bob on a light string attached to a secure clamp.
  2. Measure the length from the point of suspension to the centre of the bob.
  3. Displace the bob through a small angle and release without pushing.
  4. Time 10–20 complete oscillations, repeating the timing.
  5. Calculate the period T by dividing total time by number of oscillations.
  6. Repeat for several lengths while keeping the release angle small.
T = Time for n oscillations ÷ n

A longer pendulum generally has a longer period. For a simple pendulum at small angles, T² is proportional to length. A graph of T² against length can be used to investigate the relationship. Keep the same bob and release method, and time many oscillations to reduce reaction-time percentage error.

11. Electrical circuit investigations

Construct the circuit with the power supply switched off. Connect the ammeter in series and the voltmeter in parallel across the component. Check polarity and ranges, then close the switch briefly to take readings. Change the variable resistor to obtain several pairs of current and potential difference readings. Switch off between adjustments if the component heats up.

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is proportional to potential difference. Plot V against I; the gradient is resistance R. If I is plotted against V, the gradient is conductance, not resistance.

V = IR  and  R = V ÷ I
Electrical safety: use only the low-voltage school supply specified by the teacher. Never connect school experimental circuits to mains electricity. Avoid short circuits and switch off before changing connections.

12. Reflection and refraction practicals

Reflection: draw a normal at the point where the ray meets the mirror. Measure angles from the normal, not from the mirror surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection for a plane mirror.

Refraction: use a ray box and transparent block or the approved pin method. Mark the incident and emergent rays, draw the normal and measure angles with a protractor. Light changes direction when its speed changes at a boundary, except when it enters along the normal.

Refractive index n = sin i ÷ sin r

For the stated material and wavelength, plot sin i against sin r if requested; check which variable is on each axis before interpreting the gradient. Avoid parallax when aligning optical pins and keep the block outline fixed.

13. Heat capacity and cooling investigations

Measure a known mass of water, record its initial temperature and supply a measured amount of electrical energy using a heater of known power for a measured time. Stir gently for uniform temperature, insulate the container where appropriate and record temperature at regular intervals. A simple estimate uses:

Energy supplied E = Power P × Time t Specific heat capacity c = E ÷ (m × temperature change)

Use SI units: joules, kilograms and kelvin or degrees Celsius for a temperature difference. Account for heat lost to surroundings and energy absorbed by the container; these make a simple result differ from a reference value.

1. Selecting the correct apparatus

Choose apparatus by considering the quantity to be measured, the expected size or range, the resolution required and the material being handled. A measuring cylinder is suitable for routine liquid-volume measurements; a volumetric pipette or volumetric flask is preferred when a specified volume must be measured or prepared accurately. A beaker is mainly for holding, mixing or heating, not precise volume measurement. Use a balance for mass, a thermometer or probe for temperature, a stopwatch or light gate for time, and a suitable length instrument for dimensions.

2. Reading scales correctly

  1. Identify the unit and the numbered scale marks.
  2. Determine the value represented by one smallest division.
  3. Place the eye perpendicular to the scale to reduce parallax.
  4. For liquid levels, read the appropriate part of the meniscus at eye level; for most aqueous liquids this is the bottom of the meniscus.
  5. Record the reading immediately, including its unit and justified decimal places.
  6. Check whether the instrument has a zero error before applying a correction.

Least count / scale interval: the value represented by the smallest scale division. For a digital instrument, use the smallest displayed increment as its resolution, while remembering that resolution is not the same as total measurement accuracy.

3. Zero error and corrected readings

Zero error is the reading shown when the true measured quantity is zero. Record both its magnitude and sign. The corrected reading is obtained by subtracting the zero error from the observed reading. For example, if an instrument has a positive zero error of +0.02 cm and displays 3.46 cm, the corrected reading is 3.44 cm. If the zero error is negative, subtracting it increases the corrected reading. Always follow the sign convention taught for the particular instrument.

4. Accuracy, precision and uncertainty

  • Accuracy: closeness of a result to an accepted or true value.
  • Precision: closeness of repeated readings to one another.
  • Repeatability: agreement when the same person uses the same method and equipment under the same conditions.
  • Reproducibility: agreement when conditions such as operator, equipment or location change.
  • Uncertainty: an estimate of the range within which the measured value is expected to lie.
Absolute error = |Measured value − Accepted value| Percentage error = (Absolute error ÷ Accepted value) × 100% Percentage uncertainty = (Absolute uncertainty ÷ Measured value) × 100%

Do not confuse percentage error, which compares a result with an accepted value, with percentage uncertainty, which expresses the uncertainty in a measurement. The method for estimating instrument uncertainty depends on the instrument and examination-board convention. For an analogue scale, courses commonly use a fraction of the smallest division; for a digital instrument, the last displayed digit is often used. Follow the stated convention.

5. Repeats, averages and anomalous results

Repeat measurements where practical. Calculate the mean of suitable repeated readings. Compare the readings before calculating: an anomalous value should not be silently deleted. Repeat the measurement if possible, record the original value and explain any decision to exclude it using evidence or a stated rule. Repeats improve confidence in consistency but do not automatically remove systematic error.

Mean = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) ÷ n

6. Writing and following a reproducible procedure

A good procedure is sufficiently detailed that another learner could repeat the investigation. Use numbered steps and include quantities, units, apparatus positions, timing, control variables and how readings are taken.

  1. State what is being investigated and identify the independent variable.
  2. State exactly how the independent variable will be changed and list the values or range.
  3. Explain how the dependent variable will be measured, including the instrument and units.
  4. Identify the variables to be kept constant and explain how they will be controlled.
  5. Describe the apparatus arrangement clearly enough to reproduce it.
  6. Give the sequence of actions in the correct order, including when to start and stop timing or take readings.
  7. State how many repeats will be made and how results will be processed.
  8. Include relevant safety precautions at the step where the hazard arises.
  9. Prepare a results table before collecting data.
Procedure writing tip: avoid vague instructions such as “heat gently”, “add some solution” or “measure carefully”. Where the method allows, specify a measurable quantity, a controlled condition or a clear endpoint. Do not invent quantities that are not supplied by the practical method.

7. Planning a fair test

Change one independent variable systematically, measure the dependent variable consistently and control other factors that could affect the result. A control setup can help show whether the observed effect is due to the factor being investigated. In biological investigations, account for natural variation by using adequate sample sizes and representative sampling. In chemical and physical investigations, keep apparatus arrangement and measurement technique consistent.

Gradient = (Change in vertical-axis quantity) ÷ (Change in horizontal-axis quantity)

A curve indicates that the rate of change may vary. Describe the observed pattern before explaining it scientifically.

Practical Investigations: Worked Examples and Skills Practice

Use these examples to practise planning, collecting, processing and evaluating practical evidence. The example values are illustrative teaching data, not official examination results or prescribed country experiments.

Biology worked investigation 1: Effect of solution concentration on plant tissue

Aim: investigate how the concentration of a surrounding solution affects the mass of equal-sized plant tissue pieces.

Apparatus and materials

Plant tissue such as potato, cork borer, scalpel, ruler, balance, labelled containers, measuring cylinder, prepared solutions of different concentrations, forceps, paper towel and stopwatch.

Procedure

  1. Use a cork borer to prepare tissue cylinders of the same diameter.
  2. Trim each cylinder to the same length and remove damaged ends.
  3. Blot each piece in the same way and record its initial mass.
  4. Place equal numbers of pieces into labelled containers with equal volumes of the test solutions.
  5. Keep immersion time and temperature the same for every concentration.
  6. Remove the pieces, blot consistently and record final mass.
  7. Repeat with additional tissue pieces at each concentration and calculate the mean percentage change in mass.
Percentage change in mass = [(Final mass − Initial mass) ÷ Initial mass] × 100%

A positive percentage change means mass increased; a negative value means mass decreased. Plot solution concentration on the x-axis and mean percentage change in mass on the y-axis. Explain the pattern using net water movement by osmosis. Improve reliability by using more tissue pieces and improve validity by keeping tissue source, dimensions, blotting method, solution volume, time and temperature consistent.

Biology worked investigation 2: Estimating plant distribution with a quadrat

Aim: estimate the abundance of a plant species in a defined habitat.

  1. Define the study area and identify the target species before sampling.
  2. Use random coordinates or a random-number method to choose quadrat positions.
  3. Place the quadrat without moving it to favour areas where the plant is abundant.
  4. Count individuals rooted inside the quadrat, or estimate percentage cover using the same rule each time.
  5. Repeat at a suitable number of positions and record each result.
  6. Calculate the mean count per quadrat and estimate total abundance only if the sampled quadrats represent the habitat adequately.
Mean count per quadrat = Sum of counts ÷ Number of quadrats

For a 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrat, area = 0.25 m². If the mean count is 8 plants per quadrat, estimated density = 8 ÷ 0.25 = 32 plants m⁻². State assumptions and uncertainty; clumped distributions and small sample sizes can make estimates less reliable.

Biology worked investigation 3: Enzyme activity and temperature

Plan a controlled investigation using an appropriate enzyme-substrate system approved by the teacher. Use a water bath to establish temperatures, measure a defined reaction endpoint consistently, keep enzyme and substrate concentrations and volumes constant, and repeat each temperature. If measuring time to a fixed endpoint, a shorter time indicates a faster reaction under the same method. Do not compare results if the endpoint or mixing method changes between trials.

Chemistry worked investigation 1: Acid–alkali titration

Aim: determine the volume of a standard solution needed to react completely with a measured volume of another solution.

Apparatus

Burette, stand and clamp, volumetric pipette, pipette filler, conical flask, funnel, suitable indicator, white tile, wash bottle and the solutions specified by the practical method.

Procedure and recording

  1. Wear eye protection and check the labels and concentrations of all solutions.
  2. Rinse the burette with the solution that will go into it, then fill it and ensure the jet contains liquid without an air bubble.
  3. Rinse the pipette with the solution to be measured and transfer the specified aliquot into a conical flask using a pipette filler.
  4. Add the required indicator if the method calls for one.
  5. Record the initial burette reading at eye level.
  6. Add solution while swirling; approach the endpoint slowly and add dropwise near the colour change.
  7. Record the final burette reading and calculate the titre.
  8. Repeat until the required concordant results are obtained under the stated course or examination tolerance.
Titre (cm³) = Final burette reading − Initial burette reading

Illustrative readings: initial 0.20 cm³ and final 24.60 cm³ gives a titre of 24.40 cm³. If repeat titres are 24.40, 24.35 and 24.40 cm³, use the concordant values according to the stated tolerance and explain which readings are included. Do not select readings merely to obtain a preferred answer.

Chemistry worked investigation 2: Paper chromatography

  1. Draw a pencil baseline near the bottom of chromatography paper.
  2. Apply a small concentrated spot of the sample to the baseline and allow it to dry; repeat spotting if instructed.
  3. Place a shallow layer of solvent in a suitable container, keeping the solvent below the baseline.
  4. Stand the paper in the container with the sample spot above the solvent and cover the container.
  5. Allow the solvent to rise; remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top.
  6. Immediately mark the solvent front in pencil and allow the paper to dry.
  7. Measure from the baseline to the centre of each separated spot and from the baseline to the solvent front.
Rf value = Distance moved by component ÷ Distance moved by solvent front

Rf values have no units and are valid for comparison only when the stationary phase, solvent and conditions are comparable. A single Rf value alone does not prove the identity or purity of a substance.

Chemistry worked investigation 3: Rate of reaction

Investigate a reaction rate by measuring a suitable change, such as gas volume produced over time, using an appropriate gas syringe and a gas-tight apparatus. Keep total volume, temperature, mass or surface area of solid and other relevant variables controlled. Record readings at regular time intervals, repeat the experiment and plot gas volume against time. The gradient represents the rate of gas production; the curve becomes less steep as the reaction slows. Follow the teacher's approved method and never seal a system in which pressure could build dangerously.

Physics worked investigation 1: Hooke's law

Aim: investigate the relationship between applied force and extension of a spring.

  1. Clamp the spring securely beside a vertical ruler and measure its original length without load.
  2. Add a known load and allow oscillations to stop.
  3. Read the new length at eye level and calculate extension = loaded length − original length.
  4. Increase the load in safe equal steps, recording force and extension each time.
  5. Repeat readings where possible and plot force (N) against extension (m or cm).
  6. Do not exceed the spring's safe limit or continue if it shows permanent deformation.
Extension = Loaded length − Original length

For a force-against-extension graph, the gradient is the spring constant when extension is measured in metres: k = F ÷ x, with unit N m⁻¹. A straight-line region through the origin is consistent with Hooke's law within the elastic limit.

Physics worked investigation 2: Finding density

For a regular solid, measure its mass with a balance and its dimensions with a suitable ruler, vernier calipers or micrometer. Calculate volume from the appropriate geometric formula, then calculate density. For an irregular solid that does not dissolve or react in water, use a displacement method with a suitable measuring cylinder or displacement can.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Example: a sample has mass 54 g and volume 20 cm³. Density = 54 ÷ 20 = 2.7 g cm⁻³. State the units and the precision justified by the measurements. Avoid using a measuring cylinder whose divisions are too large for the displaced volume.

Physics worked investigation 3: Current–voltage relationship

  1. Connect the ammeter in series with the component and the voltmeter in parallel across it.
  2. Check the circuit against the diagram before switching on.
  3. Begin with a safe power-supply setting and suitable meter ranges.
  4. Adjust the variable resistor to obtain a range of potential differences and record current and voltage.
  5. Switch off between adjustments if required to limit heating.
  6. Repeat readings and plot current against potential difference, or use the axes requested by the task.

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is proportional to potential difference. If current is plotted against voltage, the gradient is conductance; resistance is voltage divided by current. Heating can change resistance, so monitor temperature and avoid excessive current.

Suggested answers and marking guidance

  1. Extra decimal places imply a resolution and precision the ruler does not provide; record only justified digits.
  2. Accuracy is closeness to an accepted value; precision is agreement between repeated readings.
  3. Rinsing removes residual water or another liquid that could dilute or contaminate the titrant.
  4. The sample must begin above the solvent so it travels with the rising solvent rather than dissolving directly into the solvent pool.
  5. Time more oscillations and divide by the number; repeat trials and calculate a mean; use a light gate if available.
  6. Speed, because gradient is change in distance divided by change in time.
  7. Bar-chart categories are discrete; histogram bars represent adjoining intervals of a continuous variable.
  8. Independent: light intensity; dependent: photosynthesis rate; controls may include temperature, carbon dioxide concentration, plant species or leaf area.
  9. Three quadrats may not represent patchy distribution; increase the number and select positions randomly across the habitat.
  10. An ammeter measures current through a component and must be in the same current path; a voltmeter measures potential difference across a component.
  11. Extension = 11.5 − 8.0 = 3.5 cm.
  12. Use a suitable data logger or gas sensor, or choose a safe method with shorter measurement intervals and suitable apparatus.

A. Biology: additional practical topics and methods

A1. Cell structure, tissues and microscopy

  • Specimen selection: choose a thin, representative specimen. Thick specimens overlap and reduce light transmission.
  • Slide preparation: use a clean slide, add a drop of water or approved mounting liquid, position the specimen flat and lower the cover slip gently from one edge.
  • Staining: use only the stain and concentration specified by the teacher. Stains increase contrast but may alter the appearance of living material.
  • Focusing: begin with the lowest-power objective; use coarse focus at low power and fine focus at high power. Centre the object before increasing magnification.
  • Field of view: the visible circular area becomes smaller as magnification increases. If the field diameter is known, estimate the number of cells across it to estimate cell size.
  • Drawing: draw only structures observed. Use a sharp pencil, single clear lines, no shading, a title and straight ruled labels. Do not invent organelles that cannot be seen.
  • Magnification: total magnification is eyepiece power multiplied by objective power. Magnification is image size divided by actual size when both measurements use the same unit.
  • Scale bar: if a scale bar is provided, measure it and the feature on the image, then use the ratio to calculate actual size. State units.

A2. Nutrition and food tests

  • Starch: add iodine solution to a small sample. A blue-black colour is a positive result; yellow-brown indicates no starch detected.
  • Reducing sugar: add Benedict's solution and heat in a hot-water bath according to the laboratory protocol. Green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate indicates reducing sugar, with colour influenced by concentration.
  • Protein: use the Biuret test as instructed. A lilac or purple colour is positive; the reagent remains blue when protein is not detected.
  • Lipid: the ethanol emulsion test produces a cloudy white emulsion when lipid is present. Keep ethanol away from flames because it is flammable.
  • Vitamin C: DCPIP is decolourised by vitamin C. A comparison is meaningful only when sample volumes, reagent concentration and drop size are standardised.
  • Controls: use a known positive and negative sample when available. Use clean apparatus to prevent cross-contamination.
  • Recording: write the sample label, reagent, method, initial appearance, final observation and conclusion in separate columns.

A3. Transport, osmosis and diffusion investigations

  • Osmosis: movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from higher water potential to lower water potential.
  • Plant tissue method: cut equal-sized pieces, measure initial mass, place in measured solutions for equal times, blot consistently and measure final mass.
  • Calculation: percentage change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100%.
  • Interpretation: positive change means mass gain; negative change means mass loss. The zero-change point estimates the isotonic concentration under the conditions used.
  • Fair test: keep tissue type, dimensions, solution volume, temperature, immersion time and blotting method constant.
  • Diffusion: investigate how temperature or surface area affects diffusion using a safe approved model. Define the measured endpoint and keep gel or sample dimensions consistent.
  • Evaluation: use several repeats per concentration, a calibrated balance and a wider range of concentrations around the zero-change point.

A4. Enzymes, respiration and photosynthesis

  • Enzyme rate: choose a measurable endpoint such as gas volume per minute, time to a colour change or amount of product formed.
  • Temperature: use a water bath to maintain the test temperature. Allow solutions to reach the target temperature before mixing.
  • pH: use buffer solutions to maintain pH. Do not assume adding the same number of drops of acid creates equal pH values.
  • Concentration: change substrate concentration while keeping enzyme concentration, temperature, pH and total volume controlled.
  • Respiration: a respirometer can estimate oxygen uptake by suitable organisms or seeds. Use a control to account for temperature or pressure changes and follow the teacher's approved setup.
  • Photosynthesis: measure oxygen production or another valid proxy while changing one limiting factor. Control temperature, carbon dioxide availability, plant species and exposed leaf area.
  • Light intensity: if using lamp distance as a proxy, measure distance consistently and recognise that distance is not itself a direct measurement of light intensity.
  • Germination: compare conditions such as water, oxygen and temperature using equal numbers of viable seeds and a clearly defined germination criterion.

A5. Ecology, fieldwork and classification

  • Random quadrats: mark a study area, generate random coordinates, place the quadrat and record the target species without choosing convenient locations.
  • Systematic transect: stretch a tape across an environmental gradient and sample at fixed intervals. Record the distance and abiotic conditions at each point.
  • Abundance: count individuals where individuals can be distinguished. For plants that spread, percentage cover may be more appropriate.
  • Frequency: number of quadrats containing a species divided by total quadrats, multiplied by 100%.
  • Density: total individuals divided by total area sampled. State the area unit.
  • Population estimate: mean density multiplied by the total habitat area, with assumptions and limitations stated.
  • Classification: use observable characteristics and a branching key. Each decision should offer contrasting statements and lead to one group or organism.
  • Field ethics: minimise disturbance, do not remove protected organisms and return safe specimens to their habitat where appropriate.

A6. Human physiology and organism responses

  • Pulse rate: count beats for a fixed time at rest and after controlled activity. Use the same counting interval and recovery period.
  • Breathing rate: count breaths per minute using a consistent definition of one breath. Avoid asking participants to over-exert themselves.
  • Reaction time: use a ruler-drop test or digital method, repeat trials and calculate a mean. Control the starting position and participant instructions.
  • Exercise investigation: use an appropriate low-risk activity, obtain teacher approval, allow rest and stop if a participant feels unwell.
  • Plant responses: investigate phototropism or germination using matched seedlings, controlled conditions and repeated observations.
  • Ethics: obtain consent, protect privacy and avoid experiments that cause pain, injury or unsafe physiological stress.

B. Chemistry: additional practical topics and methods

B1. Apparatus, measurements and solution preparation

  • Balance: check zero, use a weighing boat, avoid placing chemicals directly on the pan and record mass to the instrument's justified precision.
  • Measuring cylinder: use for approximate liquid volumes. Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level for most aqueous liquids.
  • Volumetric pipette: transfers one accurate fixed volume. Use a pipette filler; never pipette by mouth.
  • Burette: delivers a variable accurate volume. Remove the funnel before titration, check the jet is filled and read the scale at eye level.
  • Volumetric flask: prepares a solution to a fixed volume. Dissolve the solute first, transfer quantitatively and make up to the calibration mark.
  • Concentration: concentration in mol dm⁻³ = amount in moles ÷ volume in dm³. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.
  • Mass concentration: mass concentration = mass of solute ÷ volume of solution, with units such as g dm⁻³.
  • Label: include chemical name, concentration, date and hazard information required by the laboratory.

B2. Titration: accurate technique and calculations

  • Rinse the burette with the solution it will contain and the pipette with the solution it will transfer.
  • Use a pipette filler and transfer the specified volume to a conical flask.
  • Add only the required indicator quantity. Place the flask on a white tile if it helps identify the endpoint.
  • Record initial and final burette readings to the appropriate precision; titre = final reading − initial reading.
  • Carry out a rough titration if instructed, then repeat carefully to obtain concordant titres.
  • Do not average every reading automatically. Follow the stated concordance tolerance and explain which titres are used.
  • Use the balanced chemical equation to determine the mole ratio. Calculate moles using n = cV, with V in dm³.
  • Use the mole ratio to calculate the unknown amount or concentration. Show units and significant figures.
  • Common errors include air bubbles in the jet, parallax, overshooting the endpoint, contaminated glassware and an unwashed flask containing residual reagent.

B3. Acids, bases, salts and pH

  • Use indicators or a calibrated pH meter to compare acidity and alkalinity.
  • Strong and weak acids differ in degree of ionisation; concentration and strength are not interchangeable terms.
  • Neutralisation involves reaction of hydrogen ions with hydroxide ions to form water.
  • Prepare a soluble salt by reacting an appropriate acid with an insoluble base or carbonate, then filter excess solid and crystallise the solution as instructed.
  • For an insoluble salt, precipitation can occur when two suitable aqueous solutions are mixed. Filter, wash and dry the precipitate using the approved method.
  • Crystallisation: concentrate the solution safely, allow crystals to form, separate them and dry between suitable filter papers or by the prescribed method.
  • Never heat a solution to dryness unless the specific procedure requires it and the teacher has approved it.

B4. Rates of reaction and energetics

  • Rate can be measured by gas volume produced, mass lost, colour change or time taken to reach a defined endpoint.
  • Change one factor at a time: concentration, temperature, surface area or catalyst presence.
  • Keep total volume, reactant amount, apparatus, mixing and measurement interval consistent.
  • For gas collection, check the apparatus for leaks and use a suitable gas syringe. Never use a sealed setup where pressure could rise dangerously.
  • Plot product volume against time. The gradient represents rate; an initial tangent estimates initial rate.
  • For mass-loss methods, use a suitable balance and explain why gas escaping causes mass to decrease.
  • For temperature-change experiments, use insulation, a lid and consistent stirring to reduce heat loss and uneven temperature.
  • State whether a temperature change is exothermic or endothermic from the system's perspective and support the claim with measured evidence.

B5. Separation and purification

  • Filtration: separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The residue remains on the filter paper; the filtrate passes through.
  • Evaporation: removes solvent to concentrate a solution. Use controlled heating and avoid splashing.
  • Crystallisation: forms crystals from a concentrated solution as it cools or evaporates slowly.
  • Simple distillation: separates a solvent from a solution or liquids with sufficiently different boiling points, collecting vapour after condensation.
  • Fractional distillation: separates miscible liquids with closer boiling points using a fractionating column.
  • Paper chromatography: separates components by different partitioning between stationary paper and moving solvent.
  • Chromatography method: draw a pencil baseline, apply a small spot, keep the spot above solvent, cover the container and mark the solvent front immediately after removal.
  • Rf: distance moved by component divided by distance moved by solvent front. Rf has no unit and depends on conditions.

B6. Gas tests, ions and chemical observations

  • Hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint under the approved test conditions.
  • Oxygen relights a glowing splint.
  • Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky/cloudy.
  • Ammonia turns damp red litmus paper blue.
  • Chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper; it is toxic and must only be tested using the teacher-approved microscale method and suitable ventilation.
  • Flame tests use a clean wire loop and an appropriate flame-test method. Contamination, especially sodium, can mask other colours.
  • For aqueous ions, use the specified reagent and record the precipitate colour and whether it dissolves in excess reagent. Do not identify an ion from colour alone if the test is not sufficiently selective.
  • Record exact observations before writing an inference. “White precipitate forms” is an observation; “the ion is…” is an inference requiring the complete test pattern.

C. Physics: additional practical topics and methods

C1. Measurement, motion and forces

  • Length: use a ruler for ordinary lengths, vernier calipers for external/internal diameters and micrometers for small thicknesses. Check zero error.
  • Time: time several cycles or repeated events and divide by the number to reduce reaction-time effects.
  • Distance and time: calculate speed = distance ÷ time. On a distance-time graph, gradient represents speed.
  • Velocity-time: gradient represents acceleration; area under the graph represents displacement for a signed velocity graph.
  • Acceleration: change in velocity divided by time taken. State direction where relevant.
  • Force: use a calibrated spring balance or force sensor within its range. Avoid jerking the instrument.
  • Friction: identify the surface materials, normal contact force and pulling method; repeat measurements and compare like conditions.
  • Moments: balance a metre rule on a pivot and use known weights at measured distances. Moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot.
  • Density: measure mass and volume. For a regular solid calculate volume from dimensions; for an irregular insoluble object use displacement.

C2. Hooke's law, springs and pressure

  • Measure the original spring length and add known loads in safe steps.
  • Allow oscillations to settle before reading the loaded length.
  • Calculate extension = loaded length − original length.
  • Plot force against extension. In the proportional region, the gradient is the spring constant when extension is in metres.
  • Do not exceed the safe load or continue after permanent deformation.
  • Pressure = force ÷ area. Use consistent SI units to obtain pascals.
  • For liquids, pressure increases with depth and depends on density and gravitational field strength.
  • Secure masses and stands; keep feet and hands clear of falling loads.

C3. Thermal physics

  • Use a thermometer or temperature probe with a suitable range and resolution.
  • Record the starting temperature before heating or cooling and use equal time intervals.
  • Specific heat capacity investigations require mass, energy supplied and temperature change; reduce heat loss with insulation and a lid.
  • Energy supplied electrically can be calculated using E = VIt when voltage and current are measured over a known time under suitable conditions.
  • Compare insulation by using identical containers, equal volumes, equal starting temperatures and the same measurement intervals.
  • Use tongs or heatproof gloves where instructed; do not touch hot apparatus directly.
  • For cooling curves, keep the probe position consistent and plot temperature against time.
  • Explain conduction, convection and radiation separately and identify which transfer mechanism is being investigated.

C4. Waves, sound and light

  • Wave speed = frequency × wavelength. Measure several wavelengths across a wave pattern and divide to reduce uncertainty.
  • In a ripple tank, vary one factor at a time and keep the dipper frequency constant when comparing depth or gap effects.
  • Sound experiments should use safe sound levels and a clear measurement method. Echo timing over a known distance can estimate speed of sound.
  • For reflection, measure angles from the normal, not from the mirror surface.
  • For refraction, trace the incident and refracted rays, draw the normal and measure angles consistently with a protractor.
  • For a converging lens, record object distance and image distance only when a clear real image is formed on the screen. Measure from the optical centre as directed.
  • Use ray boxes at low voltage and avoid looking directly into intense light sources.

C5. Electricity and magnetism

  • Connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel across the component.
  • Check polarity and meter range before switching on. Begin with a low supply setting.
  • Use a variable resistor to obtain a range of readings and record current and potential difference with units.
  • Plot current against potential difference or use the axes requested. For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is proportional to potential difference.
  • Resistance = potential difference ÷ current. State the unit ohm (Ω).
  • Heating can change resistance. Use appropriate current limits and switch off between readings if required.
  • For series circuits, current is the same through each component and potential differences add. For parallel branches, potential difference is the same across each branch.
  • Investigate magnetic fields using a plotting compass or iron filings only as instructed; keep filings away from eyes and electronic equipment.
  • Electromagnetic induction depends on changing magnetic flux. Compare direction and size of induced voltage while changing one factor, such as magnet speed or coil turns.
  • Use low-voltage supplies for school investigations and keep liquids away from electrical connections.

D. Data handling, graphs and scientific communication

D1. Tables and recording

  • Write a clear title that identifies the variables being investigated.
  • Put the independent variable first and dependent variable next where practical.
  • Include quantity and unit in each heading, for example time / s or temperature / °C.
  • Keep decimal places consistent with the measuring instrument.
  • Record raw readings before calculated values.
  • Include repeat readings, means and any rejected anomalous result with an explanation.
  • Do not alter readings to make a graph look straight.

D2. Graphs and calculations

  • Use a line graph for continuous variables and a bar chart for discrete categories.
  • Use a histogram for continuous grouped data; bars touch because intervals are continuous.
  • Choose a scale that uses most of the graph area and is easy to read.
  • Label both axes with quantity and unit.
  • Plot points accurately and draw a best-fit line or smooth curve as appropriate.
  • Calculate a gradient using two widely separated points on the best-fit line, not two adjacent raw points.
  • Show a large gradient triangle and state the gradient unit.
  • Use the graph to describe trend, proportionality, maximum/minimum and anomalies.
  • Do not claim causation from a pattern alone unless the experimental design supports it.

D3. Reliability, accuracy, precision and validity

  • Accuracy: closeness to an accepted or true value.
  • Precision: fineness of measurement or closeness of repeated values, depending on context.
  • Reliability: consistency of results when the method is repeated.
  • Validity: whether the method tests the intended question and controls relevant variables.
  • Random error: unpredictable variation; reduce its effect by repeats and averaging.
  • Systematic error: consistent bias, such as zero error; identify and correct the cause rather than relying on repeats.
  • Resolution: smallest scale interval or change an instrument can display.
  • Parallax: apparent shift caused by viewing a scale from an angle; read at eye level.
  • Anomaly: a result that does not fit the overall pattern; investigate before excluding it.

E. Laboratory safety and responsible practical work

  • Wear eye protection when chemicals, glassware or heating create a risk. Tie back long hair and secure loose clothing.
  • Read labels before use. Never taste chemicals or directly smell unknown substances.
  • Use a pipette filler; never pipette by mouth.
  • Keep flammable solvents away from flames and ignition sources.
  • Use tongs or heatproof equipment for hot apparatus. Allow glassware to cool before handling.
  • Do not point a heated test tube at anyone. Heat gently and follow the teacher's direction.
  • Handle sharp tools on a dissecting tray and cut away from the body.
  • Use only approved biological samples and authorised microbiological procedures. Do not culture unknown environmental or body samples outside supervised protocols.
  • Keep electrical equipment and hands dry. Switch off before altering a circuit.
  • Dispose of chemicals, broken glass and biological materials as instructed. Never pour substances down a drain unless authorised.
  • Report spills, breakages, burns, cuts and exposures immediately. Do not attempt an unfamiliar clean-up.
  • Use the local risk assessment and safety data for the actual substances and concentrations; generic notes do not replace school rules.

Further Practical Topic Coverage: Extended Investigation Notes

This extension brings together additional practical themes found across secondary Biology, Chemistry and Physics courses. The exact depth and required experiments depend on the local syllabus and examination level. Treat the procedures as planning guides and use only the school-approved method, apparatus and risk assessment.

1. Biology practical extension

1.1 Respiration and gas exchange

  • Respiration releases energy from organic molecules. Practical investigations may compare respiration rates under different safe conditions using an approved respirometer or indicator system.
  • For a respirometer, the movement of a marker can indicate a change in gas volume when oxygen is taken up and carbon dioxide is absorbed by the approved absorbent. The apparatus must be airtight and the absorbent must be handled only under supervision.
  • Use a control setup to account for temperature and pressure changes that are not caused by respiration. Keep organism mass, activity state, temperature and measurement time consistent.
  • Record marker position at equal time intervals. Calculate rate as change in marker position or volume divided by time, using the correct units.
  • Explain why a control is necessary: gas volume can change with temperature even when no respiration occurs.
  • For gas exchange models, distinguish diffusion distance, surface area, concentration gradient and ventilation. A model demonstrates a principle but may not reproduce all features of a living organ.

1.2 Transpiration and water uptake

  • A potometer estimates water uptake by a cut shoot. Water uptake is not identical to water loss because some water is used in the plant.
  • Use a healthy leafy shoot and assemble the apparatus as demonstrated. Make the system watertight and introduce an air bubble in the capillary only by the approved procedure.
  • Measure bubble movement over a known time. Repeat readings and calculate a mean rate of water uptake.
  • Investigate one factor such as air movement, light intensity or temperature while keeping shoot size, leaf area and other conditions controlled.
  • Use a fan only at a safe distance and avoid excessive heating or drying of the plant. Record environmental conditions throughout.
  • Reduce uncertainty by measuring capillary diameter, using a scale, allowing the shoot to acclimatise and repeating the measurement.

1.3 Plant pigments and chromatography

  • Leaf pigments can be separated by chromatography using a suitable solvent under teacher supervision.
  • Use a small amount of leaf extract and apply a concentrated spot to a pencil baseline. Keep the spot above the solvent level.
  • Cover the container and allow the solvent to move. Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top and mark the solvent front immediately.
  • Measure the distance travelled by each pigment and the solvent front from the same baseline. Calculate Rf values.
  • Compare pigment patterns only when the paper, solvent, temperature and method are comparable. Do not identify a pigment from colour alone if confirmatory evidence is required.

1.4 Variation, inheritance and selection

  • Measure a continuous characteristic such as leaf length or seed mass using a consistent instrument and sampling rule.
  • Record a sufficiently large sample and display the distribution using a suitable frequency table, histogram or box plot as required by the course.
  • Distinguish continuous variation, which often shows a range, from discontinuous variation, which falls into distinct categories.
  • When comparing groups, use the same measuring technique and avoid selecting only unusual specimens.
  • Do not infer genetic causes from a simple measurement alone. Environmental factors may contribute to observed differences.
  • For population studies, record sample size, sampling method, location and date so that the investigation can be repeated.

1.5 Microbiology and aseptic technique

  • Microbiology practical work must use approved organisms, sealed materials and the institution's authorised safety protocol.
  • Do not culture unknown microorganisms from skin, food, soil, water or surfaces as an unsupervised activity.
  • Use sterile prepared materials where provided, keep containers closed, minimise exposure and disinfect work areas as directed.
  • Never open or sniff a culture plate. Dispose of cultures through the approved decontamination route.
  • When recording prepared plate results, use a labelled diagram or table and describe colony appearance without assuming species identity from appearance alone.

1.6 Human biology measurements

  • For pulse and breathing rate, define the counting period and use the same method for every participant.
  • Allow resting measurements before exercise and use a standardised, low-risk activity approved by the teacher.
  • Measure recovery at fixed intervals and present the results in a table or line graph.
  • Use volunteers only with consent, protect personal information and stop if anyone feels unwell.
  • State limitations such as participant variation, counting errors, fitness differences and environmental conditions.

2. Chemistry practical extension

2.1 Electrolysis

  • Electrolysis uses electrical energy to drive a chemical change in an electrolyte. The electrolyte contains mobile ions.
  • Identify the cathode as the negative electrode in an electrolytic cell and the anode as the positive electrode. Reduction occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode.
  • Use the specified low-voltage supply, electrodes and electrolyte. Check polarity before switching on and do not touch electrodes while energised.
  • Record changes at each electrode separately: gas bubbles, colour changes, metal deposits or electrode mass changes.
  • Where gas collection is authorised, use suitable small-scale apparatus and the teacher's safety method. Never ignite an unidentified gas.
  • Explain product formation using the ions present, their relative ease of discharge and the electrode material, as required by the syllabus.

2.2 Reactivity of metals

  • Compare metals using teacher-approved reactions with water, dilute acid or salt solutions. Use small quantities and appropriate concentrations.
  • Keep the mass or surface area of metal, volume and concentration of solution and temperature as consistent as possible.
  • Record the time taken for a defined observation, gas volume where suitable, temperature change or visible displacement.
  • Do not compare the vigour of reactions using different-sized pieces without accounting for surface area.
  • Use a results table with metal identity, conditions, observation, measured outcome and inference.
  • Explain displacement in terms of relative reactivity: a more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its compound under suitable conditions.

2.3 Precipitation and ionic tests

  • Mix small measured volumes of suitable solutions and record whether a precipitate forms, its colour and whether it dissolves in excess reagent where the method specifies.
  • Use clean droppers and separate wells or test tubes to prevent contamination.
  • Write observations first, then use the full test pattern to infer the possible ion.
  • For gravimetric work where included in a course, filter, wash, dry and weigh a precipitate using the prescribed method. Drying must be to a consistent endpoint.
  • Calculate percentage yield only when the theoretical yield can be determined from a balanced equation and the limiting reactant is known.

2.4 Energetics and calorimetry

  • Measure a starting temperature, carry out the specified reaction or heating process, stir consistently and record the maximum or minimum temperature reached.
  • Use the same volumes, concentrations, starting temperatures and apparatus for comparisons.
  • Reduce heat exchange with insulation and a lid. Keep the thermometer or probe in the liquid without touching the container base.
  • For a simple solution calorimetry calculation, energy change can be estimated using q = mcΔT when the mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change are known.
  • State assumptions, such as the solution having a specific heat capacity similar to water, and explain that heat loss makes the measured temperature change smaller than the ideal value.
  • Do not handle hot containers directly. Use a heatproof mat and suitable tongs where required.

2.5 Organic chemistry observations

  • Organic practicals may involve comparing physical properties, solubility, acidity or reaction observations using small quantities and approved reagents.
  • Keep ignition sources away from flammable organic liquids. Use a fume cupboard where the teacher's risk assessment requires it.
  • Record the exact conditions and observation. Avoid identifying an unknown organic substance from smell; do not directly smell chemicals.
  • Where a course includes tests for unsaturation, use only the specified reagent and safe microscale procedure and record the colour change.
  • When comparing homologous series, link observed trends to molecular structure and intermolecular forces without claiming that one observation proves an entire structure.

2.6 Purity and melting point

  • A pure solid generally melts over a narrow temperature range; impurities often broaden and may lower the melting range.
  • Use a suitable melting-point apparatus under supervision and follow its sample-loading and heating instructions.
  • Heat at a controlled rate near the expected melting point and record the temperature at which melting begins and ends.
  • Repeat measurements and compare the range with a reliable reference value where available.
  • Possible errors include heating too quickly, a wet sample, poor thermometer calibration or an unevenly packed sample.

3. Physics practical extension

3.1 Moments and equilibrium

  • Balance a metre rule on a pivot and place known loads at measured distances from the pivot.
  • Measure perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of each force.
  • Moment = force × perpendicular distance. Use newtons and metres to obtain N m.
  • For equilibrium, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment when the system is balanced and other conditions are satisfied.
  • Repeat with different load positions and compare calculated moments. Account for the rule's own weight if it is not balanced at its centre of mass.
  • Secure the pivot and loads. Keep feet clear of suspended masses.

3.2 Pressure in liquids

  • Use a pressure sensor or suitable manometer arrangement approved by the teacher to investigate pressure at different depths.
  • Keep the liquid type and temperature constant while changing depth, and measure depth from the liquid surface to the sensor position.
  • Record pressure and depth in a table and plot pressure against depth.
  • Explain the trend using the weight of liquid above a point. Pressure increases with depth and liquid density.
  • Check that the sensor is zeroed and that trapped air or leaks do not affect the reading.

3.3 Lenses and image formation

  • Use an illuminated object, converging lens and screen on an optical bench. Align the centres at the same height.
  • Move the screen until a sharp real image is formed. Measure object and image distances from the lens reference point as specified.
  • Repeat at several object distances and record whether the image is real, inverted and magnified or diminished.
  • Use the thin-lens relationship only when required by the course and define the sign convention used.
  • Reduce uncertainty by measuring from the lens centre, aligning components and repeating the focus measurement.

3.4 Electromagnetic induction

  • Connect a coil to a sensitive meter or data logger and move a bar magnet relative to the coil using a controlled method.
  • Observe the direction of deflection when the magnet enters and leaves the coil. Reverse the magnet pole and compare the direction.
  • Change one factor, such as speed of movement or number of turns, while keeping the other conditions constant.
  • Explain that an induced voltage is produced when magnetic flux linkage changes. A stationary magnet relative to the coil produces no sustained induced voltage.
  • Use suitable low-voltage measuring equipment and keep strong magnets away from sensitive devices.

3.5 Transformers and alternating current

  • Use only the low-voltage educational transformer apparatus supplied for the experiment; never connect a school investigation directly to mains electricity.
  • Measure primary and secondary voltage using suitable meters and compare results for different turns ratios.
  • For an ideal transformer, voltage ratio is related to turns ratio. Real transformers have energy losses due to heating, resistance and magnetic effects.
  • Record the turns, input voltage, output voltage and any load condition. State whether the transformer steps voltage up or down.

3.6 Radioactivity and radiation measurements

  • Radioactive source practicals are teacher-controlled demonstrations using approved sealed sources and the institution's radiation safety procedure.
  • Learners may analyse supplied count-rate data or observe a demonstration; they should not handle or improvise sources.
  • Record background count over a stated time and subtract or account for background when comparing measurements.
  • Use equal counting intervals and repeat readings because radioactive decay is random.
  • Explain that count rate depends on source activity, distance, shielding, detector efficiency and background radiation.
  • Never bring a source close to the body, remove it from its holder or attempt to open a source container.

4. Practical method writing: a complete planning template

  1. Aim: state what relationship or effect is being investigated.
  2. Prediction: state the expected pattern and scientific reason.
  3. Variables: identify the independent and dependent variables and list the controls.
  4. Apparatus: name equipment and give ranges or sizes where relevant.
  5. Diagram: draw a clear setup with labels, connections and measurement points.
  6. Method: write numbered steps in chronological order, including quantities, timing and how readings are taken.
  7. Safety: identify hazards, possible harm and specific control measures.
  8. Results: prepare a table with headings and units before collecting data.
  9. Processing: state the calculations, graph or drawing required.
  10. Conclusion: describe the pattern and support it with numerical evidence.
  11. Evaluation: identify limitations and give improvements that directly address them.

5. Common examination errors and how to avoid them

  • Do not confuse the independent variable with the dependent variable. Ask which factor is deliberately changed and which outcome is measured.
  • Do not write “keep everything constant.” Name the actual control variables and state how each is kept constant.
  • Do not write “be careful” as a safety measure. Name the hazard and the action that reduces the risk.
  • Do not omit units from table headings, calculations or graph axes.
  • Do not use a measuring instrument with a range that is too small or divisions too coarse for the measurement.
  • Do not confuse a titre with a burette reading; titre is the difference between final and initial readings.
  • Do not confuse mass with weight, or heat with temperature.
  • Do not claim a result is accurate merely because it is repeated. Repeats mainly help assess consistency and random variation.
  • Do not draw a graph by joining every point dot-to-dot when a best-fit line or curve is required.
  • Do not report an inference as an observation. State what was seen or measured first.
  • Do not remove an anomalous reading without explaining the decision and checking for a procedural cause.
  • Do not propose vague improvements. Link the improvement to a named limitation and explain how it reduces the problem.

Practical Investigation Skills: Recording, Processing, Analysing and Presenting Data

Every investigation should produce a traceable record. A good practical answer shows what was measured, how it was measured, the units, how the readings were processed and what the evidence means.

  • Put the independent variable in the first column and the dependent variable in the next column.
  • Write the quantity and unit in every heading, for example Time / s, Current / A, Temperature / °C or Length / cm.

  • Use consistent decimal places that match the measuring instrument's resolution.
  • Record raw readings immediately. Do not alter a reading to make it fit the expected pattern.
  • Repeat measurements where practical, then calculate a mean. Keep raw readings visible alongside the mean.
  • Identify an anomalous value and investigate its cause; do not silently delete it.

2. Processing and analysing results

  • Mean: add repeat readings and divide by the number of readings.
  • Range: highest reading minus lowest reading; this gives a simple indication of spread.
  • Rate: change in measured quantity divided by elapsed time.
  • Gradient: change in vertical-axis quantity divided by change in horizontal-axis quantity.
  • Percentage error: absolute uncertainty divided by measured value, multiplied by 100%, where the uncertainty is known.
  • Percentage change: (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100%.
  • Compare the pattern with the prediction. Quote at least two relevant values from the table or graph.
  • Separate observation from explanation: first state the trend, then explain it using scientific principles.
  • Discuss reliability, accuracy, precision, validity and limitations as distinct ideas.

3. Drawing and interpreting graphs

  • Use a sharp pencil, ruler and a sensible scale that uses most of the graph grid.
  • Plot the independent variable on the horizontal x-axis and the dependent variable on the vertical y-axis, unless the question specifies otherwise.
  • Label both axes with the quantity and unit.
  • Choose a uniform scale, such as 1 large square = 2 units. Avoid awkward scales when a simple scale is possible.
  • Plot each point accurately with a small cross or dot in a circle.
  • Draw a smooth curve or a straight best-fit line. Do not join points dot-to-dot unless instructed.
  • Circle any anomalous point only if it is justified and explain its treatment.
  • Calculate a gradient using a large triangle on the best-fit line, not two points close together.
  • State the meaning and unit of the gradient. For a distance-time graph, gradient represents speed; for a voltage-current graph, gradient may represent resistance depending on the axes.

  • Conclusion: answer the aim directly and state the relationship observed.

5. Stopwatch measurements: oscillations of a pendulum

Aim: investigate the relationship between pendulum length and period.

  1. Set up a small-angle pendulum using a secure support and a suitable bob.
  2. Measure length from the point of suspension to the centre of the bob.
  3. Displace the bob through a small angle and release without pushing it.
  4. Use a stopwatch to time 10 or 20 complete oscillations, starting and stopping as the bob passes a fixed reference point in the same direction.
  5. Repeat at least three times for each length and calculate the mean time.
  6. Period T = mean time for N oscillations ÷ N. Calculate T² if testing T² against length.
  7. Change only the length between sets. Keep the bob and release angle approximately constant.
  8. Plot T² against length. A straight-line relationship supports T² proportional to length for small oscillations.

A. Biology practicals: examination questions and answers

1. Microscopy, cells and biological drawings

Q1. Describe how to prepare and examine an onion epidermal peel under a light microscope. (6 marks)

Model answer: Peel a thin transparent epidermis using forceps; place it flat in a drop of water on a clean slide; add a suitable stain such as iodine as instructed; lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles; begin with the low-power objective; use the coarse focus then fine focus; centre the specimen and move to higher power, using fine focus only. Draw a large, clear, single-line biological drawing and label cell wall, cytoplasm, nucleus (if visible) and vacuole.

Q2. State the functions of the eyepiece lens, objective lens, stage, diaphragm, mirror/light source, coarse and fine adjustment knobs. (7 marks)

Eyepiece magnifies the image; objective provides primary magnification; stage supports the slide; diaphragm controls light; mirror/lamp directs light through the specimen; coarse adjustment makes large focusing movements at low power; fine adjustment sharpens the image.

Q3. How do you prepare a cheek-cell slide, and how does it differ from an onion cell? (5 marks)

Use a clean sterile swab/toothpick as directed to collect cells from the inside of the cheek; smear in a drop of saline/water; add methylene blue stain if prescribed; apply coverslip and observe at low then higher power. Cheek cells are animal cells and lack a cell wall and large permanent central vacuole; onion epidermal cells have a cellulose cell wall and typically a large vacuole.

Q4. A drawing is 40 mm long and the actual specimen is 0.20 mm. Calculate magnification. (2 marks)

Magnification = image size ÷ actual size = 40 ÷ 0.20 = ×200. Both measurements must be in the same units.

Q5. Give four rules for a biological drawing. (4 marks)

Use a sharp pencil; make large clear outlines with single unbroken lines; do not shade or sketch; use straight label lines that do not cross; include a title and magnification/scale where required.

2. Food tests and nutrition

Q6. Describe tests for starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid, giving positive observations. (8 marks)

Starch: add iodine; yellow-brown to blue-black. Reducing sugar: add Benedict's solution and heat in a hot-water bath; blue may change through green/yellow/orange to brick-red precipitate. Protein: Biuret test; blue to lilac/purple. Lipid: ethanol emulsion test; after the approved mixing and addition of water, a milky-white emulsion forms. Use separate droppers and clean test tubes.

Q7. How do you test a sample for non-reducing sugar? (4 marks)

First test a fresh portion with Benedict's to check for reducing sugar. For a separate portion, boil with dilute acid as instructed to hydrolyse non-reducing sugar; cool; neutralise carefully with the prescribed alkali; then add Benedict's and heat in a water bath. A positive colour/precipitate after hydrolysis, when the initial test was negative, indicates non-reducing sugar.

Q8. Why is a control sample useful in food tests? (2 marks)

It provides a known positive or negative comparison, helping show that the reagent and procedure are working and that the observation is due to the tested food constituent.

3. Transport in plants: support, movement and responses

Phototropism in a shoot

Q9. Define tropism and distinguish phototropism from geotropism. (4 marks)

A tropism is a directional growth response of a plant to a stimulus. Phototropism is growth in response to light; geotropism (gravitropism) is growth in response to gravity. Shoots are usually positively phototropic and negatively gravitropic; roots are usually positively gravitropic.

Q10. Describe an experiment to demonstrate phototropism in a young shoot. (6 marks)

Place similar young seedlings in a box with a small opening on one side; keep water, temperature and seedling age similar; allow light to enter from one direction; keep a control under uniform light if possible; observe and record shoot direction daily. The shoot bends towards the light. Repeat with several seedlings and measure bending angle for quantitative comparison.

Q11. Explain how auxin causes a shoot to bend towards light. (5 marks)

Auxin is redistributed towards the shaded side of the shoot; in shoots, higher auxin concentration stimulates cell elongation; cells on the shaded side elongate more; unequal growth causes the shoot to curve towards the light.

Q12. Describe positive and negative geotropism in roots and shoots. (4 marks)

Roots grow towards gravity (positive geotropism), helping anchorage and access to water/minerals. Shoots grow away from gravity (negative geotropism), helping leaves reach light.

Q13. What is hydrotropism? Suggest an experiment. (4 marks)

Hydrotropism is directional plant growth in response to water/moisture. Grow seedlings in a divided container with a moisture gradient while controlling light and gravity as far as possible; record root direction. Roots tend to grow towards the moister region.

Q14. Explain the role of roots, stems and leaves in support. (6 marks)

Roots anchor the plant and absorb water/mineral salts; stems support leaves, flowers and fruits and contain strengthening tissues such as xylem with lignified walls; leaf veins provide internal support and distribute water and assimilates; turgid cells help support soft herbaceous tissues; collenchyma and sclerenchyma provide mechanical strength.

Q15. Describe an investigation of water uptake using a potometer. Why is it not a direct measure of transpiration? (5 marks)

Cut a leafy shoot under water, fit it into a water-filled airtight potometer, introduce a small air bubble into the capillary, and record bubble movement over a measured time. Change one factor such as light intensity while controlling temperature, humidity and air movement. Potometer measures water uptake; some absorbed water is used or retained by the plant, so uptake only estimates transpiration.

Q16. State how light, temperature, wind and humidity affect transpiration. (4 marks)

Higher light often opens stomata and increases transpiration; higher temperature increases evaporation and diffusion; moving air removes humid air around the leaf and usually increases transpiration; high humidity reduces the water-vapour gradient and decreases transpiration.

Q17. Explain the roles of xylem and phloem. (4 marks)

Xylem transports water and mineral ions mainly from roots towards leaves and also provides support through lignified walls. Phloem translocates dissolved organic substances, especially sucrose and amino acids, between sources and sinks; movement can occur in different directions in different sieve tubes.

4. Movement and support in animals

Antagonistic muscles at the elbow (simplified)

Q18. Explain how the skeleton supports and protects the body. (5 marks)

The skeleton supports the body and gives it shape; protects organs (skull protects brain, ribs protect heart and lungs, vertebrae protect spinal cord); provides attachment for muscles; enables movement at joints; and bone marrow produces blood cells in appropriate bones.

Q19. Explain how the biceps and triceps bring about bending and straightening of the elbow. (6 marks)

The muscles work as an antagonistic pair because muscles pull when they contract. To bend the elbow, biceps contracts and triceps relaxes; the forearm is pulled upwards. To straighten, triceps contracts and biceps relaxes; the forearm extends. Tendons attach muscles to bones and the elbow acts as a hinge joint.

Q20. Distinguish a hinge joint from a ball-and-socket joint, giving examples. (4 marks)

A hinge joint mainly permits movement in one plane, e.g. elbow or knee. A ball-and-socket joint permits movement in many directions and rotation, e.g. shoulder or hip. Cartilage reduces friction and synovial fluid lubricates synovial joints.

Q21. State the functions of cartilage, ligaments, tendons and synovial fluid. (4 marks)

Cartilage cushions bone ends and reduces friction; ligaments connect bone to bone and stabilise joints; tendons connect muscle to bone; synovial fluid lubricates the joint and reduces friction.

Q22. Compare movement in an earthworm, fish and bird. (6 marks)

Earthworm uses circular and longitudinal muscles acting against a hydrostatic skeleton, with setae for grip. Fish use body muscles in alternating contractions and the tail fin for propulsion; fins steer and stabilise. Birds use powerful flight muscles attached to a lightweight skeleton; wings generate lift and thrust, while tail feathers aid steering.

Q23. Describe an investigation of pulse rate before and after exercise. (5 marks)

Rest the participant, measure pulse for a fixed interval, perform a standardised safe exercise, then measure pulse immediately and at fixed recovery intervals. Repeat with consent and suitable supervision. Keep exercise duration/intensity and measurement method consistent. Plot pulse rate against recovery time. Pulse rises during exercise to deliver more oxygen and nutrients and remove carbon dioxide.

Q24. Describe the role of the skeleton and muscles in locomotion. (5 marks)

Bones act as levers; joints act as pivots; muscles attach to bones by tendons; contracting muscles pull bones; antagonistic pairs reverse the movement. The nervous system coordinates muscle contraction and balance.

5. Osmosis, diffusion, photosynthesis, enzymes and respiration

Q25. Describe an osmosis experiment using potato cylinders. (6 marks)

Cut equal-sized cylinders, blot and measure initial mass/length; place them in labelled solutions of different concentrations for equal times; keep temperature and cylinder dimensions constant; remove, blot consistently and measure final mass; calculate percentage change in mass = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. Plot percentage mass change against solution concentration. A solution causing no net mass change is approximately isotonic to the cell sap.

Q26. Define diffusion and osmosis. (4 marks)

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration down a concentration gradient. Osmosis is net movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane from higher water potential (dilute solution) to lower water potential (more concentrated solution).

Q27. Describe how to investigate photosynthesis using an aquatic plant. (6 marks)

Place a freshly cut aquatic plant in water containing a suitable carbon dioxide source as instructed; expose it to a measured light distance; allow acclimatisation; count oxygen bubbles per minute or collect gas volume for a fixed time; repeat at different light intensities while controlling temperature and carbon dioxide availability. Plot rate against light intensity. Bubble count is an estimate because bubble sizes may vary.

Q28. Describe the starch test on a leaf and explain each major step. (6 marks)

Boil the leaf in water to kill cells and stop reactions; heat it in ethanol in a hot-water bath to remove chlorophyll; rinse in warm water to soften; place on a white tile and add iodine. Blue-black indicates starch. Ethanol is flammable and must never be heated directly over a flame.

Q29. How can you investigate the effect of temperature or pH on catalase? (6 marks)

Use equal volumes/concentrations of catalase source and hydrogen peroxide; set the chosen temperature using water baths or pH using buffer solutions; start timing consistently; measure oxygen volume in a fixed time or time to a defined endpoint; repeat and calculate mean rate. Keep substrate concentration, enzyme volume, pH (when testing temperature), temperature (when testing pH) and total volume controlled.

Q30. Describe a respirometer experiment using germinating seeds. (5 marks)

Place germinating seeds in a sealed respirometer with carbon dioxide absorbent separated from seeds by gauze; set up a control with glass beads or non-respiring material of equal volume; allow temperature to stabilise; record movement of the coloured fluid in the capillary over time. Oxygen uptake lowers gas volume/pressure when carbon dioxide is absorbed. Keep temperature constant and ensure airtight seals.

6. Ecology, variation and human biology

Q31. Describe random quadrat sampling to estimate plant abundance. (6 marks)

Mark out the study area; use random coordinates to place a quadrat without choosing favourable locations; identify and count the target species in each quadrat; repeat many times; calculate mean number per quadrat and estimate total population using area ratios. Use the same quadrat size and clear counting rules.

Q32. Distinguish frequency, density and percentage cover. (3 marks)

Frequency is the percentage of quadrats in which a species occurs; density is number of individuals per unit area; percentage cover is the proportion of sampled ground area covered by the species.

Q33. How would you use a transect to investigate distribution along an environmental gradient? (5 marks)

Lay a tape across the gradient; record species at fixed intervals using a line transect or place quadrats at regular intervals for a belt transect; measure relevant abiotic factors; repeat transects; plot abundance against distance or environmental factor and identify patterns.

Q34. Distinguish continuous and discontinuous variation. (3 marks)

Continuous variation has a range of values with intermediates, e.g. leaf length or human height, often influenced by many genes and environment. Discontinuous variation falls into distinct categories with no intermediates, e.g. a simple inherited characteristic such as blood group.

Q35. Explain why pulse and breathing rates increase during exercise. (4 marks)

Working muscles need more ATP; aerobic respiration requires more oxygen and glucose and produces more carbon dioxide; heart rate and ventilation increase to deliver oxygen/glucose and remove carbon dioxide. Rates gradually return towards resting levels during recovery.

B. Chemistry practicals: examination questions and answers

1. Apparatus, solutions, acids and titration

Q36. Explain how to prepare a standard solution from a soluble solid. (7 marks)

Accurately weigh the required mass; dissolve it in a small volume of deionised water in a beaker; transfer quantitatively through a funnel into a volumetric flask; rinse beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask; add water to near the calibration mark; use a dropper to bring the bottom of the meniscus to the mark at eye level; stopper and invert several times to mix. Calculate concentration using moles = mass ÷ molar mass and concentration = moles ÷ volume in dm³.

Q37. Describe an acid–alkali titration and how to obtain a reliable titre. (7 marks)

Rinse burette with titrant and pipette with solution to be measured; use a pipette filler to transfer a fixed aliquot to a conical flask; add a few drops of suitable indicator; record initial burette reading; add titrant while swirling and approach endpoint dropwise; record final reading; titre = final − initial; repeat until concordant titres are obtained and calculate their mean. Read burette at eye level.

Q38. A 25.0 cm³ sample of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ monoprotic acid is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ alkali. Calculate alkali concentration for a 1:1 reaction. (4 marks)

Moles acid = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. 1:1 ratio means alkali moles = 0.00250 mol. Alkali volume = 0.0200 dm³. Concentration = 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = 0.125 mol dm⁻³.

Q39. Why should a burette be rinsed with the solution it will contain, and why should the funnel be removed before titration? (3 marks)

Rinsing prevents residual water diluting the titrant. Removing the funnel prevents extra drops entering the burette after the initial reading and changing the delivered volume.

3. Separation, purification and chromatography

Q43. Choose suitable methods to separate sand and salt, ethanol and water, and dyes in ink. Explain. (6 marks)

Sand and salt: dissolve salt in water, filter off sand, evaporate/crystallise salt solution. Ethanol and water: fractional distillation because they are miscible liquids with different boiling points. Ink dyes: paper chromatography because components partition differently between stationary paper and moving solvent.

Q44. In chromatography a spot travels 4.5 cm and solvent front 7.5 cm. Calculate Rf. (2 marks)

Rf = 4.5 ÷ 7.5 = 0.60. Rf has no unit and is measured from the baseline to the centre of the spot.

Q45. State four precautions in paper chromatography. (4 marks)

Draw baseline in pencil; keep solvent below baseline; apply a small concentrated spot and let it dry; cover the chamber; do not touch the paper in the separation area; mark solvent front immediately after removal.

4. Organic chemistry practical questions

Q46. How can an alkene be distinguished from an alkane using bromine water? (4 marks)

Add equal small amounts of bromine water to separate labelled samples and mix as directed. An alkene decolourises bromine water because bromine adds across the C=C bond; an alkane shows no rapid decolourisation under the same conditions in the dark. Use a fume cupboard or approved ventilation and microscale quantities.

Q47. Describe an approved test for a carboxylic acid. (3 marks)

Use blue litmus, which turns red, or add a small amount of carbonate/hydrogencarbonate: effervescence occurs and the gas is carbon dioxide, confirmed with limewater. Acidic behaviour alone is not a unique identification.

Q48. How may aldehydes be distinguished from ketones? (4 marks)

Use a syllabus-approved aldehyde reagent such as Tollens' or Fehling's. Aldehydes give a silver mirror/grey silver deposit with freshly prepared Tollens' reagent or brick-red Cu₂O precipitate with Fehling's under prescribed conditions; simple ketones generally do not. Tollens' reagent must be freshly prepared and residues disposed of immediately by the teacher; never store it.

Q49. Describe esterification and state two safety precautions. (4 marks)

A carboxylic acid reacts reversibly with an alcohol, often using an acid catalyst and controlled warming, to form an ester and water. Use teacher-specified microscale quantities; wear goggles; keep flammable liquids away from flames; heat with an approved water bath; never smell directly.

Q50. How do you calculate Rf for separated organic components? (2 marks)

Measure distance from baseline to centre of spot and distance from baseline to solvent front. Rf = distance moved by component ÷ distance moved by solvent front.

5. Rates, thermal chemistry and physical chemistry

Q51. Describe how to investigate the effect of concentration on reaction rate using gas production. (6 marks)

Use a fixed volume of each concentration and the same mass/surface area of solid reactant; keep temperature and total volume controlled; connect a gas syringe before starting if safe and appropriate; mix and start timer consistently; record gas volume at equal time intervals; repeat; plot gas volume against time. Initial gradient estimates initial rate.

Q52. Explain how temperature affects rate of reaction. (4 marks)

At higher temperature particles have greater average kinetic energy; they move faster and collide more frequently; a greater fraction of collisions have energy equal to or greater than activation energy; successful collisions per second increase, so rate increases.

Q53. Describe a simple calorimetry experiment and calculate heat energy. (6 marks)

Measure known volumes and initial temperatures of reactants; mix in an insulated cup with lid; stir and record maximum/minimum temperature; calculate ΔT. Estimate q = mcΔT, where m is solution mass in g, c is specific heat capacity (often assumed 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ for dilute aqueous solution) and ΔT is in °C. State assumptions and discuss heat lost to surroundings and cup.

Q54. Distinguish exothermic and endothermic changes using temperature observations. (3 marks)

Exothermic releases heat to surroundings and the measured surroundings/solution temperature generally rises. Endothermic absorbs heat from surroundings and the measured temperature generally falls. Temperature change is evidence; explain the energy transfer in the conclusion.

Q55. Describe an investigation of electrolysis and identify what should be recorded. (5 marks)

Use a teacher-approved low-voltage DC supply, suitable electrolyte and inert or specified electrodes; connect electrodes with correct polarity; run for a controlled time; observe and record gas, colour changes, mass changes or deposits at each electrode. Keep concentration, current and time controlled as appropriate. Do not use mains electricity.

Q56. State how changing concentration or temperature may affect a chemical equilibrium. (4 marks)

A system at equilibrium responds to a change by shifting in the direction that partially opposes that change (Le Chatelier's principle). Adding a reactant tends to shift towards products; increasing pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules; increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction. A catalyst changes the time to reach equilibrium, not its position.

Q57. Describe how to measure the diameter of a wire using a micrometer screw gauge. (6 marks)

Check zero error with spindle and anvil gently closed; place wire between them; turn ratchet until it clicks; read sleeve/main scale; read thimble division; multiply thimble division by least count and add to main scale; apply zero correction; repeat at several positions and orientations and calculate mean. Use the inside jaws; insert them gently and open until they just touch the inner walls; read main scale before vernier zero; find coinciding vernier division; add its value times least count; correct for zero error; repeat in different orientations.

Q59. A metre rule reading begins at 2.4 cm and ends at 17.8 cm. Find the length. (2 marks)

Q60. Give four ways of reducing error when measuring a small object. (4 marks)

Use an instrument with suitable resolution; check and correct zero error; read at eye level; repeat measurements at several positions; use the ratchet consistently; calculate a mean and report appropriate units.

2. Mechanics, force, density and motion

Q61. Describe an experiment to verify Hooke's law. (6 marks)

Suspend a spring beside a vertical scale; record its unloaded length; add known loads in steps within the elastic limit; allow oscillations to stop; record new length and calculate extension; repeat readings; plot load/force against extension. A straight line through the origin shows extension proportional to force within the limit. Gradient of force-extension graph is spring constant.

Q62. How do you determine density of an irregular solid? (4 marks)

Measure mass using a balance; record initial water volume in a measuring cylinder; fully immerse the object without splashing or trapping air; record final volume; object volume = final − initial volume; density = mass ÷ volume with consistent units.

Q63. Describe how to investigate the principle of moments. (5 marks)

Balance a metre rule on a pivot; hang known weights at measured perpendicular distances on both sides; adjust until horizontal equilibrium; calculate clockwise and anticlockwise moments (force × perpendicular distance); compare their sums. In equilibrium, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment.

Q64. Distinguish distance-time and velocity-time graphs. (4 marks)

Gradient of a distance-time graph gives speed. Gradient of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration. Area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement; a horizontal velocity-time line represents constant velocity.

3. Oscillations and waves

Simple pendulum setup

Q65. Describe how to determine the period of a simple pendulum accurately. (5 marks)

Measure length from pivot to centre of bob; displace through a small angle and release without pushing; time 20 oscillations using the same reference crossing direction; repeat; calculate mean time and divide by 20 to obtain period T. Timing many oscillations reduces percentage reaction-time error.

Q66. A pendulum makes 20 oscillations in 36 seconds. Calculate its period. (2 marks)

T = total time ÷ number of oscillations = 36 ÷ 20 = 1.8 s.

Q67. What graph tests the relationship between pendulum period and length? (2 marks)

Plot T² (s²) on the vertical axis against length L (m) on the horizontal axis. A straight line through the origin supports T² proportional to L for small oscillations.

4. Electricity and magnetism

Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel

Q68. Explain how to connect an ammeter and voltmeter to measure the resistance of a resistor. (4 marks)

Connect ammeter in series with resistor; connect voltmeter in parallel across resistor; observe polarity and select suitable ranges; switch on briefly and record current I and potential difference V. Calculate R = V/I in ohms.

Q69. State the procedure for verifying Ohm's law. (5 marks)

Connect a resistor, ammeter, voltmeter and variable low-voltage supply; vary potential difference in steps; record corresponding current; switch off between readings to reduce heating; plot V against I. A straight line through the origin indicates V proportional to I at constant temperature; gradient is resistance.

Q70. How does resistance of a wire depend on its length and cross-sectional area? (3 marks)

For the same material and temperature, resistance is directly proportional to length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area: R = ρL/A. A longer or thinner wire has greater resistance.

Q71. Describe how to map the magnetic field around a bar magnet. (4 marks)

Place magnet beneath paper; use a plotting compass to mark direction at several positions; move compass stepwise and repeat; join marks into smooth field lines with arrows from north to south outside the magnet. Keep other magnets and magnetic materials away.

5. Light, heat and modern physics

Q72. Describe an experiment to verify the law of reflection. (5 marks)

Fix a plane mirror upright on a line; direct a narrow ray at the mirror; mark incident and reflected rays; remove mirror and draw the normal at the point of incidence; measure angle of incidence and reflection from the normal with a protractor; repeat at different angles. The angles are equal within experimental uncertainty.

Q73. How can the focal length of a convex lens be estimated? (4 marks)

Point the lens at a distant object; move a screen until a sharp real image forms; measure distance from lens centre to screen. For a distant object, this distance approximates focal length. Repeat and average; do not look directly at the Sun.

Q74. Describe how to determine specific heat capacity electrically. (6 marks)

Measure mass of the block; insert heater and thermometer into close-fitting holes with thermal paste if available; wrap with insulation; record initial temperature; heat for measured time while recording voltage and current; record final temperature. Energy supplied E = VIt; specific heat capacity c = E/(mΔT). Discuss heat losses and energy absorbed by heater/thermometer.

Q75. How would you compare cooling rates of two materials? (5 marks)

Use equal masses or equal volumes in identical containers; start at the same temperature; place thermometers consistently; record temperature at equal time intervals in the same surroundings; plot temperature against time and compare gradients. Control exposed surface area, insulation and airflow.

Q76. Why are radioactive-source practicals controlled by a teacher? (3 marks)

Ionising radiation can damage living tissue. Only authorised, teacher-controlled sealed sources and approved procedures may be used; minimise exposure time, maximise distance, use appropriate shielding, never handle a source directly and follow school radiation-safety rules. Many learning outcomes can be assessed using supplied count-rate data instead.
