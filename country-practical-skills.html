🧬 Online Biology Practical Notes

Biology practical work involves observing living organisms and biological materials, using instruments to examine structures, sampling populations, carrying out investigations and recording evidence accurately.

1. Essential Biology apparatus and uses

ApparatusUse and correct handling
Light microscopeMagnifies cells and small specimens. Begin with the low-power objective, secure the slide, focus with coarse adjustment at low power, then use fine adjustment for a sharp image. Do not allow the objective lens to strike the slide.
Slide and cover slipHold a thin specimen for viewing. Lower the cover slip gently at an angle to reduce trapped air bubbles.
Hand lensProvides low magnification for external features. Bring the lens close to the eye and adjust the specimen distance to focus.
ForcepsPick up small specimens or tissues without using fingers.
Scalpel and dissecting scissorsMake controlled cuts during dissection. Cut away from the body and keep sharp edges directed safely.
Mounted needleSeparate, tease or position small structures.
Dissecting traySupports a specimen during dissection and contains fluids.
Petri dishHolds small specimens or cultures. Use aseptic technique where microbiological work is authorised.
QuadratSamples organisms in a defined area to estimate abundance, frequency or percentage cover.
Transect tape or lineMeasures a line across a habitat to study changes in distribution along an environmental gradient.
Thermometer, stopwatch and rulerMeasure temperature, time and length in biological investigations.
Balance and measuring cylinderMeasure mass and liquid volume, for example in food, growth or displacement investigations.
ColorimeterMeasures light absorbance or transmission by a coloured solution, often used to estimate concentration.

2. Microscope skills and magnification

  1. Carry the microscope with two hands and place it on a stable bench.
  2. Clean lenses with lens tissue only.
  3. Place the prepared slide on the stage and secure it.
  4. Select the lowest-power objective and focus carefully.
  5. Centre the specimen before changing to higher power.
  6. Use fine adjustment at high power and adjust illumination.
  7. Draw what is actually visible, using clear single lines and labels.
Total magnification = Eyepiece magnification × Objective lens magnification Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

Image size and actual size must be in the same units before division. Convert millimetres to micrometres using 1 mm = 1000 µm.

Biological drawing rules: use a sharp pencil, large clear outline, no shading, straight label lines that do not cross, and a title. Include magnification or a scale bar when required. Draw relative sizes and proportions accurately.

3. Sampling organisms using quadrats and transects

A quadrat is placed randomly or systematically in a habitat. Count individuals or estimate percentage cover within each sample. Repeat at several locations and calculate a mean. Avoid choosing only places where the target organism is obvious.

Mean number per quadrat = Total individuals counted ÷ Number of quadrats Estimated population = Mean density per unit area × Total habitat area

For a belt transect, place a tape across the habitat and sample at regular intervals or continuously along the line. Record abiotic conditions such as light intensity, soil moisture or temperature when investigating distribution. State the sampling method and limitations.

4. Common biological investigations

  • Food tests: use appropriate reagents and controls to test for starch, reducing sugars, protein and lipids. Record the initial and final colour or appearance. Use the correct reagent procedure taught in the laboratory.
  • Osmosis: compare changes in mass or length of equal-sized plant tissue pieces placed in solutions of different concentrations. Keep tissue type, dimensions, time and temperature consistent; blot consistently before weighing.
  • Enzyme activity: investigate how temperature or pH affects reaction rate. Keep enzyme and substrate concentrations, volumes and timing consistent. Repeat each condition.
  • Photosynthesis: investigate the effect of light intensity or another limiting factor on photosynthetic rate. Control temperature and carbon dioxide availability where possible.
  • Transpiration: use a potometer to estimate water uptake, not a direct measurement of water loss from leaves. Reduce leaks, cut the shoot under water where instructed, and control environmental conditions.
  • Germination: investigate the conditions needed for germination while changing one factor at a time and using a suitable control group.

5. Biological observations and records

Separate qualitative observations (colour, shape, texture, presence of structures) from quantitative measurements (mass, length, number, time, temperature). Use a labelled table, consistent units and repeat samples. Distinguish observation from interpretation: “brown region present” is an observation; “the tissue is diseased” is an interpretation that needs supporting evidence.

6. Biology practical evaluation

Discuss sample size, random sampling, measurement resolution, biological variation, environmental changes, contamination and observer bias. Improvements should address the stated limitation: for example, increase the number of randomly selected quadrats, standardise tissue dimensions or use a calibrated balance.

⚗ Online Chemistry Practical Notes

Chemistry practical work involves handling substances safely, measuring quantities, preparing solutions, separating mixtures, carrying out reactions and analysing experimental evidence.

1. Chemistry apparatus and uses

ApparatusUse and correct handling
BeakerHolds, mixes or heats liquids. Graduations are approximate and should not be used when high volume accuracy is required.
Measuring cylinderMeasures liquid volume more accurately than a beaker. Place on a level bench and read the meniscus at eye level.
Volumetric flaskPrepares a solution to one accurate fixed volume. Dissolve the solute, transfer quantitatively, make up to the calibration mark and invert to mix.
Volumetric pipetteTransfers one accurate fixed volume. Use a pipette filler; never pipette by mouth. Allow it to drain as designed; do not blow out a standard volumetric pipette unless specified.
BuretteDelivers accurately measured variable volumes, especially in titration. Rinse with titrant, remove air bubbles from the tip and read the scale at eye level.
Conical flaskHolds the analyte during titration and can be swirled without easily spilling.
Funnel and filter paperSeparate an insoluble solid from a liquid by filtration. Fold and seat the paper correctly; keep the liquid level below the paper edge.
Separating funnelSeparates immiscible liquids by density. Allow layers to settle, remove the stopper before draining and close the tap before the interface reaches it.
Evaporating basinConcentrates a solution or evaporates solvent using controlled heating.
Crucible and tongsStrongly heat small quantities of solid; use suitable tongs and heat-resistant support.
Gas syringeCollects and measures gas volume. Check that the plunger moves freely and the apparatus is gas-tight.
ThermometerMeasures temperature; select a suitable range and avoid contact with the vessel where it would distort the sample temperature.
Balance and spatulaMeasure mass and transfer small amounts of solid. Do not place chemicals directly on the balance pan.
Distillation condenserCools vapour into liquid. Cooling water normally enters at the lower connector and exits at the upper connector.

2. Measurement and preparation of solutions

Use a balance to measure solid mass. Transfer the solid to a beaker and dissolve it in a portion of solvent. Transfer the solution into a volumetric flask, rinsing the beaker and funnel into the flask to avoid loss. Add solvent close to the mark, then use a dropper to bring the bottom of the meniscus to the calibration line at eye level. Stopper and invert repeatedly to mix.

Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = Amount of substance (mol) ÷ Volume (dm³) Amount (mol) = Mass (g) ÷ Molar mass (g mol⁻¹)

Remember: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Convert volume into dm³ when using mol dm⁻³.

3. Titration technique

  1. Rinse the burette with the titrant and the pipette with the solution it will deliver.
  2. Use a pipette filler to transfer the fixed aliquot into a clean conical flask.
  3. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator if the method requires one.
  4. Record the initial burette reading at eye level.
  5. Add titrant while swirling the flask; add dropwise near the endpoint.
  6. Record the final reading and calculate titre = final reading − initial reading.
  7. Repeat until concordant titres are obtained, following the examination board's stated tolerance.
Accuracy tips: use a white tile to see the endpoint, rinse flask walls with distilled water, remove air bubbles from the burette tip, read the bottom of the meniscus and record readings to the burette's appropriate resolution. Do not copy a titre or force concordance.

4. Separation and purification methods

  • Filtration: separates an insoluble solid from a liquid; the solid retained is residue and the liquid collected is filtrate.
  • Crystallisation: concentrates a solution and allows crystals to form on cooling or slow evaporation. Avoid overheating substances that decompose.
  • Simple distillation: recovers a solvent from a solution or separates liquids with sufficiently different boiling points.
  • Fractional distillation: separates miscible liquids with closer boiling points using a fractionating column.
  • Chromatography: separates mixture components by different affinities for stationary and mobile phases. Keep the baseline above the solvent level.
  • Separating funnel: separates immiscible liquid layers; identify which layer is which rather than assuming the denser liquid is always the lower layer without checking.

5. Common chemical tests

Use only the reagents and procedures specified by the teacher or syllabus. Record the reagent, conditions and positive observation. Examples include tests for common gases, ions, acids and alkalis. Never identify a gas by smelling it directly. Use approved splint tests or other prescribed methods with appropriate supervision and small quantities.

6. Reaction-rate investigations

Change one independent variable, such as concentration, temperature or surface area, while controlling other relevant factors. Measure rate by a suitable method, for example gas volume produced per unit time or time taken for a visible change. Repeat measurements and use a consistent endpoint.

Average rate = Change in measured quantity ÷ Time taken

7. Chemistry practical safety

Wear eye protection and suitable protective clothing. Read hazard labels and safety data. Use a fume cupboard where instructed, keep flammable solvents away from flames, add acid to water when preparing dilute acid, never taste chemicals and never pipette by mouth. Dispose of chemical waste as directed. Never improvise gas tests or mix unknown chemicals.

⚛ Online Physics Practical Notes

Physics practical work uses measurement and mathematical relationships to investigate matter, motion, energy, waves, electricity and fields.

1. Physics apparatus and uses

ApparatusUse and correct handling
Metre rule / rulerMeasures length. Align the zero mark with the object or subtract initial from final readings. Read at eye level.
Vernier calipersMeasure internal diameter, external diameter and depth. Check zero error, read the main scale and add the vernier contribution.
Micrometer screw gaugeMeasures small thicknesses or diameters. Use the ratchet to apply consistent force and check for zero error.
BalanceMeasures mass. Zero or tare before use and record the displayed unit.
Stopwatch / light gatesMeasure time intervals. Use light gates where possible to reduce reaction-time uncertainty.
Thermometer / temperature probeMeasure temperature. Wait for a stable reading and use an appropriate range.
Spring balance / newton meterMeasures force. Check zero, hang vertically and read without exceeding the scale range.
AmmeterMeasures current and is connected in series. Select a suitable range and observe polarity for analogue meters.
VoltmeterMeasures potential difference and is connected in parallel across a component.
Rheostat / variable resistorChanges resistance and controls current in a circuit. Start with a safe high-resistance setting where appropriate.
Power supply, switch and leadsProvide, control and connect electrical energy. Switch off before changing circuit connections.
Ray box, lens and optical pinsInvestigate reflection, refraction and image formation. Avoid looking directly into intense light sources.
Pendulum bob and clamp standInvestigate oscillations. Keep the swing small and measure many oscillations for a better period estimate.
Trolley, runway and ticker timerInvestigate motion and acceleration. Keep the track stable and repeat runs under the same conditions.

2. Measurement instruments and resolution

Choose an instrument whose range and resolution suit the quantity. A ruler may be adequate for a large length, while vernier calipers or a micrometer are more suitable for small diameters. Do not report more decimal places than the instrument supports. Check zero before and after measurements when appropriate.

Vernier reading: main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × least count). Apply the zero correction using the sign of the zero error.

Micrometer reading: sleeve reading + (thimble reading × least count), followed by the appropriate zero correction.

3. Core physics investigations

  • Density: measure mass with a balance and volume by dimensions for regular objects or displacement for irregular objects. Ensure units are consistent.
  • Hooke's law: add known loads, measure extension from the original length and plot force against extension. Do not exceed the elastic limit.
  • Moments: measure the perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action of force. Balance moments in equilibrium.
  • Pendulum: measure the time for 10–20 oscillations, repeat and divide by the number of oscillations to estimate the period.
  • Electric circuits: connect ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel. Record current and potential difference for several settings; switch off between adjustments if required.
  • Optics: align ray box, block and pins carefully. Avoid parallax when aligning pins and record angles from the normal.
  • Motion: use a ticker timer, light gates or measured distance and time. Repeat runs and keep track slope and release conditions consistent.

4. Graphs in Physics and all sciences

Choose the graph to match the data. A line graph is used for continuous variables; a bar chart compares discrete categories; a histogram shows a continuous frequency distribution with touching bars; a scatter graph shows the relationship between two numerical variables; a pie chart shows proportions of a whole. A frequency polygon and cumulative frequency graph are used for grouped distributions.

Parts of a scientific graph

  1. Descriptive title.
  2. Horizontal x-axis and vertical y-axis.
  3. Correct variable names and units on both axes.
  4. Even, sensible scale using most of the graph area.
  5. Accurately plotted points, usually small crosses.
  6. Line or curve of best fit where appropriate.
  7. Key or legend when more than one data series is shown.
Axis convention: the independent variable is usually plotted on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. Label axes as Quantity (unit), for example Time (s), not just “Time”. Do not join scatter points dot-to-dot unless the task specifically requires it.
Gradient = Change in y ÷ Change in x = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

Use two widely separated points on the best-fit line. Include units in the gradient. The meaning of the gradient depends on the quantities on the axes: distance–time gives speed; velocity–time gives acceleration; current–potential difference gives conductance if current is on y and potential difference on x.

5. Data processing and uncertainty

Mean = Sum of repeated readings ÷ Number of readings Percentage error = (Absolute error ÷ Accepted value) × 100% Percentage uncertainty = (Absolute uncertainty ÷ Measured value) × 100%

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. Precision describes closeness of repeated readings to one another. Reliability concerns consistency on repetition. Validity concerns whether the method tests the intended relationship. A precise result can still be inaccurate if a systematic error is present.

6. Types and sources of experimental error

  • Systematic error: shifts readings consistently, for example a zero error or poor calibration. Check zero and calibrate equipment.
  • Random error: causes unpredictable variation, for example reaction time or fluctuating conditions. Repeat readings and calculate a mean.
  • Parallax error: occurs when a scale is viewed from an angle. Read at eye level.
  • Gross error: a major mistake such as recording the wrong unit or connecting a meter incorrectly. Check readings, labels and circuit diagrams.

7. Following a physics procedure

  1. Read the aim and identify independent, dependent and control variables.
  2. Draw or inspect the apparatus arrangement before switching on.
  3. Check instrument ranges, zero settings and electrical connections.
  4. Change only the independent variable systematically.
  5. Measure the dependent variable using the same method each time.
  6. Repeat readings and record all values with units.
  7. Plot the graph, calculate gradients and interpret the relationship.
  8. Identify limitations and propose improvements linked to the source of error.

Shared practical skills for Biology, Chemistry and Physics

1. Scientific investigation variables

  • Independent variable: the factor deliberately changed.
  • Dependent variable: the factor measured or observed.
  • Controlled variables: factors kept constant to make the comparison fair.

2. Qualitative and quantitative data

Qualitative data describe qualities, such as colour change or formation of a precipitate. Quantitative data are numerical measurements, such as mass, time, temperature, volume or number of organisms. Record observations immediately and distinguish raw data from calculated values.

3. Significant figures and decimal places

Non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are not. For multiplication and division, round the result to the least number of significant figures in the input data. For addition and subtraction, round to the least precise decimal place. Follow the precision required by the task and do not imply unsupported accuracy.

5. Accuracy, precision, reliability, validity and sensitivity

  • Accuracy: closeness to the true or accepted value.
  • Precision: closeness of repeated measurements to each other.
  • Reliability: consistency when a method is repeated.
  • Validity: whether the method tests the intended question and controls relevant variables.
  • Resolution: smallest scale interval or change an instrument can display or distinguish.
  • Sensitivity: how much an instrument's output changes for a given change in the measured quantity.

6. A complete practical report

  1. Title and aim.
  2. Hypothesis or prediction, where appropriate.
  3. Apparatus and materials.
  4. Variables and controls.
  5. Risk assessment and safety precautions.
  6. Numbered procedure in logical order.
  7. Results table with units in headings.
  8. Calculations and graphs.
  9. Discussion of patterns, anomalies and scientific explanation.
  10. Conclusion linked to the aim and evidence.
  11. Evaluation: limitations, errors and specific improvements.

7. General laboratory safety

Wear appropriate eye protection and protective clothing; tie back long hair; never taste chemicals; never pipette by mouth; do not directly smell chemicals; handle glass and sharp tools carefully; use tongs for hot apparatus; keep flammable materials away from ignition sources; report spills and injuries immediately; follow hazard labels and safety data; dispose of biological and chemical waste as instructed; wash hands after practical work. Follow the teacher's risk assessment and local regulations.

8. Examination checklist

  • Can I identify apparatus and explain why it is suitable?
  • Can I select the correct measuring instrument and read its scale?
  • Can I identify independent, dependent and controlled variables?
  • Can I write a safe, repeatable procedure?
  • Can I record measurements with correct units and precision?

  • Can I write a conclusion supported by results?

Practical Skills Handbook: Measurement, Procedures and Graphs

This section develops the skills learners use repeatedly in Biology, Chemistry and Physics. Always follow the exact method, safety instructions and assessment requirements supplied by your teacher or examination board.

1. Selecting the correct apparatus

Choose apparatus by considering the quantity to be measured, the expected size or range, the resolution required and the material being handled. A measuring cylinder is suitable for routine liquid-volume measurements; a volumetric pipette or volumetric flask is preferred when a specified volume must be measured or prepared accurately. A beaker is mainly for holding, mixing or heating, not precise volume measurement. Use a balance for mass, a thermometer or probe for temperature, a stopwatch or light gate for time, and a suitable length instrument for dimensions.

Measurement taskSuitable apparatusImportant check
Small external diameterVernier calipers or micrometer, depending on sizeCheck zero error and use the correct least count.
Liquid volume for titrationVolumetric pipette and buretteUse a pipette filler; remove burette-tip air bubbles.
Fixed solution volumeVolumetric flaskBring the meniscus to the calibration mark at eye level.
Mass of a solidElectronic or top-pan balanceZero or tare first; use a weighing boat or paper.
Temperature changeThermometer or temperature probeKeep the sensing part correctly positioned and wait for a stable reading.
Short time intervalsStopwatch, light gate or data loggerRepeat manual timings to reduce reaction-time effects.
Small specimen detailsHand lens or microscopeStart at low magnification and focus carefully.

2. Reading scales correctly

  1. Identify the unit and the numbered scale marks.
  2. Determine the value represented by one smallest division.
  3. Place the eye perpendicular to the scale to reduce parallax.
  4. For liquid levels, read the appropriate part of the meniscus at eye level; for most aqueous liquids this is the bottom of the meniscus.
  5. Record the reading immediately, including its unit and justified decimal places.
  6. Check whether the instrument has a zero error before applying a correction.

Least count / scale interval: the value represented by the smallest scale division. For a digital instrument, use the smallest displayed increment as its resolution, while remembering that resolution is not the same as total measurement accuracy.

3. Zero error and corrected readings

Zero error is the reading shown when the true measured quantity is zero. Record both its magnitude and sign. The corrected reading is obtained by subtracting the zero error from the observed reading. For example, if an instrument has a positive zero error of +0.02 cm and displays 3.46 cm, the corrected reading is 3.44 cm. If the zero error is negative, subtracting it increases the corrected reading. Always follow the sign convention taught for the particular instrument.

4. Accuracy, precision and uncertainty

  • Accuracy: closeness of a result to an accepted or true value.
  • Precision: closeness of repeated readings to one another.
  • Repeatability: agreement when the same person uses the same method and equipment under the same conditions.
  • Reproducibility: agreement when conditions such as operator, equipment or location change.
  • Uncertainty: an estimate of the range within which the measured value is expected to lie.
Absolute error = |Measured value − Accepted value| Percentage error = (Absolute error ÷ Accepted value) × 100% Percentage uncertainty = (Absolute uncertainty ÷ Measured value) × 100%

Do not confuse percentage error, which compares a result with an accepted value, with percentage uncertainty, which expresses the uncertainty in a measurement. The method for estimating instrument uncertainty depends on the instrument and examination-board convention. For an analogue scale, courses commonly use a fraction of the smallest division; for a digital instrument, the last displayed digit is often used. Follow the stated convention.

5. Repeats, averages and anomalous results

Repeat measurements where practical. Calculate the mean of suitable repeated readings. Compare the readings before calculating: an anomalous value should not be silently deleted. Repeat the measurement if possible, record the original value and explain any decision to exclude it using evidence or a stated rule. Repeats improve confidence in consistency but do not automatically remove systematic error.

Mean = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) ÷ n

6. Writing and following a reproducible procedure

A good procedure is sufficiently detailed that another learner could repeat the investigation. Use numbered steps and include quantities, units, apparatus positions, timing, control variables and how readings are taken.

  1. State what is being investigated and identify the independent variable.
  2. State exactly how the independent variable will be changed and list the values or range.
  3. Explain how the dependent variable will be measured, including the instrument and units.
  4. Identify the variables to be kept constant and explain how they will be controlled.
  5. Describe the apparatus arrangement clearly enough to reproduce it.
  6. Give the sequence of actions in the correct order, including when to start and stop timing or take readings.
  7. State how many repeats will be made and how results will be processed.
  8. Include relevant safety precautions at the step where the hazard arises.
  9. Prepare a results table before collecting data.
Procedure writing tip: avoid vague instructions such as “heat gently”, “add some solution” or “measure carefully”. Where the method allows, specify a measurable quantity, a controlled condition or a clear endpoint. Do not invent quantities that are not supplied by the practical method.

7. Planning a fair test

Change one independent variable systematically, measure the dependent variable consistently and control other factors that could affect the result. A control setup can help show whether the observed effect is due to the factor being investigated. In biological investigations, account for natural variation by using adequate sample sizes and representative sampling. In chemical and physical investigations, keep apparatus arrangement and measurement technique consistent.

9. Parts of a graph and presentation checklist

  • Write a concise title stating the relationship or data being shown.
  • Put the independent variable on the horizontal axis in the usual experimental convention and the dependent variable on the vertical axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and unit, for example “Time (s)” and “Temperature (°C)”.
  • Choose a uniform scale that uses most of the graph space and is easy to interpolate.
  • Plot points accurately with small crosses or clear dots.
  • Draw a best-fit line or smooth curve where appropriate; do not join scatter points dot-to-dot unless instructed.
  • Add a key or legend if more than one data set is shown.
  • Show any calculation or gradient triangle clearly when required.

10. Scale selection example

If time values range from 0 to 60 seconds, a horizontal scale of 10 seconds per large square may be convenient if it uses the graph width well. If measured temperatures range from 21°C to 47°C, a vertical axis from 20°C to 50°C may use the graph area better than starting at zero. Clearly show any non-zero axis start; do not conceal a scale break.

11. Gradient, intercept and relationship

Gradient = (Change in vertical-axis quantity) ÷ (Change in horizontal-axis quantity)

Choose two well-separated points on the best-fit line, read their coordinates and calculate the gradient. Include the compound unit. The gradient's physical meaning depends on the axes: on a distance–time graph it is speed; on a velocity–time graph it is acceleration. The vertical intercept is the value of the y-variable when x is zero, where that interpretation is meaningful.

A straight line through the origin can indicate direct proportionality. A straight line that does not pass through the origin indicates a linear relationship but not direct proportionality. A curve indicates that the rate of change may vary. Describe the observed pattern before explaining it scientifically.

12. Evaluating results and suggesting improvements

Evaluation should identify a limitation, explain how it affects the result and propose a realistic improvement. Examples: human reaction time can be reduced using light gates; a small biological sample can be improved by increasing the number of randomly selected samples; heat loss can be reduced using insulation and a lid; a coarse scale can be replaced with an instrument of suitable higher resolution; endpoint judgement can be improved by using a consistent procedure or an appropriate instrumental method where available.

13. Final practical-work checklist

  • I understand the aim and scientific principle.
  • I can name each apparatus item and explain why it is suitable.
  • I can identify the variables and controls.
  • I can follow the procedure in sequence and work safely.
  • I can read scales, check zero error and record units.
  • I can distinguish accuracy, precision, reliability and validity.
  • I can organise raw results in a suitable table.
  • I can select and draw an appropriate graph with all required parts.
  • I can calculate means, rates, percentages and gradients where relevant.
  • I can write a conclusion supported by the evidence.
  • I can identify a specific limitation and a matching improvement.

Practical Investigations: Worked Examples and Skills Practice

Use these examples to practise planning, collecting, processing and evaluating practical evidence. The example values are illustrative teaching data, not official examination results or prescribed country experiments.

Biology worked investigation 1: Effect of solution concentration on plant tissue

Aim: investigate how the concentration of a surrounding solution affects the mass of equal-sized plant tissue pieces.

Apparatus and materials

Plant tissue such as potato, cork borer, scalpel, ruler, balance, labelled containers, measuring cylinder, prepared solutions of different concentrations, forceps, paper towel and stopwatch.

Procedure

  1. Use a cork borer to prepare tissue cylinders of the same diameter.
  2. Trim each cylinder to the same length and remove damaged ends.
  3. Blot each piece in the same way and record its initial mass.
  4. Place equal numbers of pieces into labelled containers with equal volumes of the test solutions.
  5. Keep immersion time and temperature the same for every concentration.
  6. Remove the pieces, blot consistently and record final mass.
  7. Repeat with additional tissue pieces at each concentration and calculate the mean percentage change in mass.
Percentage change in mass = [(Final mass − Initial mass) ÷ Initial mass] × 100%

A positive percentage change means mass increased; a negative value means mass decreased. Plot solution concentration on the x-axis and mean percentage change in mass on the y-axis. Explain the pattern using net water movement by osmosis. Improve reliability by using more tissue pieces and improve validity by keeping tissue source, dimensions, blotting method, solution volume, time and temperature consistent.

Biology worked investigation 2: Estimating plant distribution with a quadrat

Aim: estimate the abundance of a plant species in a defined habitat.

  1. Define the study area and identify the target species before sampling.
  2. Use random coordinates or a random-number method to choose quadrat positions.
  3. Place the quadrat without moving it to favour areas where the plant is abundant.
  4. Count individuals rooted inside the quadrat, or estimate percentage cover using the same rule each time.
  5. Repeat at a suitable number of positions and record each result.
  6. Calculate the mean count per quadrat and estimate total abundance only if the sampled quadrats represent the habitat adequately.
Mean count per quadrat = Sum of counts ÷ Number of quadrats

For a 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrat, area = 0.25 m². If the mean count is 8 plants per quadrat, estimated density = 8 ÷ 0.25 = 32 plants m⁻². State assumptions and uncertainty; clumped distributions and small sample sizes can make estimates less reliable.

Biology worked investigation 3: Enzyme activity and temperature

Plan a controlled investigation using an appropriate enzyme-substrate system approved by the teacher. Use a water bath to establish temperatures, measure a defined reaction endpoint consistently, keep enzyme and substrate concentrations and volumes constant, and repeat each temperature. If measuring time to a fixed endpoint, a shorter time indicates a faster reaction under the same method. Do not compare results if the endpoint or mixing method changes between trials.

Chemistry worked investigation 1: Acid–alkali titration

Aim: determine the volume of a standard solution needed to react completely with a measured volume of another solution.

Apparatus

Burette, stand and clamp, volumetric pipette, pipette filler, conical flask, funnel, suitable indicator, white tile, wash bottle and the solutions specified by the practical method.

Procedure and recording

  1. Wear eye protection and check the labels and concentrations of all solutions.
  2. Rinse the burette with the solution that will go into it, then fill it and ensure the jet contains liquid without an air bubble.
  3. Rinse the pipette with the solution to be measured and transfer the specified aliquot into a conical flask using a pipette filler.
  4. Add the required indicator if the method calls for one.
  5. Record the initial burette reading at eye level.
  6. Add solution while swirling; approach the endpoint slowly and add dropwise near the colour change.
  7. Record the final burette reading and calculate the titre.
  8. Repeat until the required concordant results are obtained under the stated course or examination tolerance.
Titre (cm³) = Final burette reading − Initial burette reading

Illustrative readings: initial 0.20 cm³ and final 24.60 cm³ gives a titre of 24.40 cm³. If repeat titres are 24.40, 24.35 and 24.40 cm³, use the concordant values according to the stated tolerance and explain which readings are included. Do not select readings merely to obtain a preferred answer.

Chemistry worked investigation 2: Paper chromatography

  1. Draw a pencil baseline near the bottom of chromatography paper.
  2. Apply a small concentrated spot of the sample to the baseline and allow it to dry; repeat spotting if instructed.
  3. Place a shallow layer of solvent in a suitable container, keeping the solvent below the baseline.
  4. Stand the paper in the container with the sample spot above the solvent and cover the container.
  5. Allow the solvent to rise; remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top.
  6. Immediately mark the solvent front in pencil and allow the paper to dry.
  7. Measure from the baseline to the centre of each separated spot and from the baseline to the solvent front.
Rf value = Distance moved by component ÷ Distance moved by solvent front

Rf values have no units and are valid for comparison only when the stationary phase, solvent and conditions are comparable. A single Rf value alone does not prove the identity or purity of a substance.

Chemistry worked investigation 3: Rate of reaction

Investigate a reaction rate by measuring a suitable change, such as gas volume produced over time, using an appropriate gas syringe and a gas-tight apparatus. Keep total volume, temperature, mass or surface area of solid and other relevant variables controlled. Record readings at regular time intervals, repeat the experiment and plot gas volume against time. The gradient represents the rate of gas production; the curve becomes less steep as the reaction slows. Follow the teacher's approved method and never seal a system in which pressure could build dangerously.

Physics worked investigation 1: Hooke's law

Aim: investigate the relationship between applied force and extension of a spring.

  1. Clamp the spring securely beside a vertical ruler and measure its original length without load.
  2. Add a known load and allow oscillations to stop.
  3. Read the new length at eye level and calculate extension = loaded length − original length.
  4. Increase the load in safe equal steps, recording force and extension each time.
  5. Repeat readings where possible and plot force (N) against extension (m or cm).
  6. Do not exceed the spring's safe limit or continue if it shows permanent deformation.
Extension = Loaded length − Original length

For a force-against-extension graph, the gradient is the spring constant when extension is measured in metres: k = F ÷ x, with unit N m⁻¹. A straight-line region through the origin is consistent with Hooke's law within the elastic limit.

Physics worked investigation 2: Finding density

For a regular solid, measure its mass with a balance and its dimensions with a suitable ruler, vernier calipers or micrometer. Calculate volume from the appropriate geometric formula, then calculate density. For an irregular solid that does not dissolve or react in water, use a displacement method with a suitable measuring cylinder or displacement can.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Example: a sample has mass 54 g and volume 20 cm³. Density = 54 ÷ 20 = 2.7 g cm⁻³. State the units and the precision justified by the measurements. Avoid using a measuring cylinder whose divisions are too large for the displaced volume.

Physics worked investigation 3: Current–voltage relationship

  1. Connect the ammeter in series with the component and the voltmeter in parallel across it.
  2. Check the circuit against the diagram before switching on.
  3. Begin with a safe power-supply setting and suitable meter ranges.
  4. Adjust the variable resistor to obtain a range of potential differences and record current and voltage.
  5. Switch off between adjustments if required to limit heating.
  6. Repeat readings and plot current against potential difference, or use the axes requested by the task.

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is proportional to potential difference. If current is plotted against voltage, the gradient is conductance; resistance is voltage divided by current. Heating can change resistance, so monitor temperature and avoid excessive current.

How to construct a results table

  • Give the table a clear heading describing the investigation.
  • Put the measured variable and unit in each column heading, not repeatedly in every cell.
  • Keep decimal places consistent with the instrument resolution.
  • Record raw readings before calculated values.
  • Include repeat readings and a mean where appropriate.
  • Do not leave unexplained blank cells; use a clear dash or note when a value was not measured.

Practice questions

  1. A learner measures a length as 12.4 cm using a ruler. Explain why recording 12.400 cm may be misleading.
  2. State two differences between accuracy and precision.
  3. Explain why a burette should be rinsed with the titrant before use.
  4. Why must the solvent level be below the pencil baseline in paper chromatography?
  5. Describe two ways to reduce random error when timing a pendulum.
  6. A graph has time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. What physical quantity is represented by its gradient?
  7. Explain why a histogram has touching bars but a bar chart has gaps.
  8. Identify the independent, dependent and two controlled variables in an investigation of how light intensity affects photosynthesis.
  9. Suggest one limitation of using only three quadrats to estimate plant abundance in a large field.
  10. Explain why an ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel.
  11. A spring has an original length of 8.0 cm and a loaded length of 11.5 cm. Calculate its extension.
  12. Give one suitable improvement when a reaction produces gas too quickly for manual stopwatch readings.

Suggested answers and marking guidance

  1. Extra decimal places imply a resolution and precision the ruler does not provide; record only justified digits.
  2. Accuracy is closeness to an accepted value; precision is agreement between repeated readings.
  3. Rinsing removes residual water or another liquid that could dilute or contaminate the titrant.
  4. The sample must begin above the solvent so it travels with the rising solvent rather than dissolving directly into the solvent pool.
  5. Time more oscillations and divide by the number; repeat trials and calculate a mean; use a light gate if available.
  6. Speed, because gradient is change in distance divided by change in time.
  7. Bar-chart categories are discrete; histogram bars represent adjoining intervals of a continuous variable.
  8. Independent: light intensity; dependent: photosynthesis rate; controls may include temperature, carbon dioxide concentration, plant species or leaf area.
  9. Three quadrats may not represent patchy distribution; increase the number and select positions randomly across the habitat.
  10. An ammeter measures current through a component and must be in the same current path; a voltmeter measures potential difference across a component.
  11. Extension = 11.5 − 8.0 = 3.5 cm.
  12. Use a suitable data logger or gas sensor, or choose a safe method with shorter measurement intervals and suitable apparatus.

Marking approach: award credit for the scientific principle, correct apparatus or variable, a specific method detail, correct units or calculation and a relevant safety or evaluation point. Accept equivalent scientifically correct wording; apply the local syllabus or examination-board mark scheme where one is provided.
