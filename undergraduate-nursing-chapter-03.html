1. Cardiovascular system overview

The cardiovascular system consists of the heart, blood and blood vessels. Its central functions are transport of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, heat and metabolic waste; maintenance of tissue perfusion; and contribution to immune defence and homeostasis. The heart is a muscular pump with right and left sides. The right side sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side sends oxygenated blood to systemic tissues.

The circulation is arranged in pulmonary and systemic circuits. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood and capillaries provide exchange surfaces. Vessel walls contain tunica intima, tunica media and tunica externa in varying proportions. Arterioles regulate resistance; capillaries permit exchange; veins act as capacitance vessels and use valves and skeletal-muscle activity to assist venous return.

2. Heart anatomy and cardiac cycle

The heart lies in the mediastinum and is enclosed by the pericardium. Its chambers are right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle. The tricuspid valve lies between right atrium and ventricle; the pulmonary valve guards the pulmonary outflow; the mitral valve lies between left atrium and ventricle; and the aortic valve guards the aortic outflow. Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles help prevent atrioventricular valve prolapse during ventricular contraction.

The cardiac cycle alternates diastole, when chambers fill, and systole, when ventricles eject blood. Valve movement follows pressure gradients. Stroke volume is the amount ejected by a ventricle per beat; cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Preload, afterload and contractility influence stroke volume. These concepts help explain why pulse, blood pressure, perfusion and fluid status must be interpreted together.

3. Electrical conduction and blood pressure

The sinoatrial node normally initiates the cardiac impulse. It spreads through atrial tissue to the atrioventricular node, then through the bundle of His, bundle branches and Purkinje fibres to coordinate ventricular contraction. The electrocardiogram records electrical activity at the body surface; it does not directly measure mechanical pumping or tissue perfusion.

Arterial blood pressure reflects cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, modified by arterial compliance and circulating volume. Systolic pressure occurs during ventricular ejection; diastolic pressure is measured during relaxation. Accurate measurement requires appropriate cuff size, positioning, rest and technique. A single reading should be considered with symptoms, repeat measurements and local clinical thresholds.

4. Blood and haemostasis

Blood consists of plasma and formed elements: erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets. Erythrocytes contain haemoglobin for oxygen transport and contribute to carbon dioxide carriage. Leukocytes participate in innate and adaptive immunity. Platelets adhere and aggregate at sites of vascular injury. Plasma carries proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones and waste products.

Haemostasis includes vascular constriction, platelet plug formation and coagulation. Coagulation factors generate fibrin, stabilising the platelet plug. Natural anticoagulant pathways limit clotting, and fibrinolysis helps remove fibrin. Anaemia, thrombocytopenia and coagulation disorders affect oxygen delivery or bleeding risk; interpretation of laboratory tests requires clinical context and local reference ranges.

5. Lymphatic and immune circulation

The lymphatic system returns excess interstitial fluid and proteins to the bloodstream, transports absorbed dietary lipids from the intestine and supports immune surveillance. Lymph travels through vessels and nodes before entering large veins. Lymph nodes contain immune cells and filter lymph; the spleen filters blood and participates in immune responses.

Lymph flow depends on skeletal-muscle movement, respiratory pressure changes, smooth muscle in vessel walls and one-way valves. Swelling can result from impaired lymph drainage, venous disease, inflammation or systemic fluid imbalance. Assessment should describe distribution, onset, skin changes, discomfort and associated symptoms, with escalation for concerning features.

6. Respiratory system anatomy

The respiratory tract is divided into upper and lower airways. The nose, pharynx and larynx condition and conduct air; the trachea divides into bronchi, bronchioles and terminal airways. Respiratory bronchioles and alveoli form the gas-exchange region. The lungs are covered by visceral pleura; parietal pleura lines the thoracic cavity, with a thin fluid layer between them.

The diaphragm is the principal muscle of quiet inspiration. External intercostal muscles assist expansion of the thorax. The conducting airways warm, humidify and filter air, while mucociliary clearance and immune defences help remove particles and pathogens. Alveoli provide a large, thin surface closely associated with pulmonary capillaries.

7. Ventilation, gas exchange and transport

Ventilation is movement of air into and out of the lungs. Inspiration occurs when thoracic volume increases and alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure; quiet expiration is usually passive as elastic recoil reduces thoracic volume. Compliance describes ease of lung expansion; airway resistance is influenced by airway calibre, secretions and smooth-muscle tone.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across the alveolar-capillary membrane down partial-pressure gradients. Most oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin; a small amount is dissolved in plasma. Carbon dioxide is carried mainly as bicarbonate, with smaller amounts dissolved or bound to proteins. Ventilation-perfusion matching is essential for efficient gas exchange.

8. Regulation of breathing and acid-base balance

Respiratory centres in the brainstem generate and adjust breathing patterns. Central and peripheral chemoreceptors respond to changes in carbon dioxide, hydrogen ion concentration and oxygen, with differing sensitivities. Increased carbon dioxide generally stimulates ventilation, helping reduce arterial carbon dioxide through exhalation.

The lungs regulate the carbon dioxide component of acid-base balance, while kidneys regulate bicarbonate and hydrogen ion handling over a longer timescale. Buffer systems reduce rapid pH changes. Acid-base interpretation uses pH, carbon dioxide and bicarbonate together; clinical decisions require training, context and current protocols.

9. Digestive system and nutrient absorption

The digestive tract extends from mouth to anus and includes oral cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine. Accessory organs include salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas. Mechanical and chemical digestion reduce food into absorbable molecules. Most nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine, whose folds, villi and microvilli increase surface area.

The stomach stores and mixes food and begins substantial protein digestion. The liver processes nutrients, produces bile, metabolises substances and synthesises important plasma proteins. The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile. The pancreas releases digestive enzymes and bicarbonate into the duodenum. The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes and houses a diverse microbiota.

10. Urinary system and renal physiology

The urinary system includes kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra. Each kidney contains cortex and medulla and functional units called nephrons. A nephron includes a renal corpuscle, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule and collecting system. Filtration moves water and small solutes from glomerular capillaries into Bowman's space; tubular reabsorption returns needed substances to blood; secretion adds selected substances to tubular fluid.

The kidneys regulate extracellular fluid volume, electrolytes, acid-base balance and blood pressure, and produce erythropoietin and renin while activating vitamin D. Urine concentration depends on countercurrent mechanisms and antidiuretic hormone. Urine output, fluid balance, renal laboratory tests and clinical signs must be interpreted together; thresholds for escalation follow local protocols.

11. Reproductive systems and human development

The reproductive system includes organs that produce gametes and support fertilisation and development. The ovaries produce oocytes and hormones; uterine tubes are common sites of fertilisation; the uterus supports implantation and pregnancy; the cervix connects uterus and vagina. The testes produce sperm and testosterone; ducts and accessory glands contribute to sperm transport and semen formation.

The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis regulates reproductive function through gonadotropin-releasing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone. Ovarian cycles involve follicular development, ovulation and luteal activity. Reproductive anatomy and physiology should be taught respectfully, recognising variation, consent, privacy and inclusive communication.

12. Integrated homeostasis

Homeostasis is dynamic regulation of the internal environment within ranges compatible with cellular function. Sensors detect changes, control centres integrate information and effectors produce responses. Negative feedback opposes a disturbance; examples include thermoregulation, blood glucose regulation and blood pressure control. Positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint, as in selected reproductive events.

Organ systems interact continuously. Reduced cardiac output can affect renal perfusion; renal dysfunction can alter fluid and electrolyte balance; respiratory changes can affect acid-base status; endocrine signals influence metabolism and circulation. Nursing assessment therefore looks for patterns rather than treating each observation as isolated.

Integrated case-based learning

Case: A student nurse is assigned to observe an adult patient during a supervised learning placement. The patient has a change from their usual condition and expresses concern about the proposed care. The student must gather relevant information, listen respectfully, check identity and consent, consider immediate safety, communicate findings to the supervising nurse and document objectively.

Discussion: Identify the information that is subjective and objective. Which findings require prompt escalation? What additional context would help interpretation? How can the student preserve dignity and confidentiality? Which actions are within the student's authorised competence, and which require direct supervision? Explain how the chapter's concepts support safe decisions without attempting to diagnose or prescribe independently.

Learning point: Sound nursing practice combines scientific knowledge, careful observation, communication, ethical conduct, teamwork and timely escalation. A textbook scenario is not a substitute for local protocols or direct clinical supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For longer questions, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and relate the science to safe nursing care.

  1. Define nursing and identify four major purposes of nursing practice.

    Show answer guidance

    Nursing is a professional discipline that promotes health, prevents illness, supports recovery, relieves suffering and protects dignity. Examples include health promotion, prevention, care coordination and rehabilitation.

  2. Outline the five stages of the nursing process.

    Show answer guidance

    Assessment; analysis or nursing diagnosis; planning; implementation; evaluation. The process is iterative and should be revised when new information emerges.

  3. Differentiate autonomy and beneficence.

    Show answer guidance

    Autonomy concerns respect for a person's informed choices; beneficence concerns acting to promote wellbeing. Ethical decisions may require balancing these and other principles.

  4. State three features of effective therapeutic communication.

    Show answer guidance

    Active listening, open questions, clarification, empathy, summarising and checking understanding are examples.

  5. Explain why accurate documentation is important.

    Show answer guidance

    It supports continuity, communication, accountability, evaluation, legal record-keeping and patient safety.

  6. Differentiate anatomy from physiology.

    Show answer guidance

    Anatomy studies body structures and their relationships; physiology studies how those structures function.

  7. Name the four primary tissue classes.

    Show answer guidance

    Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

  8. Describe the role of the plasma membrane.

    Show answer guidance

    It forms a selectively permeable boundary, regulates transport, supports signalling and contributes to cell recognition.

  9. State two functions of the integumentary system.

    Show answer guidance

    Barrier protection, thermoregulation, sensation, limiting water loss and vitamin D synthesis.

  10. Explain the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems.

    Show answer guidance

    The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS.

  11. Trace the normal flow of blood through the heart.

    Show answer guidance

    Venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  12. What is cardiac output?

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute; it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.

  13. State the main function of alveoli.

    Show answer guidance

    Alveoli provide a large, thin surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

  14. Name the three basic processes of urine formation.

    Show answer guidance

    Glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion.

  15. List the links in the chain of infection.

    Show answer guidance

    Infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry and susceptible host.
