Postgraduate Mathematics

Advanced study notes and graduate problem sets for Master's and doctoral learners. The pathway spans pure mathematics, applied analysis, probability, computation and mathematical research.

← Back to Global Mathematics

Academic level and use

This is a graduate-level learning framework, not an accredited degree, official university syllabus, or substitute for a supervisor's guidance. Course depth and prerequisites differ by institution and research area. Learners should compare each module with their programme handbook and current course syllabus.

Postgraduate expectation: Work from definitions and hypotheses, prove central results, test the limits of theorems with counterexamples, connect results across fields, and communicate arguments in precise mathematical language. Computational work must record methods, software, parameters and verification checks.

Prerequisite profile: Strong undergraduate real analysis, linear algebra, abstract algebra, topology, probability and proof writing. Individual modules may require more specialised preparation.

01

Postgraduate topic 1 of 20

Measure theory and integration

Constructing measures and Lebesgue integration on abstract measure spaces.

Advanced study scope

Sigma-algebras; outer measure and Carathéodory extension; measurable functions; Lebesgue integral; monotone, Fatou and dominated convergence; product measures and Fubini–Tonelli; Radon–Nikodym and signed measures; modes of convergence; Lp spaces and duality.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Carathéodory extension theorem; monotone convergence; Fatou lemma; dominated convergence; Radon–Nikodym theorem; Fubini–Tonelli theorem.

Graduate problem set

  1. Let (X,Σ,μ) be a measure space. Prove that if f_n increases pointwise to f with f_n≥0, then ∫f_n dμ increases to ∫f dμ. State precisely where measurability is used.
  2. Construct a measurable function sequence that converges pointwise but for which the integrals do not converge to the integral of the pointwise limit. Identify the missing hypothesis.
  3. Prove that Lp(μ) is complete for 1≤p≤∞, or identify the additional assumptions needed for your chosen proof.
  4. For a finite measure space, compare convergence almost everywhere, in measure, and in Lp. Prove the valid implications and give counterexamples to the converses.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 2 of 20

Functional analysis and operator theory

Study Banach and Hilbert spaces, bounded operators and spectral structure.

Advanced study scope

Normed spaces and completions; dual spaces; Hahn–Banach separation; uniform boundedness, open mapping and closed graph theorems; weak and weak-star topologies; compact operators; adjoints; spectrum and resolvent; self-adjoint and normal operators; Fredholm theory; unbounded operators and domains.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Hahn–Banach theorem; Banach–Steinhaus; open mapping theorem; spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators; Fredholm alternative.

Graduate problem set

  1. Prove that every bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space has a unique Riesz representation. Explain why completeness is essential.
  2. Let T be compact on an infinite-dimensional Banach space. Prove that T cannot be boundedly invertible on the whole space.
  3. For a bounded operator T, establish that the spectrum is nonempty and compact over a complex Banach space, stating the analytic ingredients.
  4. Construct an operator whose range is not closed and analyse how this obstructs a bounded inverse.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 3 of 20

Advanced real and complex analysis

Develop higher analysis through differentiation, integration, harmonicity and complex function theory.

Advanced study scope

Uniform and absolute convergence; equicontinuity and Arzelà–Ascoli; implicit and inverse function theorems; Fourier analysis; distributions; harmonic and subharmonic functions; Cauchy theory and residues; normal families; analytic continuation; conformal maps; Riemann surfaces and meromorphic differentials.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Arzelà–Ascoli theorem; inverse function theorem; Cauchy integral formula; residue theorem; maximum modulus principle; Montel theorem; identity theorem.

Graduate problem set

  1. Use the residue theorem to evaluate an explicitly chosen real integral with poles off the real axis. Justify contour choice and limiting arc contribution.
  2. Prove a normal-family convergence result using Montel's theorem, and distinguish local uniform convergence from pointwise convergence.
  3. Show how a distributional derivative extends classical differentiation and compute the distributional derivative of a piecewise smooth function with a jump.
  4. State and prove a version of the identity theorem, then apply it to analytic continuation on a connected domain.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 4 of 20

Advanced linear and multilinear algebra

Investigate vector spaces, tensor constructions, canonical forms and structure-preserving maps.

Advanced study scope

Duality and annihilators; tensor, symmetric and exterior algebras; multilinear maps; canonical forms; Jordan and rational canonical forms; bilinear and sesquilinear forms; spectral decompositions; matrix perturbation; singular values; determinants and exterior powers.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Rank–nullity; Jordan decomposition over suitable fields; Cayley–Hamilton; spectral theorem; canonical-form uniqueness statements.

Graduate problem set

  1. Derive the Jordan form of a parameterised family of matrices and identify parameter values where the Jordan structure changes.
  2. Prove the singular value decomposition for a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space and explain its relation to the spectral theorem for A* A.
  3. Use tensor products to express a bilinear map as a linear map on a tensor product; verify the universal property.
  4. Classify nondegenerate symmetric bilinear forms over the real numbers and explain which parts change over other fields.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 5 of 20

Ring, field and module theory

Build structural algebra from ideals, modules, field extensions and exact sequences.

Advanced study scope

Ideals and quotient rings; Noetherian and Artinian conditions; PID and UFD; localization; modules and presentations; tensor products; exact sequences; field extensions; splitting fields; separability; Galois groups; integral extensions; commutative algebra foundations.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Isomorphism theorems; Hilbert basis theorem; structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID; fundamental theorem of Galois theory; Nakayama lemma.

Graduate problem set

  1. Prove the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID in a form suitable for classifying finitely generated abelian groups.
  2. Compute a Galois group for a selected polynomial over Q and justify the group identification using factorisation and resolvents or a valid alternative.
  3. Give an example showing why localisation can simplify a module or ring problem; compute the localisation explicitly.
  4. Use Nakayama's lemma to prove a finite-generation result for a local ring, carefully stating the hypotheses.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

  2. Prove Maschke's theorem and explain exactly why the characteristic condition matters.
  3. Classify groups of a specified small order using Sylow constraints, explicitly showing all cases and exclusions.
  4. Use a group action to derive a counting identity and explain how the orbit decomposition yields it.

Algebraic and analytic number theory

Connect arithmetic structures with analytic tools and distribution of primes.

Advanced study scope

Dedekind domains and ideals; algebraic integers; norms and traces; ideal class groups; units; splitting of primes; zeta and L-functions; Dirichlet characters; analytic properties of the zeta function; prime number theorem framework; sieve methods; Diophantine approximation and modular forms introductions.

  2. Explain the Euler product for ζ(s), establish its initial domain of absolute convergence and show how unique factorisation enters.
  3. For a quadratic number field, compute its ring of integers, discriminant and factorisation of selected rational primes.
  4. Compare an elementary and an analytic strategy for a prime-distribution question; state what each method can and cannot establish.

Algebraic topology and homological methods

Use algebraic invariants to distinguish spaces and organise geometric information.

Advanced study scope

Graduate problem set

  3. Construct a chain homotopy in a simple example and prove that chain-homotopic maps induce the same map on homology.
  4. Explain how a spectral sequence organises a filtered complex and work through a small illustrative filtration.

Differential geometry and manifolds

Develop smooth manifolds, tangent structures, differential forms and curvature.

Advanced study scope

  3. Compute the curvature tensor of a standard constant-curvature model and interpret sectional curvature.
  4. Apply Stokes' theorem on an oriented manifold with boundary, checking orientation conventions.

  2. Show that a simple random walk stopped at a suitable hitting time is a martingale; calculate the stopping expectation where justified.
  3. Use Itô's formula on a twice differentiable function of Brownian motion and derive the associated drift and martingale terms.
  4. Establish a martingale convergence result under a stated uniform-integrability or boundedness assumption.

  4. Construct a concentration bound for a sum of independent bounded random variables and discuss sharpness.

Ergodic theory and dynamical systems

Analyse long-term behaviour of measure-preserving and smooth dynamical systems.

  2. Apply Birkhoff's theorem to an ergodic transformation and interpret almost-sure time averages.
  3. Distinguish ergodicity, mixing and weak mixing by definitions and examples.
  4. Analyse the invariant measures of a simple circle rotation and determine when the system is ergodic.

Advanced numerical analysis

  3. Compare finite difference and finite element formulations for a boundary-value problem, including approximation spaces and error norms.
  4. Design a reproducible numerical experiment that separates discretisation error, solver error and floating-point effects.

Convex and nonlinear optimisation

  4. Give an example where KKT conditions fail to be sufficient because a required regularity or convexity assumption is absent.

Postgraduate topic 18 of 20

Research seminars, literature review and proof writing

Move from learning established theory to communicating and evaluating mathematical research.

Advanced study scope

Reading research papers; identifying definitions and dependencies; theorem-proof architecture; counterexample design; mathematical exposition; LaTeX; seminar talks; referee-style critique; citation practice; open-problem framing; research proposal and oral defence.

Core theorems, methods and standards

No single universal theorem: assessment focuses on correct reconstruction, logical completeness, attribution, critical comparison and a defensible research question.

Graduate problem set

  1. Select a theorem from a research paper and write a self-contained proof map: prerequisites, lemmas, dependencies, main argument and limitations.
  2. Prepare a critical literature review comparing at least three research papers on one narrowly defined mathematical question; distinguish established results from conjectures.
  3. Rewrite a dense proof for clarity without weakening hypotheses or omitting logical steps; annotate every dependency.
  4. Deliver a 20-minute seminar plan with motivation, precise statements, proof architecture, examples and open questions.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 19 of 20

Research design, reproducibility and scholarly communication

Design mathematical research that is transparent, verifiable and responsibly communicated.

Advanced study scope

Research question and scope; literature search strategy; proof verification; computational mathematics; code and data provenance; version control; numerical reproducibility; research integrity; authorship and citation; preprints and peer review; LaTeX manuscripts; open research artefacts.

Core theorems, methods and standards

Research quality is judged through verifiable reasoning, transparent methods, accurate attribution, reproducible computation where applicable and clear communication—not a single theorem.

Graduate problem set

  1. Write a research protocol for a computational mathematics project specifying algorithms, software versions, parameters, test cases, error checks and archival artefacts.
  2. Audit a mathematical manuscript for unsupported claims, missing assumptions, citation gaps and non-reproducible computational results.
  3. Design a version-controlled workflow for a collaborative proof or computational project, including review and release practices.
  4. Draft an abstract and methods section that distinguish theorem-proof results from numerical evidence and conjectural interpretation.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Postgraduate topic 20 of 20

Dissertation and doctoral research

Plan, execute and defend a substantial original mathematical investigation.

Advanced study scope

Problem selection and significance; proposal and research questions; background and literature review; preliminary lemmas; originality and contribution; proof or computational methodology; chapter architecture; limitations; publication strategy; supervisor and committee feedback; thesis writing and oral defence.

Core theorems, methods and standards

A dissertation must make a clearly delimited, defensible contribution appropriate to its degree and institution; originality, scope and examination rules are programme-specific.

Graduate problem set

  1. Draft a dissertation proposal with problem statement, motivation, literature gap, precise objectives, methods, expected contribution, risks and timeline.
  2. Construct a chapter plan for a thesis proving a new result, showing where definitions, lemmas, main theorem, examples and limitations belong.
  3. Prepare an oral-defence examination with questions probing novelty, assumptions, proof gaps, alternative approaches and future work.
  4. Write a reproducibility and verification appendix for a thesis containing computational experiments or computer-assisted proofs.

Expected postgraduate standard: State all hypotheses; define notation; justify every inference; distinguish theorem, conjecture and example; provide complete proofs or a transparent computational argument; cite sources for results not proved.

Extended lecture framework for all 20 postgraduate topics

Each topic now has a dedicated graduate lecture framework for formal definitions, theorem proof development, worked-example method and assessed exercises. Topic-specific proof solutions and extended problem banks should be developed alongside these frameworks.

Lecture 1: Measure theory and integration

Definitions and foundations

Begin with precise definitions, ambient spaces, domains, codomains, regularity assumptions and notation. State all standing hypotheses before using any result. Distinguish algebraic, topological, measure-theoretic and analytic structures relevant to this subject.

Theorem and proof development

For each central theorem in this area, write the exact statement with every hypothesis, identify prerequisite lemmas, prove each implication, and explain where completeness, compactness, measurability, finite dimensionality or other assumptions enter. Include a counterexample when an essential hypothesis is removed.

Worked-example method

Develop a nontrivial example from the definitions, compute intermediate objects explicitly, justify each transformation and verify the final result independently. Include a second example where a tempting method fails and explain the obstruction.

Graduate exercise and marking guide

Exercise: formulate and prove a proposition in this topic, then apply it to a new example and construct a counterexample to an overgeneralised version. Marking: definitions and hypotheses 20%; proof structure 35%; calculations or construction 20%; counterexample and limitations 15%; mathematical exposition and citations 10%.

Additional Master's and doctoral mathematical problem bank

Fresh, calculation-led and proof-oriented problems extending the existing graduate lectures and sample examinations. State all hypotheses, justify theorem use, and distinguish a numerical observation from a rigorous conclusion.

Real analysis, measure theory and functional analysis

  1. Uniform integrability (12 marks). Let (fn) be a family in L¹(μ). State the definition of uniform integrability and prove that Lp-boundedness for some p>1 on a finite measure space implies uniform integrability. Give an example showing why an L¹ bound alone is insufficient.
  2. Weak convergence (12 marks). In a Hilbert space H, prove that every weakly convergent sequence is norm-bounded. Then show that weak convergence need not imply norm convergence by constructing a sequence in ℓ².
  3. Compact operators (12 marks). Let T be compact and self-adjoint on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Prove that every nonzero spectral value is an eigenvalue of finite multiplicity and explain why 0 may remain in the spectrum.
  4. Fourier analysis (12 marks). For f(x)=x on (−π,π), compute its Fourier coefficients, establish the pointwise convergence value at the jump under the Dirichlet theorem, and discuss convergence in L².

Algebra, topology and geometry

  1. Galois theory (12 marks). Determine the splitting field and Galois group of x⁴−2 over ℚ. Identify the real and complex roots, describe generators of the extension and justify the degree.
  2. Modules over a PID (12 marks). Classify the finitely generated abelian group ℤ/12ℤ ⊕ ℤ/18ℤ in invariant-factor form. Determine its order, exponent and number of elements of order exactly 6.
  3. Fundamental group (12 marks). Use van Kampen's theorem to compute the fundamental group of a wedge of two circles. Describe the reduced-word representation and explain why the group is non-abelian.
  4. Differential geometry (12 marks). For the surface z=f(x,y) with f(x,y)=½(ax²+by²), compute the first and second fundamental forms at the origin and determine the principal curvatures with a consistent choice of unit normal.

Probability, PDE and stochastic analysis

  1. Conditional expectation (12 marks). Let X be integrable and 𝒢 a sub-σ-algebra. Prove the defining uniqueness of E[X|𝒢] almost surely and derive the tower property for 𝒢⊂ℋ. Identify where integrability is used.
  2. Brownian motion (12 marks). For standard Brownian motion Bt, use Itô's formula to derive the stochastic differential of exp(λBt−½λ²t). Explain why the resulting process is a martingale and state any integrability or localisation qualification needed.
  3. Elliptic PDE (12 marks). Consider −Δu=f in a bounded domain Ω with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Formulate the weak problem in H₀¹(Ω), prove uniqueness using the energy method, and state conditions sufficient for existence by Lax–Milgram.
  4. Heat equation (12 marks). Solve ut=κuxx on 0<x<L with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions and initial data u(x,0)=sin(2πx/L). Derive the solution and its decay rate.

Numerical analysis, optimisation and research mathematics

  1. Finite-element error (12 marks). For a coercive variational problem and conforming finite-element space Vh, state Céa's lemma and derive the quasi-optimal energy-norm estimate. Explain how interpolation estimates convert it into an h-rate.
  2. Convex duality (12 marks). Derive the Fenchel dual of minimising f(x)+g(Ax), state conditions for strong duality, and apply the result to a quadratic least-squares objective with an ℓ¹ penalty.
  3. Concentration inequality (12 marks). State Hoeffding's inequality for independent bounded random variables and use it to derive a sample-size bound ensuring P(|X̄−E X̄|≥ε)≤δ. Explain the role of boundedness and independence.
  4. Research-level counterexample (12 marks). Construct an explicit sequence of functions that converges pointwise but not uniformly on [0,1]. Determine whether convergence holds in L¹, and explain how the example demonstrates the distinction between pointwise and uniform convergence.

Graduate marking standard: definitions and hypotheses 15%; mathematical derivation/proof 40%; correct computation 20%; counterexamples or interpretation 15%; exposition and notation 10%. In research problems, cite any theorem imported rather than proved.

Research methodology and dissertation assessment

Critical literature review — 20 marks

Review at least eight primary sources on one focused question. Compare assumptions and methods, identify a research gap, and distinguish established results from conjectures. Marking: source quality 4; synthesis 6; gap 4; mathematical accuracy 4; citations 2.

Research proposal — 20 marks

Prepare 2,000–3,000 words: problem, motivation, questions, literature gap, definitions, method, expected contribution, feasibility, risks, timeline and references. Marking: significance 4; precision 4; literature 3; method 4; feasibility 3; presentation 2.

Proof portfolio and referee report — 20 marks

Reconstruct a substantial theorem, map dependencies, provide a proof or label imported lemmas, test boundary cases and write a referee-style critique. Marking: correctness 8; completeness 4; critique 4; exposition 4.

Reproducible computational investigation — 15 marks

Submit code, environment versions, data provenance, seeds, precision, error analysis, tests and checkable outputs. For theoretical work, submit a proof-audit dossier. Marking: reproducibility 5; verification 4; analysis 4; documentation 2.

Dissertation and oral defence — 25 marks

Present original contribution, theorem claims, proof architecture, limitations and future directions. Defend novelty, assumptions, counterexamples and alternative methods in a 20–30-minute viva. Marking: contribution 6; validity 8; literature 3; limitations 3; defence 5.

Integrity: Cite sources, label reproduced results, and do not present conjectures or numerical evidence as proved original mathematics. Institutional degree rules take precedence.
