1. Scope and periodic organisation of the main group

Main-group chemistry concerns the s- and p-block elements, whose valence electrons occupy ns and np orbitals. The p block spans groups 13–18 and includes metals, metalloids and non-metals, so no single bonding model describes all of its compounds. The chemistry is governed by valence-shell configuration, periodicity, accessible oxidation states, bond energies, molecular geometry and the stability of extended solids. A sound approach begins with electronic structure, then connects it to observed structures and reactivity rather than memorising isolated reactions.

2. Atomic structure and valence electron configurations

Write ground-state configurations using the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle and Hund's rule, while remembering that measured configurations can show exceptions where subshell energies are close. For p-block atoms the valence configuration is ns²np¹ to ns²np⁶. Core electrons shield nuclear charge imperfectly; the resulting effective nuclear charge helps explain atomic size, ionisation energy and electronegativity. In heavier atoms, relativistic effects and poor shielding by d and f electrons can modify simple periodic predictions.

3. Periodic trends: effective nuclear charge and shielding

Across a period, effective nuclear charge generally increases because proton number rises while added electrons enter the same principal shell. Atomic radii therefore tend to contract, and ionisation energies usually rise, although subshell and electron-pairing effects cause irregularities. Down a group, additional shells and shielding usually increase size. These are trends, not laws: compare like-for-like species, distinguish covalent from metallic radii, and account for charge and coordination number when interpreting ionic radii.

4. Atomic and ionic radii across the p block

Atomic radius is not a directly observable hard boundary. Covalent radii are inferred from bond lengths, metallic radii from crystal structures, and van der Waals radii from non-bonded contacts. Cations are generally smaller than their parent atoms, while anions are larger, because electron removal or addition changes electron–electron repulsion and sometimes the occupied shell. For isoelectronic ions, radius decreases as nuclear charge increases. This reasoning is useful when predicting lattice dimensions and coordination environments.

5. Ionisation energies and electron affinities

Ionisation energy is the enthalpy required to remove an electron from a gaseous species. Successive ionisation energies increase, with a large jump after all valence electrons have been removed. Electron affinity describes the energy change when a gaseous atom accepts an electron; sign conventions differ between sources, so state the convention. Subshell stability, electron pairing and electron–electron repulsion explain departures from smooth trends. Neither quantity alone determines whether a salt forms: lattice and solvation energies also matter.

6. Electronegativity, polarity and bond character

Electronegativity measures an atom's tendency to attract shared electrons in a bond. Pauling values are relative and depend on bond-energy comparisons; Mulliken and other scales use different definitions. Greater electronegativity differences often increase bond polarity, but ionic character is continuous rather than a sharp category. Molecular dipole moments also depend on geometry: polar bonds can cancel in a symmetric molecule. Use vector addition of bond dipoles rather than inferring molecular polarity from formula alone.

7. Oxidation states and the inert-pair effect

Main-group elements exhibit oxidation states that reflect valence electron counts, bond polarity and compound stability. Heavier p-block elements often favour oxidation states two units below the group maximum; this is associated with the inert-pair effect, in which the ns² pair is less available for bonding. The effect becomes important down groups 13–16, but its magnitude depends on the element and ligand environment. Oxidation state is formal bookkeeping, not a direct measurement of atomic charge.

8. Catenation and multiple bonding

Catenation is the formation of bonds between atoms of the same element. Carbon shows extensive catenation because C–C bonds are strong and carbon forms stable multiple bonds. Silicon forms chains and networks, but Si–Si multiple bonding is less favourable and oxygen-containing networks dominate much of its chemistry. Multiple bonding depends on orbital overlap and energy matching; pπ–pπ overlap is strongest for compact second-period atoms. Do not assume heavier congeners reproduce carbon chemistry.

9. Hydrides: structure, bonding and trends

Hydrides reveal systematic changes in bond strength, polarity, structure and acid–base behaviour. Group 15 hydrides are commonly pyramidal, while group 16 hydrides are bent and group 17 hydrogen halides are linear. Down a group, bond strengths often decrease as bonds lengthen, which can increase thermal instability and acidity in water. Basicity trends may differ from gas-phase proton affinity because solvation and hydrogen bonding affect aqueous equilibria.

10. Halides and interhalogen compounds

Binary halides range from ionic solids to covalent molecular compounds. Their structures depend on cation size, charge density, electronegativity and the availability of acceptor orbitals. Interhalogens such as XY, XY₃ and XY₅ display geometries that can be rationalised with electron-domain models, while heavier systems may require molecular-orbital descriptions. Hydrolysis can be vigorous, particularly for electron-deficient covalent halides; reactions must be interpreted with moisture sensitivity and corrosivity in mind.

11. Oxides and their acid–base behaviour

Oxides span basic, amphoteric, acidic and nearly neutral behaviour. Metallic oxides are often basic, non-metal oxides often acidic, and metalloids commonly form amphoteric or network oxides, but oxidation state and structure matter. An oxide's reaction with water is not always a reliable acidity test because some are insoluble or kinetically inert. Acid–base behaviour can be assessed through reactions with acids and bases, oxide-ion transfer, and the structure of the solid.

12. Oxoacids and oxyanions

Oxoacids contain oxygen bonded to a central element, often with O–H groups that can ionise. Their acid strength depends on bond polarisation, oxidation state, number of terminal oxo groups, resonance stabilisation of the conjugate base and solvation. Oxyanion charge is delocalised over several oxygen atoms in many cases. Compare species with the same central atom carefully; increasing oxidation state often strengthens acidity, but structural and solvent effects remain important.

13. Group 13: boron chemistry

Boron is electron-deficient in many compounds because it has three valence electrons and commonly forms three-coordinate, six-electron environments. Its chemistry includes covalent trihalides, boric acid, borates, boranes and strong Lewis-acid adducts. Boron compounds are often polymeric or cluster-based rather than simple ionic salts. The small size of boron and effective overlap with oxygen and fluorine contribute to distinctive bonding and high affinity for hard donor atoms.

14. Boranes and electron-deficient bonding

Boranes contain multicentre bonding because conventional two-centre two-electron bonds cannot account for their electron-deficient frameworks. Diborane, B₂H₆, contains bridging B–H–B three-centre two-electron bonds in addition to terminal B–H bonds. Wade–Mingos rules relate skeletal electron pairs to cluster geometry for many boranes and related clusters. Apply these rules by identifying the cluster class and skeletal electron count, while checking the assumptions and exceptions for the species under study.

15. Boron halides and Lewis acidity

Boron trihalides are planar, electron-deficient Lewis acids. Their Lewis acidity does not follow a simplistic electronegativity argument: π donation from halogen lone pairs into an empty boron orbital influences the strength of the B–X bond and acceptor behaviour. BF₃ is often a weaker Lewis acid than BCl₃ in common adduct comparisons because F-to-B π donation is particularly effective. Solvent, donor identity and measurement method can change the apparent order.

16. Aluminium chemistry and applications

Aluminium commonly forms Al³⁺ compounds, but its high charge density gives substantial covalent character in many halides and organoaluminium compounds. AlCl₃ exists as molecular Al₂Cl₆ under some conditions and as different species in donor solvents. Aluminium oxide is amphoteric and forms a protective surface layer, explaining corrosion resistance in many environments. Industrial extraction uses the Hall–Héroult process, whose energy demand motivates recycling and lower-carbon electricity.

34. VSEPR and molecular geometry

Then connect the proposed bonding model to measurable evidence such as bond lengths, bond angles, magnetic behaviour, conductivity, equilibrium constants or spectra. If two models appear possible, state what observation would distinguish them.

Worked interpretation. For a comparison question, identify the dominant variable and hold the others as constant as possible. A trend across a period may reflect increasing effective nuclear charge, but a change in subshell occupancy or electron pairing can create an exception. A trend down a group may reflect increasing size and shielding, but inert-pair effects and relativistic contributions can modify oxidation-state stability. Explain both the general trend and the reason the specific example may depart from it.

Applications and limitations. Connect the chemical principle to synthesis, materials, biological systems, environmental chemistry or industrial processing. Distinguish thermodynamic favourability from reaction rate, and distinguish formal oxidation state from measured charge density. Models such as VSEPR, hybridisation and hard–soft acid–base theory are useful organising tools, not substitutes for experimental data. In advanced work, combine them with molecular orbital reasoning, thermodynamic measurements and structural characterisation.

Revision checkpoint. Be able to define the key terms, write a balanced representative equation, predict a plausible structure, explain the controlling trend, and identify at least one limitation or exception. A complete undergraduate answer should show the reasoning chain rather than merely state a memorised order. Include units in numerical work and make assumptions explicit.

Worked example 1

Question: Predict the shape of BF₃ and explain its Lewis acidity.

Solution: BF₃ is trigonal planar with an electron-deficient boron centre. It accepts an electron pair to complete the boron valence shell. Strong F→B π donation moderates its Lewis acidity relative to some heavier boron trihalides; donor and solvent must be specified.

Structured revision questions with answers

Use the model answers as marking guidance. In examinations, show equations, assumptions, units and the reasoning that links structure to properties.

Question 1

Define effective nuclear charge and explain its role in periodic trends.

Model answer / marking guidance: Effective nuclear charge is the net attraction experienced by an electron after shielding. Its general increase across a period contracts valence orbitals and influences size, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

Question 2

Explain the inert-pair effect and give one chemical consequence.

Model answer / marking guidance: The ns² pair in heavier p-block elements can be less available for bonding. Consequently lower oxidation states, such as Tl(I), Pb(II) and Bi(III), can be favoured over the group maximum.

Question 3

Compare covalent, metallic and van der Waals radii.

Model answer / marking guidance: Covalent radii derive from bonded distances, metallic radii from metal structures, and van der Waals radii from non-bonded contacts. They are operational definitions and are not interchangeable.

Question 4

Explain why ionisation energies do not increase perfectly smoothly across a period.

Model answer / marking guidance: Subshell energies and electron pairing create exceptions. Removing a p electron or a paired electron can require less energy than a simple across-period trend predicts.

Question 5

Distinguish electronegativity from electron affinity.

Model answer / marking guidance: Electronegativity describes attraction for shared electrons in a bond; electron affinity is an energy change for adding an electron to a gaseous atom. They have different definitions and units.

Question 6

Explain catenation and compare carbon with silicon.

Model answer / marking guidance: Catenation is element–element bonding. Carbon forms strong C–C chains and multiple bonds; silicon more commonly forms strong Si–O networks and less extensive Si–Si multiple bonding.

Question 7

Describe the bonding in diborane using multicentre bonding.

Model answer / marking guidance: Diborane contains terminal B–H bonds and two bridging three-centre two-electron B–H–B bonds, which distribute electron density over three atoms.

Question 8

Why can BF₃ behave as a Lewis acid?

Model answer / marking guidance: Boron in BF₃ has an incomplete valence shell and can accept a donor lone pair. F-to-B π donation affects its Lewis acidity and makes donor/solvent comparisons important.

Question 9

Explain the amphoteric behaviour of aluminium oxide.

Model answer / marking guidance: Al₂O₃ reacts with acids to form aluminium salts and with strong bases to form aluminate species, showing both basic and acidic behaviour.

Question 10

Describe the structural units in silicate minerals.

Model answer / marking guidance: Silicate minerals contain SiO₄ tetrahedra linked by shared oxygen atoms into isolated units, chains, sheets or frameworks, with cations balancing charge.

Question 11

Compare nitrogen and phosphorus in multiple-bond formation.

Model answer / marking guidance: N₂ has strong pπ–pπ multiple bonding, whereas phosphorus is larger and favours single-bond frameworks and higher coordination. Orbital overlap explains much of the contrast.

Question 12

Explain why ammonia is a Lewis base.

Model answer / marking guidance: Ammonia has a lone pair on nitrogen that can accept a proton or coordinate to an electron-pair acceptor.

Question 13

Discuss the environmental significance of nitrogen oxides.

Model answer / marking guidance: NOx contributes to photochemical smog, acid deposition and atmospheric ozone chemistry. NO₂ participates in radical cycles and reacts to form nitrate species.

Question 14

Compare white, red and black phosphorus.

Model answer / marking guidance: White phosphorus is molecular P₄ and highly reactive; red phosphorus is polymeric and less reactive; black phosphorus is layered and has anisotropic properties.

Question 15

Explain why phosphorous acid is diprotic.

Model answer / marking guidance: H₃PO₃ has two P–OH groups and one P–H bond. Only the two O–H hydrogens are ionisable in ordinary aqueous acid–base chemistry.

Question 16

Compare oxygen and sulfur in catenation and oxidation states.

Model answer / marking guidance: Oxygen has strong multiple bonding and limited catenation; sulfur forms S–S rings/chains and a wider range of oxidation states and coordination environments.

Question 17

Distinguish peroxide and superoxide ions.

Model answer / marking guidance: Peroxide is O₂²⁻ with an O–O single bond; superoxide is O₂⁻ with one unpaired electron and distinct magnetic/reactivity properties.

Question 18

Describe the lambda transition in sulfur.

Model answer / marking guidance: Heating sulfur opens S₈ rings and produces longer chains; changes in chain length and entanglement cause a sharp viscosity change.

Question 19

Outline the Contact process and its equilibrium considerations.

Model answer / marking guidance: The Contact process oxidises SO₂ to SO₃ catalytically, then absorbs SO₃ to produce sulfuric acid. Temperature and gas composition balance rate and equilibrium yield.

Question 20

Explain the oxidising trend of the halogens in aqueous solution.

Model answer / marking guidance: Fluorine is generally the strongest oxidant in aqueous halogen comparisons, with oxidising strength decreasing down the group. Hydration and bond energies both contribute.

Question 21

Predict the geometry of ICl₃ and justify it with electron domains.

Model answer / marking guidance: ICl₃ is T-shaped: five electron domains around iodine, with two equatorial lone pairs and three bonding domains in a trigonal-bipyramidal arrangement.

Question 22

What evidence disproves the claim that noble gases are inert?

Model answer / marking guidance: Xenon forms stable fluorides and oxides under controlled conditions, showing that sufficiently strong oxidants can overcome the high ionisation energy of heavier noble gases.

Question 23

Describe the shape and bonding in XeF₄.

Model answer / marking guidance: XeF₄ is square planar; six electron domains are arranged octahedrally with two trans lone pairs and four Xe–F bonds.

Question 24

Define Lewis acidity and discuss solvent effects on adduct formation.

Model answer / marking guidance: A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair and a Lewis base donates one. Solvents compete for binding and can change measured adduct equilibria.

Question 25

State the limitations of VSEPR theory.

Model answer / marking guidance: VSEPR is qualitative, does not calculate precise bond angles, and can be unreliable for delocalised or hypercoordinate bonding without additional orbital models.

Question 26

Explain why hybridisation is a model rather than a directly measured observable.

Model answer / marking guidance: Hybridisation is a convenient valence-bond construction used to rationalise directionality; it is not a directly observable partition of electron density.

Question 27

Describe three-centre four-electron bonding.

Model answer / marking guidance: Three-centre four-electron bonding delocalises four electrons over three aligned atoms and helps explain linear hypervalent species such as XeF₂ and I₃⁻.

Question 28

Explain how hydrogen bonding affects boiling points.

Model answer / marking guidance: Hydrogen bonding strengthens intermolecular attraction, often raising boiling points and affecting viscosity, solubility and crystal packing.

Question 29

Distinguish thermodynamic and kinetic stability with an example.

Model answer / marking guidance: Thermodynamic stability compares Gibbs energies; kinetic stability depends on activation barriers. A metastable compound may persist because decomposition is slow.

Question 30

Explain the role of lattice energy in ionic compound formation.

Model answer / marking guidance: Lattice energy stabilises an ionic crystal and increases in magnitude with greater ionic charges and smaller ionic separations, subject to crystal structure.

Question 31

Describe hydrolysis of a highly charged metal cation.

Model answer / marking guidance: Highly charged cations polarise coordinated water, promoting O–H bond cleavage and release of H⁺; subsequent hydrolysis can produce several species.

Question 32

Balance a representative disproportionation reaction.

Model answer / marking guidance: Disproportionation involves simultaneous oxidation and reduction of one element. A balanced example is Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O.

Question 33

List key controls in moisture-sensitive inorganic synthesis.

Model answer / marking guidance: Use dry glassware, compatible seals, an inert atmosphere where needed, controlled reagent addition, temperature monitoring and appropriate quenching and waste procedures.

Question 34

Model answer / marking guidance: Aluminium production consumes substantial electrical energy and creates process emissions. Recycling and low-carbon electricity can reduce life-cycle impacts.

Question 35

Explain the biological roles of phosphorus and sulfur.

Model answer / marking guidance: Phosphorus is central to ATP, nucleic acids and phospholipids; sulfur occurs in amino acids and cofactors. Biological function depends on chemical form and dose.

Question 36

Distinguish hazard from risk in inorganic chemical safety.

Model answer / marking guidance: Hazard is an intrinsic capacity to cause harm; risk combines hazard with exposure likelihood and magnitude. Route, dose, duration and speciation matter.

Question 38

Compare IR and Raman selection rules.

Model answer / marking guidance: IR absorption generally requires a changing dipole moment; Raman scattering requires a changing polarisability. Their complementary selection rules make them useful together.

Model answer / marking guidance: Single-crystal X-ray diffraction establishes crystalline atomic arrangement, bond lengths and angles, subject to data quality, disorder and refinement limitations.

Advanced Main-Group Chemistry: Bonding Models, Reactivity and Laboratory Analysis

This extension develops the electronic, structural and thermodynamic reasoning used to explain p-block chemistry. Main-group behaviour should be interpreted through valence-shell configuration, orbital interactions, molecular geometry, acid–base character, redox thermodynamics and experimentally observed speciation.

Expanded theoretical treatment

1. The inert-pair effect: an energetic explanation

The reluctance of heavier p-block elements to use their ns² electrons in bonding is not simply an absolute inability to form higher oxidation states. It reflects the combined effects of orbital contraction, poor shielding by intervening d and f electrons, relativistic stabilisation in the heaviest elements, and the energetic cost of promoting or involving the s pair. The relative stability of oxidation states is therefore a thermodynamic question that depends on bond energies, lattice energies, solvation and reaction conditions. For example, the +1 oxidation state becomes increasingly important down Group 13, while +2 becomes more stable down Group 14.

2. Electron-deficient bonding in boranes

Diborane, B₂H₆, cannot be described adequately using only localised two-centre, two-electron bonds. Its two bridging hydrogens participate in three-centre, two-electron B–H–B interactions. Molecular orbital treatment combines suitable boron and hydrogen orbitals into bonding, non-bonding and antibonding combinations. Electron counting must include bridge bonds as delocalised units rather than assigning an ordinary two-electron bond independently to each B–H contact. This framework extends to larger boranes and Wade–Mingos skeletal electron-pair rules.

3. Catenation and multiple bonding across the p block

Catenation depends on the strength and polarity of element–element bonds, atomic size and the availability of effective orbital overlap. Carbon forms strong C–C bonds and stable multiple bonds through efficient 2p–2p overlap. Heavier congeners generally show weaker π bonding because larger, more diffuse orbitals overlap less effectively side-on. This trend helps explain why carbon readily forms chains and rings, whereas silicon chemistry favours extended networks and oxygen-bridged structures.

4. Acid–base character of oxides and oxyacids

Oxide acid–base character reflects the polarising power of the element, oxidation state and bonding environment. Across a period, increasing effective nuclear charge and electronegativity often increase covalent character and acidic oxide behaviour. Down a group, metallic character often increases and basic oxide behaviour becomes more prominent. For oxyacids, acidity is influenced by the electron-withdrawing power of the central atom and the number of terminal oxo groups; conjugate-base stabilisation is a key explanation, but solvent and concentration must be specified when comparing measured acid strengths.

5. Hydrides: structure, acidity and reducing behaviour

Main-group hydrides vary in bond polarity, bond strength, molecular geometry, thermal stability and acid–base behaviour. Down a group, E–H bond strength often decreases as the central atom becomes larger, which can increase thermal decomposition or hydride acidity. Across a period, electronegativity influences bond polarity and proton-transfer behaviour. Reducing power is not determined by one periodic trend alone; it depends on the thermodynamics and kinetics of the specific redox reaction.

6. Halogen and interhalogen reactivity

Halogen oxidising ability in aqueous solution is governed by the overall free-energy change, which includes atomisation, electron affinity and hydration contributions—not electron affinity alone. Interhalogen structures are predicted using electron-pair geometry, while their reactivity reflects polar X–Y bonds and accessible reaction pathways. Hydrolysis, disproportionation and ligand transfer may occur depending on medium and reagent ratios.

7. Silicates and extended solid-state structures

Silicate chemistry is organised around SiO₄ tetrahedra linked by shared oxygen atoms. The ratio of bridging to non-bridging oxygen controls connectivity, charge balance and the need for counter-cations. Isolated tetrahedra, chains, sheets and three-dimensional frameworks have distinct stoichiometries and physical properties. In glass and ceramic chemistry, network modifiers disrupt Si–O–Si connectivity and alter viscosity, thermal expansion and chemical durability.

8. Redox speciation and Pourbaix reasoning

Main-group elements can form multiple oxidation states and oxyanions whose stability depends on pH and potential. A Pourbaix diagram maps thermodynamically predominant species under specified conditions; it does not directly predict reaction rates. Balanced half-reactions must include H₂O and H⁺ in acidic media or OH⁻ in alkaline media, and electron accounting should be checked for both atoms and charge.

University-level derivation

For a generic oxyacid HₙEOₘ, a useful first step is to write its successive deprotonation equilibria: HₙEOₘ ⇌ H⁺ + Hₙ₋₁EOₘ⁻, followed by further ionisations. For a monoprotic acid HA, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. If the analytical concentration is C and x dissociates, then Ka = x²/(C−x). When x/C is small, C−x ≈ C and x ≈ √(KaC), giving pH ≈ ½(pKa−log C). The approximation must be checked after calculation: if x/C is not small (commonly above about 5%), solve the quadratic x² + Ka x − Ka C = 0, giving x = [−Ka + √(Ka²+4KaC)]/2. For polyprotic oxyacids, each ionisation has its own equilibrium constant and overlapping equilibria may require a full mass-balance and charge-balance treatment.

Laboratory experiment and data interpretation

Investigate acid–base behaviour of selected main-group oxides or oxyacid salts using a calibrated pH meter and standardised titrant. Prepare solutions at known analytical concentration, calibrate the electrode with at least two appropriate buffers, maintain constant temperature, and record pH after each measured addition. Plot pH against titrant volume and identify buffer regions and equivalence points. Where suitable, compare conductivity or UV–visible response to infer speciation. For a solid oxide, document sample mass, particle size, solvent volume and equilibration time; incomplete dissolution must not be mistaken for weak acidity. Use appropriate PPE and segregate boron, phosphorus, sulfur and heavy-element waste according to institutional procedures.

Challenging structured examination questions with model answers

Question 1

A 0.100 M monoprotic acid has Ka = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵. Estimate its pH using the weak-acid approximation and verify the degree of ionisation.

Model answer

[H⁺] ≈ √(KaC) = √(1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻³ M, so pH ≈ 3.00. Degree of ionisation = 0.001/0.100 × 100 = 1.0%, so the approximation is reasonable.

Question 2

Explain why diborane is electron deficient and describe the bonding in its bridging hydrogens.

Model answer

Diborane has fewer valence electrons than would be required for six conventional localised B–H bonds plus a B–B bond. The two bridging hydrogens form three-centre, two-electron B–H–B bonds in which one electron pair is delocalised over three atoms.

Question 3

A silicate has isolated [SiO₄]⁴⁻ tetrahedra. Explain the charge-balance requirement and predict the role of counter-cations.

Model answer

Each isolated tetrahedron carries a formal −4 charge because four O²⁻ atoms are bonded to Si(IV) without shared bridging oxygen. Four monovalent cations, two divalent cations or an equivalent combination are required for charge balance. The cations stabilise the anionic framework and influence lattice properties.

Question 4

Why is electron affinity alone insufficient to rank halogen oxidising strength in water?

Model answer

Aqueous oxidising strength depends on the full Gibbs-energy balance, including atomisation, electron attachment and hydration/solvation of the halide and halogen species. Standard reduction potentials capture the overall process under defined standard conditions.

Question 5

A monoprotic acid of concentration 0.0200 M has Ka = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴. Calculate [H⁺] using the quadratic expression and the pH.

Model answer

x = [−0.0004 + √((0.0004)² + 4(0.0004)(0.0200))]/2 = 0.00263 M (approximately). pH = −log₁₀(0.00263) = 2.58. The ionisation fraction is about 13.2%, so the small-dissociation approximation would not be sufficiently accurate.

Question 6

Distinguish thermodynamic stability of an oxidation state from kinetic persistence.

Model answer

Thermodynamic stability compares Gibbs energies of species under specified conditions. Kinetic persistence concerns the activation barrier and rate of conversion. A species can be thermodynamically disfavoured yet persist because conversion is slow, or be thermodynamically favoured but form slowly.
