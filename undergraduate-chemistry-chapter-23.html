Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Oxidation is loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation state; reduction is gain of electrons and a decrease. The oxidising agent accepts electrons and is reduced; the reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidised. Oxidation-state bookkeeping is formal and does not always equal physical charge.

Balance redox equations by separating oxidation and reduction half-reactions. Balance atoms other than O and H, add H2O for oxygen, H+ for hydrogen in acid, then electrons for charge. In alkaline solution neutralise H+ with OH−, cancel water and verify both atoms and total charge.

A galvanic cell converts a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical work; an electrolytic cell uses an external supply to drive a non-spontaneous process. Oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrode signs differ between the two cell types.

Cell notation places the anode on the left and cathode on the right. A single vertical line marks a phase boundary; a double line marks a salt bridge or separator. Electrons move through the external wire from anode to cathode in a galvanic cell, while ions migrate internally to maintain electroneutrality.

Standard reduction potentials are measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode. E°cell=E°cathode−E°anode when both are written as reductions. Potentials are intensive and must not be multiplied when half-reactions are scaled; ΔG and electron amount scale together.

Electrical work links voltage to Gibbs energy: ΔG=−nFE. A positive Ecell for the reaction as written means ΔG is negative. F≈96485 C mol⁻¹. Standard voltage describes standard activities; actual voltage changes with Q.

The Nernst equation follows from ΔG=ΔG°+RTlnQ and ΔG=−nFE. Thus E=E°−(RT/nF)lnQ. At 298 K, E=E°−(0.05916/n)log10Q. Include gas fugacity or solution activities when non-ideality matters.

A concentration cell uses identical electrodes but different activities of the same redox couple. The activity gradient creates a potential difference, and the cell discharges as the concentration difference decreases. The voltage can be predicted directly from the Nernst equation.

Faraday's law uses Q=It and n(e−)=Q/F. If product formation requires z electrons per formula unit, n(product)=It/(zF), and m=ItM/(zF). Competing electrode reactions reduce current efficiency below 100%.

Overpotential is the additional potential required beyond equilibrium potential to sustain current. Activation, concentration and ohmic polarisation contribute. Electrode composition, roughness, adsorption, electrolyte conductivity and mass transfer influence observed performance.

Primary batteries are designed for one discharge; secondary batteries are rechargeable. Cell voltage, capacity, energy density, power density, cycle life and safety are different performance metrics. Battery reactions must be reversible enough for repeated charge/discharge.

Lithium-ion batteries move Li+ through an electrolyte between insertion electrodes while electrons flow through the external circuit. SEI formation, electrolyte stability, dendrites, thermal management and electrode degradation affect lifetime and safety. Alternative chemistries trade energy density against cost, resource availability and cycle life.

Corrosion is an electrochemical process involving anodic metal dissolution and a cathodic reaction such as oxygen reduction. Galvanic coupling, differential aeration and electrolyte composition can localise attack. Coatings, inhibitors, alloy design and cathodic protection reduce corrosion rate.

Potentiometry measures potential at near-zero current; a pH glass electrode is a common example. Voltammetry measures current as potential is scanned; amperometry measures current at fixed potential. Reference electrode stability, junction potentials, fouling and calibration affect data.

EIS applies a small alternating perturbation over a frequency range. Nyquist and Bode plots can reveal charge-transfer resistance, double-layer capacitance and diffusion. Equivalent-circuit fits are not unique; interpret them with independent electrochemical and materials evidence.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Electrochemical derivations and cell analysis

1. Deriving the Nernst equation

For a cell reaction transferring n electrons, ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q. Electrical work gives ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°. Substitution and division by −nF gives E = E° − (RT/nF)ln Q. At 298.15 K, RT ln10/F = 0.05916 V, yielding E = E° − (0.05916/n)log10Q. Q must be constructed from activities for rigorous work.

2. Connecting equilibrium and cell voltage

At equilibrium E=0 and Q=K. Thus E°=(RT/nF)lnK and lnK=nFE°/RT. A positive standard cell potential corresponds to K>1 for the reaction as written. Reversing the cell reaction reverses the signs of E° and ΔG° and replaces K with 1/K.

3. Faraday's law

Charge passed is Q=It. One mole of electrons carries charge F. If the cathode reaction requires z electrons per mole of product, n(product)=It/(zF), and mass m=ItM/(zF). This ideal relation must be corrected for current efficiency when side reactions occur.

4. Electrode kinetics

At equilibrium, anodic and cathodic partial currents are equal in magnitude and net current is zero. Driving the electrode away from equilibrium creates an overpotential η. The Butler–Volmer equation relates current density to η through anodic and cathodic exponential terms; at sufficiently large activation-controlled overpotential, a Tafel approximation can be used. Mass transport limitation causes additional concentration polarisation.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Calculate a standard cell potential

Problem: E°(Cu²⁺/Cu)=+0.340 V and E°(Zn²⁺/Zn)=−0.763 V. Calculate E° for Zn|Zn²⁺||Cu²⁺|Cu.

Solution: Cathode is Cu reduction; anode is Zn oxidation. E°cell=0.340−(−0.763)=+1.103 V. Positive voltage indicates the cell reaction as written is spontaneous under standard conditions.

Worked numerical problem 2: Nernst calculation

Problem: For Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, let [Zn²⁺]=1.0 M and [Cu²⁺]=0.010 M at 298 K. Use E°=1.103 V.

Solution: Q=[Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]=100. E=1.103−(0.05916/2)log10(100)=1.044 V. The voltage is lower than standard because products are relatively high.

Worked numerical problem 3: Equilibrium constant from E°

Problem: A cell transfers n=2 electrons and has E°=0.295 V at 298 K. Find log10K.

Solution: log10K=nE°/0.05916=2×0.295/0.05916=9.97. K≈9.3×10⁹; the standard-state equilibrium strongly favours products.

Worked numerical problem 4: Electroplating mass

Problem: A current of 1.50 A flows for 45.0 min to plate Ag+ + e− → Ag. Find theoretical silver mass.

Solution: Q=1.50×2700=4050 C. n(e−)=4050/96485=0.04198 mol. One electron plates one Ag atom, so m=0.04198×107.87=4.53 g, assuming 100% efficiency.

Worked numerical problem 5: Calculate pH from hydrogen electrode potential

Problem: At 298 K, a hydrogen electrode at 1 bar has E = −0.1775 V relative to SHE. Estimate pH.

Solution: For 2H+ +2e−→H2, E=−0.05916 pH at unit H2 pressure. pH=0.1775/0.05916=3.00. This assumes ideal response and standard hydrogen pressure.

Worked numerical problem 6: Gibbs energy from cell voltage

Problem: A cell with n=3 has E=0.420 V. Calculate ΔG.

Solution: ΔG=−nFE=−3×96485×0.420=−1.22×10⁵ J mol⁻¹=−122 kJ mol⁻¹.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Why are electrode potentials not multiplied when a half-equation is scaled?

Answer: Potential is an intensive energy-per-charge quantity. Scaling the reaction scales ΔG and electron amount proportionally, leaving E=−ΔG/nF unchanged.

Model answer 2

Question: Explain the role of a salt bridge.

Answer: It allows ion migration to maintain electroneutrality in each half-cell and completes the ionic circuit while limiting bulk mixing.

Model answer 3

Question: Distinguish anode and cathode.

Answer: Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode in every electrochemical cell. Their signs depend on whether the cell is galvanic or electrolytic.

Model answer 4

Question: Why does a real electrolytic process require extra voltage?

Answer: Overpotentials, ohmic resistance and concentration gradients add to the reversible thermodynamic voltage. Side reactions can further reduce current efficiency.

Model answer 5

Question: How can a concentration cell generate voltage?

Answer: Different activities of the same redox species create different electrode potentials. The resulting voltage drives the system toward equalisation of chemical potential.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: A cell reaction transfers 2 electrons and has E°=0.500 V. Estimate K at 298 K.

Answer: log10K=nE°/0.05916=1.000/0.05916=16.9. K is approximately 8×10¹⁶, strongly product-favoured under standard conditions.

Model answer 7

Question: Why does electrode polarisation occur?

Answer: Current changes electrode potential away from its equilibrium value due to activation barriers, concentration gradients and ohmic losses. The resulting overpotential reduces energy efficiency.

Model answer 8

Question: How does a sacrificial anode protect steel?

Answer: A more easily oxidised metal such as zinc or magnesium is electrically connected to steel. The sacrificial metal acts as the anode and corrodes preferentially, keeping the steel cathodically protected.

Model answer 9

Question: Distinguish capacity from energy density in a battery.

Answer: Capacity measures charge stored, often in ampere-hours. Energy density measures energy per mass or volume and depends on both capacity and operating voltage.

Model answer 10

Question: Why can aqueous electrolysis produce gases instead of the intended metal?

Answer: Water reduction or oxidation may be thermodynamically or kinetically favoured over metal-ion discharge. Electrode potential, overpotential, pH, concentration and electrode material determine selectivity.
