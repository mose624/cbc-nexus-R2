Complex adult nursing and prioritisation

Complex care involves interacting health problems, high treatment burden, functional limitations, uncertainty and coordination across services. Nurses combine comprehensive assessment with repeated reassessment, risk recognition, shared goals and clear team communication. Priorities change as new information emerges.

Use a structured primary survey in emergencies as taught locally, prioritising immediate threats and calling for help early. In non-emergency settings, identify the most significant change from baseline, the person's goals and risks of deterioration. A checklist supports but never replaces clinical judgement or escalation.

Neurological assessment and disorders

Neurological assessment may include consciousness, orientation, pupils, speech, motor strength, sensation, coordination, gait and relevant cranial nerve function. Common disorders include stroke, seizures, traumatic brain injury, meningitis, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis and peripheral neuropathy.

Sudden facial weakness, arm weakness, speech disturbance, acute visual loss, severe new headache, seizure, rapidly changing consciousness or new focal deficit requires urgent response. Use validated tools and local stroke or neurological pathways. Record onset or last-known-well time when relevant and communicate it promptly.

Stroke and cerebrovascular disease

Stroke occurs when brain tissue is injured by interruption of blood supply (ischaemic stroke) or bleeding (haemorrhagic stroke). Transient ischaemic attack causes temporary neurological dysfunction without acute infarction by current tissue-based definitions. Risk factors include hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, smoking and vascular disease.

Rapid recognition and time-sensitive assessment are essential. Nursing care may involve neurological observations, swallowing screening by trained staff, aspiration precautions, mobility and skin protection, communication support, rehabilitation and secondary-prevention education. Do not give food, drink or oral medicines until swallowing safety is established under protocol.

Seizures, epilepsy and altered consciousness

A seizure is a transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity. Epilepsy is a disorder characterised by an enduring predisposition to seizures. Causes of seizures include epilepsy, metabolic disturbance, infection, injury, toxins and acute neurological disease.

During a seizure, protect the person from injury, clear nearby hazards, protect the head, observe timing and features, and follow the emergency plan. Do not restrain movements or put objects in the mouth. Seek emergency help for prolonged or repeated seizures, breathing difficulty, injury, pregnancy, first seizure or failure to recover as expected.

Musculoskeletal disorders and mobility

Musculoskeletal conditions include fractures, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis, spinal disorders and soft-tissue injuries. They can cause pain, inflammation, deformity, reduced strength and impaired mobility. Assessment considers mechanism of injury, pain, swelling, alignment, movement, neurovascular status and functional impact.

Support safe mobilisation and prescribed rehabilitation. After injury or surgery, monitor circulation, sensation, movement, swelling and pain according to protocol. New severe pain, loss of distal perfusion, numbness, weakness or rapidly increasing swelling may indicate a limb-threatening complication and requires urgent escalation.

Haematological disorders and transfusion concepts

Haematological disorders affect red cells, white cells, platelets, coagulation or bone marrow. Examples include iron-deficiency anaemia, haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell disease, leukaemia, lymphoma, thrombocytopenia and clotting disorders. Manifestations may include fatigue, infection, bruising, bleeding, pain or thrombosis.

Transfusion practice is governed by strict local policy, consent, identification, compatibility checks, monitoring and documentation. Nurses must know the local procedure for suspected transfusion reactions, stop or pause actions as specified, maintain patient safety and seek urgent clinical support. Never rely on memory instead of the current transfusion protocol.

Cancer biology and oncology nursing

Cancer develops through accumulated genetic and epigenetic changes that disrupt normal control of cell growth, differentiation, repair and death. Tumours may be benign or malignant; malignant cells can invade surrounding tissue and metastasise. Cancer types are classified by tissue or cell of origin and biological features.

Oncology nursing addresses symptom burden, treatment education, nutrition, infection risk, fatigue, pain, psychosocial distress, fertility concerns and shared decisions. Treatment may include surgery, radiotherapy, systemic medicines, targeted therapy, immunotherapy or combinations. Explain that effects and monitoring depend on the specific regimen and patient.

Cancer treatment effects and supportive care

Cancer treatments can affect rapidly dividing normal tissues and specific organs. Possible effects include nausea, mucositis, myelosuppression, infection risk, fatigue, neuropathy, skin changes and organ toxicity. Immunotherapy can produce immune-related inflammation affecting different organs.

Monitor according to the treatment plan and report fever, bleeding, breathlessness, severe diarrhoea, confusion, new weakness or other red flags promptly. Provide education on when and how to contact the oncology team. Do not offer generic thresholds or self-treatment instructions where the person's regimen has specific emergency guidance.

Perioperative nursing and surgical safety

Perioperative care spans preoperative preparation, intraoperative support and postoperative recovery. Preoperative assessment considers identity, procedure, consent, allergies, medicines, fasting instructions, baseline observations, risks and patient questions. Surgical safety systems use structured checks and team communication to reduce wrong-person, wrong-site and wrong-procedure events.

Postoperative care includes airway and breathing assessment, circulation, consciousness, pain, nausea, wound and drain observations, fluid status, mobility, temperature and prevention of complications. Escalate unexpected deterioration, excessive bleeding, severe pain, respiratory compromise or altered consciousness.

Emergency and critical care principles

Emergency nursing involves rapid assessment, stabilisation support, prioritisation and coordination. Critical care supports people with life-threatening illness or organ dysfunction using continuous monitoring and advanced therapies. Key concepts include oxygen delivery, perfusion, ventilation, neurological status, renal function and prevention of secondary injury.

Understand the purpose and limitations of monitoring devices. A displayed number is not the patient: assess the person, verify signal quality, compare trends and respond to clinical changes. Invasive devices and advanced interventions require specialist training, authorisation and supervision.

Palliative care, symptom relief and end-of-life support

Palliative care aims to improve quality of life for people and families facing serious illness through prevention and relief of suffering. It can be provided alongside disease-directed treatment and is not limited to the final days of life. Care addresses physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs.

Communication should explore the person's priorities, understanding, fears and preferred decision-making involvement. Symptom management is individualised and prescribed by authorised clinicians. Support family and carers with consent, respect cultural and spiritual preferences, and follow local law and policy for advance care planning and end-of-life decisions.

Rehabilitation, continuity and complex discharge

Rehabilitation aims to optimise function, independence and participation after illness or injury. It may involve physical, occupational, speech and language, psychological and social interventions. Nursing support reinforces agreed goals, safe activity, self-management and prevention of avoidable complications.

Complex discharge planning identifies equipment, medication access, follow-up, home environment, caregiver capacity, transport, financial or social barriers and warning signs. Use multidisciplinary planning and clear handover. Confirm that the person and carers understand the plan and know how to access help.

Integrated clinical-learning case

Scenario: During a supervised placement, a student encounters a patient whose condition or care needs require closer assessment. The patient asks questions and expresses preferences that may affect the proposed plan. The student must listen, gather relevant information, check identity and consent, assess immediate safety, communicate findings and seek supervision.

Discussion prompts: Which information is subjective and which is objective? What additional assessment would be appropriate within the student's competence? Which features require urgent escalation? How can dignity, privacy and shared decision-making be protected? What information should be included in a structured handover?

Learning point: Safe nursing integrates knowledge, observation, communication, ethical conduct and teamwork. Clinical decisions and interventions must follow approved local protocols and qualified supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For extended responses, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and connect the science to safe nursing care.

  1. What is meant by complex care?

    Show answer guidance

    Care involving interacting health problems, treatment burden, functional limitations, uncertainty and coordination across services.

  2. Name three common stroke warning features.

    Show answer guidance

    Sudden facial weakness, arm weakness and speech disturbance; sudden visual or other focal neurological changes are also concerning.

  3. What should be done during a seizure to reduce injury?

    Show answer guidance

    Protect from nearby hazards, protect the head, time and observe the event, do not restrain movements or put objects in the mouth, and follow emergency guidance.

  4. Why is swallowing assessment important after stroke?

    Show answer guidance

    Dysphagia can lead to aspiration and serious complications; oral intake should follow trained assessment and local protocol.

  5. State two neurovascular observations after limb injury.

    Show answer guidance

    Distal circulation, sensation, movement, swelling and pain are assessed according to protocol.

  6. What are the three broad phases of perioperative care?

    Show answer guidance

    Preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative care.

  7. Name two possible effects of cancer treatment.

    Show answer guidance

    Myelosuppression, nausea, mucositis, fatigue, neuropathy, infection risk and organ toxicity are examples.

  8. What is palliative care?

    Show answer guidance

    An approach that improves quality of life by preventing and relieving suffering for people with serious illness and their families.

  9. Why must transfusion checks follow a strict protocol?

    Show answer guidance

    Correct identification and compatibility checks reduce the risk of serious transfusion error and reaction.

  10. What is rehabilitation intended to achieve?

    Show answer guidance

    To optimise function, independence and participation after illness or injury.

  11. Give two signs that require urgent neurological escalation.

    Show answer guidance

    Sudden focal deficit, rapidly changing consciousness, new seizure or severe sudden headache are examples.

  12. Why is a monitor reading not enough by itself?

    Show answer guidance

    Signal artefacts and device limitations occur; readings must be checked against the person's clinical condition and trends.

  13. What should be considered in complex discharge planning?

    Show answer guidance

    Medicines, equipment, follow-up, home environment, caregiver capacity, transport, social barriers and warning signs.

  14. How should the nurse support a person receiving palliative care?

    Show answer guidance

    Assess symptoms and goals, communicate compassionately, coordinate support, respect preferences and follow authorised care plans.

  15. What should a nurse do if limb perfusion appears compromised after injury?

    Show answer guidance

    Seek urgent clinical review through the local escalation pathway; do not delay while completing routine tasks.
