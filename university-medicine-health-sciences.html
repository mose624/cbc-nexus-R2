Scope, learning outcomes and safety

Health sciences integrate biological mechanisms, population evidence, health systems and ethical practice. These notes support foundational learning and do not provide diagnosis, prescribing instructions, treatment plans or authority to practise. Clinical decisions and professional practice must follow accredited training, supervised competence, current clinical guidance and local law.

Use current textbooks, peer-reviewed evidence and recognised public-health or clinical guidance. Drug doses, treatment protocols and emergency decisions must never be inferred from general study notes.

Foundational health sciences

Human anatomy

Anatomy describes body structures and their relationships. Standard anatomical position provides a shared reference: standing upright, face forward, arms by the sides, palms forward. Directional terms include superior/inferior, anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal and superficial/deep. Body organisation progresses from cells to tissues, organs and organ systems.

Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism

The major systems include integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic/immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary and reproductive systems. Anatomical descriptions should be precise about planes, regions and laterality.

Physiology

Physiology explains how living systems function. Homeostasis maintains variables within ranges compatible with life through sensors, control centres and effectors. Negative feedback opposes a disturbance; positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint. Blood glucose regulation is a familiar negative-feedback example involving hormonal signalling, but physiological regulation is multi-layered and context dependent.

Biochemistry

Biochemistry studies the molecules and reactions of life. Proteins are polymers of amino acids; carbohydrates provide energy and structural functions; lipids form membranes and store energy; nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. Enzymes lower activation energy and alter reaction rates without changing the overall equilibrium position. Enzyme activity depends on conditions such as temperature, pH, substrate concentration and inhibitors.

Microbiology and infection principles

Microbiology examines bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoa, viruses and other microscopic agents. Distinguish colonisation, infection and disease. Transmission may occur through contact, droplets, airborne particles, vehicles or vectors. Infection prevention combines hand hygiene, environmental cleaning, appropriate protective equipment, vaccination programmes and evidence-based infection-control systems. Laboratory identification requires validated methods and biosafety procedures.

Undergraduate biomedical and population-health study

Cell and molecular biology

Cell membranes regulate transport and signalling. Passive transport follows electrochemical gradients; active transport requires energy directly or indirectly. DNA replication, transcription and translation connect genotype to gene expression, while regulation and environment influence phenotype. Mutations may be neutral, harmful or beneficial depending on context.

Human systems physiology

Cardiovascular physiology considers cardiac output (CO), heart rate (HR) and stroke volume (SV): CO=HR×SV. Respiratory physiology studies ventilation, diffusion and perfusion; renal physiology examines filtration, reabsorption, secretion and fluid-electrolyte balance; neurophysiology studies electrical signalling, synapses and integrated control. Interpret each system in relation to whole-body regulation.

Pharmacology principles

Pharmacology studies interactions between substances and biological systems. Pharmacokinetics describes absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME); pharmacodynamics describes effects and mechanisms. Concepts include receptor affinity, efficacy, agonism, antagonism, therapeutic index and variability in response. These principles are explanatory, not a basis for self-medication or prescribing.

Public health and epidemiology

Public health focuses on improving health at population level through prevention, policy, services and attention to social determinants. Incidence counts new cases over a period; prevalence measures existing cases in a population at a point or over a period. Risk is shaped by age, environment, income, education, housing, occupation, access to care and structural conditions.

Research design and ethics

Observational designs include cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies; experimental designs allocate interventions under defined protocols. Bias, confounding, missing data and measurement error affect inference. Research involving people requires appropriate ethics review, informed consent where applicable, privacy protection and safeguards for vulnerable groups.

Postgraduate and research-level study

Advanced pathways may include molecular diagnostics, immunology, neuroscience, advanced physiology, infectious disease epidemiology, health economics, implementation science, clinical research methods and health-systems evaluation. Critical appraisal asks whether the research question is clear, the design fits the question, sampling is defensible, measurements are valid, analyses are appropriate and conclusions match the evidence.

Evidence synthesis

Systematic reviews use explicit eligibility criteria and reproducible searches. Meta-analysis statistically combines compatible study estimates, while heterogeneity, publication bias and risk of bias must be assessed. A pooled estimate is not automatically applicable to every population or setting.

Health systems and equity

Analyse access, quality, safety, workforce, financing, medicines supply, information systems and governance. Equity asks whether avoidable and unfair differences exist between groups. Evaluate policies by distributional effects as well as overall averages.

Evidence interpretation and worked calculations

Incidence example: In a defined population of 2,000 initially disease-free people, 40 new cases occur over one year. Cumulative incidence = 40/2,000 = 0.02 = 2% over that year, assuming complete follow-up and a clearly defined at-risk population.

Prevalence example: If 150 of 3,000 people have a condition on a survey date, point prevalence = 150/3,000 = 5%. Prevalence is influenced by both incidence and duration and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of risk.

Critical appraisal checklist: identify population, exposure/intervention, comparator, outcomes, study design, follow-up, confounders, effect estimate, confidence interval, adverse effects, applicability and limitations.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Explain negative feedback using a physiological example.
A sensor detects deviation from a regulated range; a control system activates effectors that oppose the deviation. Explain the variable, sensor, control pathway and response, noting that biological systems often use interacting feedback loops.
2. Distinguish incidence from prevalence.
Incidence measures new cases among an at-risk population over time; prevalence measures existing cases in a population at a specified time or period.
3. State the difference between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.
Pharmacokinetics concerns what the body does to a substance (ADME); pharmacodynamics concerns what the substance does to the body and its biological mechanisms.
4. Identify three sources of bias in an observational study.
Examples include selection bias, information or measurement bias, recall bias and confounding. Explain how each could distort the estimated association.
5. Why must foundational notes not be used as clinical protocols?
Clinical decisions require patient-specific assessment, current evidence, accredited competence, supervision where required and jurisdictional standards. General learning material cannot safely replace these safeguards.

Academic use, safety and scope

These are independent learning materials, not an accredited programme, professional qualification or substitute for institutional teaching. Module order, prerequisites and assessment standards vary. Follow your university's current syllabus and consult qualified lecturers or supervisors. For laboratory, clinical, engineering and legal activities, follow the applicable institutional procedures, professional standards and country-specific rules.

CBE Nexus aims to explain concepts clearly and support independent study. Verify current facts, standards, legislation, clinical guidance and technical specifications against authoritative sources before applying them.

Frequently asked questions

Concise answers to common university study questions. Use the chapter explanations and recommended institutional materials for deeper study.

1. What do medicine and health sciences notes cover?

They cover foundational anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, microbiology, pharmacology principles, epidemiology, public health, health systems and research methods.

2. Can these notes be used to diagnose or treat a patient?

No. They are educational resources, not diagnostic or treatment instructions. Clinical decisions require accredited training, supervised competence and current local clinical guidance.

3. What is the difference between incidence and prevalence?

Incidence measures new cases in a population over a specified period; prevalence measures existing cases at a point or over a defined period.

4. Why is evidence-based practice important?

It integrates the best available research evidence with professional expertise, patient context and applicable ethical and regulatory standards.

Chapter 1: Foundations of Human Biology, Organisation and Homeostasis

Learning outcomes. Describe levels of human biological organisation; explain homeostasis and feedback; relate structure to function; distinguish normal physiological variation from disease; and identify ethical boundaries for foundational health-sciences learning.

1.1 Levels of organisation

The human body can be studied at molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, organ-system and whole-organism levels. Molecules such as proteins, lipids and nucleic acids interact within cells. Similar cells and extracellular materials form tissues; tissues combine to form organs; organs cooperate in systems such as cardiovascular, respiratory, nervous, endocrine, renal, digestive, musculoskeletal, immune and reproductive systems. No level operates in isolation. A change in blood oxygen, for example, may affect cellular metabolism, respiratory drive, cardiac function and tissue performance.

1.2 Anatomical language and planes

Standard anatomical terminology supports precise communication. Anatomical position is an agreed reference posture. Superior and inferior describe relative position toward the head or feet; anterior and posterior refer to front and back; medial and lateral refer to proximity to the midline; proximal and distal describe relative position along a limb or structure. Sagittal, coronal and transverse planes describe common section orientations. These terms are relational: a structure is medial to another only when the comparison is specified.

1.3 Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions within ranges compatible with function. It is dynamic, not a fixed state. Variables such as temperature, blood glucose, pH, osmolarity and blood pressure are regulated through interacting sensors, control centres and effectors. Negative feedback reduces a deviation from a regulated range. Positive feedback amplifies a process and is useful in limited contexts, such as aspects of childbirth and blood clotting, where the process terminates through an external event or regulatory mechanism.

Stimulus or deviation → receptor/sensor → integrating control centre → effector response → variable moves toward regulated range (negative feedback)

1.4 Temperature regulation example

When core temperature rises, thermoregulatory pathways can increase skin blood flow and sweating, promoting heat loss. When temperature falls, vasoconstriction and shivering can reduce heat loss and increase heat production. Responses depend on age, hydration, environment, medication, illness and individual physiology. A simple feedback diagram is useful for learning but does not capture every neural, hormonal and behavioural pathway.

1.5 Cells, tissues and transport

Cell membranes regulate movement of water, ions and molecules. Epithelial tissues cover surfaces and line cavities; connective tissues support and connect structures; muscle tissues generate force; nervous tissue transmits and processes signals. Tissue properties arise from cell types, extracellular matrix, architecture and blood supply. Oxygen and nutrients must reach cells, while metabolic waste must be removed. Diffusion is effective over short distances; bulk transport through circulation supports larger organisms.

1.6 Physiological regulation and variation

Physiological values vary with time, activity, age, environment, measurement method and health status. A reference interval is generally derived from a defined population and method; it is not an absolute boundary between health and disease. Interpretation of a laboratory result requires clinical context, history, examination, analytical quality and relevant guidance. Students should avoid treating a single number as a diagnosis.

1.7 Introduction to health research and evidence

Health research uses observational and experimental designs. Cohort studies follow groups over time; case-control studies compare prior exposures between people with and without an outcome; cross-sectional studies measure characteristics at a point or period; randomised trials allocate interventions under controlled conditions. Each design has strengths and biases. Confounding occurs when another factor is associated with both exposure and outcome and distorts their relationship. Randomisation, restriction, matching, stratification and statistical adjustment can help address confounding, but none guarantees perfect inference.

1.8 Ethics and professional boundaries

Health research and care involve autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, privacy and informed consent. Research with human participants requires appropriate independent ethical review and lawful consent processes. Clinical practice requires accredited education, supervised competence, registration where applicable and current local protocols. These notes provide foundational academic explanations; they are not a diagnostic tool, prescription guide or substitute for professional clinical supervision.

Worked example – feedback: If blood glucose rises after a meal, pancreatic beta cells respond by releasing insulin. Insulin promotes glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues and supports storage pathways, helping glucose return toward its regulated range. This simplified explanation omits several hormones, organs and feedback interactions.

Chapter summary and review

  • Human function emerges from interacting levels of biological organisation.
  • Homeostasis maintains regulated variables within functional ranges.
  • Negative feedback generally counteracts deviations; positive feedback amplifies a process until a stopping condition.
  • Physiological measurements require context and should not be interpreted as diagnoses in isolation.
  • Health research requires appropriate design, ethical oversight and careful interpretation of bias.
  1. Explain how the respiratory and cardiovascular systems cooperate to deliver oxygen.
  2. Compare negative and positive feedback with suitable physiological examples.
  3. Define anatomical position and explain why standard directional terms are useful.
  4. Distinguish cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies.
  5. Explain why a laboratory reference interval cannot independently establish a diagnosis.

Chapter 2: Foundations of Cardiovascular and Respiratory Physiology

This chapter introduces normal system function for academic study. It does not provide diagnostic criteria or treatment instructions.

2.1 Cardiovascular system overview

The cardiovascular system transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, immune components and metabolic waste. The heart functions as a muscular pump; blood vessels distribute and return blood; blood carries cells and dissolved substances. The right side of the heart sends blood through the pulmonary circulation for gas exchange, while the left side supplies the systemic circulation. Valves support one-way flow, while pressure gradients drive movement through the chambers and vessels.

2.2 Cardiac cycle and electrical coordination

The cardiac cycle includes alternating filling and ejection phases. Electrical activity coordinates contraction: specialised pacemaker tissue initiates impulses, conduction pathways distribute them, and myocardial cells generate force. Electrocardiography records electrical potentials at the body surface; it is not a direct image of mechanical pumping. Heart rate, stroke volume and vascular resistance contribute to cardiac output and arterial pressure. These variables are regulated by autonomic, hormonal, renal and local mechanisms.

2.3 Blood vessels and circulation

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and generally have thicker elastic and muscular walls than veins. Arterioles contribute importantly to resistance and distribution of flow. Capillaries provide exchange surfaces for gases, nutrients and waste. Veins return blood and act as capacitance vessels; skeletal-muscle contraction and valves can support venous return. Vessel structure is adapted to pressure, flow and exchange functions.

2.4 Respiratory system and ventilation

The respiratory system moves air between the atmosphere and alveoli, where gases exchange with pulmonary capillary blood. Ventilation depends on pressure differences created by changes in thoracic volume. During quiet inspiration, contraction of the diaphragm increases thoracic volume and lowers intrapulmonary pressure relative to atmospheric pressure. Elastic recoil contributes to expiration. Airway resistance, lung compliance, surface tension and respiratory muscle function influence ventilation.

2.5 Gas exchange and transport

Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse down partial-pressure gradients across the alveolar-capillary membrane. Efficient exchange depends on ventilation-perfusion matching, surface area, membrane thickness, blood flow and gas properties. Most oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin, while carbon dioxide is carried in dissolved form, bound to proteins and largely as bicarbonate. Haemoglobin affinity varies with factors such as pH, carbon dioxide, temperature and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate; these relationships support oxygen loading and unloading in different tissues.

Atmospheric air → conducting airways → alveoli ⇄ pulmonary capillary blood → left heart → systemic tissues; venous blood returns to right heart and pulmonary circulation.

2.6 Integration of cardiovascular and respiratory function

Oxygen delivery depends on both blood oxygen content and cardiac output. Ventilation alone does not guarantee adequate tissue oxygenation if perfusion, haemoglobin concentration or circulation is impaired. Conversely, adequate circulation cannot compensate fully for severe limitations in gas exchange. Exercise increases metabolic demand and requires coordinated changes in ventilation, heart rate, stroke volume and blood-flow distribution.

2.7 Regulation and adaptation

Chemoreceptors detect changes associated with carbon dioxide, pH and oxygen and contribute to respiratory control. Baroreceptors detect stretch in major arteries and contribute to short-term blood-pressure regulation. Kidneys and hormones support longer-term regulation of blood volume and pressure. Responses are integrated and context-dependent; simple diagrams are teaching models rather than complete clinical algorithms.

2.8 Research and measurement considerations

Physiological studies may measure heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, lung volumes or gas exchange. Every measure has limitations. Pulse oximetry estimates peripheral oxygen saturation and may be affected by perfusion, movement, device quality, skin pigmentation and other factors. Spirometry depends on effort, technique, calibration and reference equations. In research, standardised protocols, repeated measures and transparent reporting improve comparability.

Review questions

  1. Trace blood flow through pulmonary and systemic circulation.
  2. Explain how changes in thoracic volume produce airflow during inspiration.
  3. Describe three forms of carbon dioxide transport in blood.
  4. Why must ventilation and perfusion be considered together?
  5. Identify limitations of one physiological measurement method.

Chapter 3: Human Cell Biology, Biochemistry and Genetics

3.1 Biomolecules in human function

Human cells depend on carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, water, electrolytes and micronutrients. Carbohydrates contribute to energy metabolism and cell recognition; lipids form membranes, store energy and act as signalling molecules; proteins provide structural support, catalysis, transport and regulation; nucleic acids store and express genetic information. Nutrients are processed through digestion, absorption, transport and cellular metabolism, with regulation by neural, endocrine and local signals.

3.2 Enzymes and metabolic pathways

Enzymes accelerate reactions by lowering activation energy. Their activity depends on three-dimensional structure, substrate availability, temperature, pH, cofactors and regulatory molecules. Metabolic pathways are organised sequences of reactions, often compartmentalised within cells. Catabolic pathways break down molecules and capture energy; anabolic pathways build complex molecules and require energy. ATP couples energy-releasing processes to energy-consuming processes. Redox reactions transfer electrons through carriers such as NAD⁺/NADH and FAD/FADH₂.

3.3 Membranes and cellular signalling

Cell membranes form selective boundaries and organise signalling complexes. Receptors detect extracellular ligands and initiate intracellular pathways. Signalling may involve ion channels, G-protein-coupled receptors, enzyme-linked receptors and intracellular receptors. Second messengers such as cyclic AMP and calcium ions can amplify and coordinate signals. Signal strength and duration are controlled through receptor regulation, feedback, enzymatic breakdown and changes in gene expression. Dysregulation can contribute to disease, but the pathway from molecular change to clinical outcome is rarely one-dimensional.

3.4 Genetics and gene regulation

Most human cells contain a nuclear genome organised into chromosomes, with additional genetic material in mitochondria. Genes encode functional products and are regulated by promoter regions, transcription factors, chromatin structure, non-coding RNAs and environmental signals. Variants may affect protein sequence, splicing, expression or regulation. A genetic variant's significance depends on population frequency, inheritance pattern, functional evidence, penetrance, expressivity and clinical context. A variant should not be labelled pathogenic solely because it differs from a reference sequence.

3.5 Cell cycle and tissue renewal

The cell cycle coordinates growth, DNA replication and division. Checkpoints monitor DNA integrity and completion of key events. Mitosis produces daughter cells with broadly equivalent chromosome complements, while meiosis generates haploid cells for sexual reproduction. Stem cells can self-renew and differentiate, contributing to tissue maintenance and repair. Regulation of proliferation, differentiation and cell death is essential; disruption may contribute to cancer or degenerative processes.

3.6 Immunology foundations

The immune system includes innate and adaptive components. Innate immunity provides rapid responses through barriers, phagocytes, natural killer cells, complement and inflammatory mediators. Adaptive immunity involves antigen-specific B and T lymphocytes and can generate immunological memory. Antibodies bind specific epitopes; T cells recognise antigen fragments presented by major histocompatibility molecules. Immune responses must be regulated to limit tissue damage and maintain tolerance to self. Immunology is complex and varies with age, genetics, exposures and health status.

3.7 Microbiology and host interaction

Microorganisms include bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoa and viruses. The human microbiome includes microbial communities that interact with nutrition, immunity and barrier function. Pathogenicity depends on organism properties, host susceptibility, route of exposure and environment. Colonisation does not always mean infection, and infection does not always produce symptoms. Antimicrobial resistance can arise through mutation and gene transfer and is selected by antimicrobial exposure; stewardship and infection prevention are important public-health strategies.

3.8 Research methods in molecular health sciences

Cell culture, microscopy, flow cytometry, immunoassays, PCR, sequencing and protein analysis answer different questions. Controls are needed to assess specificity, background and assay performance. Biological replicates represent independent biological samples; technical replicates assess measurement repeatability. Sample size, pre-registration, blinding where feasible, batch effects and transparent data processing influence reliability. Findings from cell or animal models require cautious translation to human populations.

Review questions

  1. Compare catabolic and anabolic metabolism.
  2. Explain how a receptor can convert an extracellular signal into a cellular response.
  3. Distinguish a genetic variant from a pathogenic variant.
  4. Compare innate and adaptive immunity.
  5. Explain why colonisation is not synonymous with infection.
  6. Distinguish biological and technical replicates in a laboratory study.

Chapter 4: Epidemiology, Biostatistics and Evidence-Based Health Research

4.1 Epidemiology and population health

Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health-related states and events in populations and applies findings to prevent and control health problems. Descriptive epidemiology characterises patterns by person, place and time. Analytical epidemiology investigates associations and possible causes. Public-health interpretation requires attention to population definitions, case definitions, surveillance quality and changes in testing or reporting.

4.2 Incidence and prevalence

Incidence measures new cases arising in a population at risk over a specified period. Cumulative incidence estimates the proportion developing an outcome over a defined interval when follow-up is adequate. Incidence rate uses person-time in the denominator. Prevalence measures existing cases in a population at a point or period. Prevalence is influenced by both incidence and duration, so it is not a direct measure of risk of developing disease.

Worked example: In a cohort of 1,000 people initially free of an outcome, 50 new cases arise over one year with complete follow-up. One-year cumulative incidence is 50/1,000 = 0.05, or 5%. If follow-up times differ, a person-time incidence rate may be more appropriate.

4.3 Study designs and causal inference

Randomised controlled trials allocate interventions randomly and can reduce confounding, subject to adherence, loss to follow-up and ethical constraints. Cohort studies compare outcomes over time by exposure status. Case-control studies select participants by outcome and compare prior exposure. Cross-sectional studies measure exposure and outcome in a defined period. Ecological studies analyse group-level data and risk ecological fallacy when group associations are attributed to individuals. Each design answers different questions and has characteristic biases.

4.4 Bias and confounding

Selection bias arises when inclusion or retention relates to exposure and outcome. Information bias arises from systematic measurement error, misclassification or recall differences. Confounding occurs when a third variable is associated with exposure and outcome and distorts an observed relationship. Randomisation, restriction, matching, stratification and regression adjustment can address some confounding, but residual and unmeasured confounding may remain. A causal diagram can clarify assumptions about pathways and confounders.

Exposure → Outcome
Confounder → Exposure
Confounder → Outcome
A confounder is a common cause or proxy under the causal model; do not automatically adjust for mediators or colliders.

4.5 Statistical inference

Descriptive statistics summarise data using measures such as mean, median, proportions and dispersion. Inferential methods estimate population quantities or test hypotheses using sample data. A confidence interval expresses uncertainty under a specified procedure and model; it is not the probability that a fixed parameter lies in the observed interval after the data are collected. A p-value is the probability, under a null model and assumptions, of observing data at least as extreme as those obtained. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

4.6 Effect measures and clinical significance

Risk difference, risk ratio, odds ratio and rate ratio describe different aspects of association. Absolute measures help communicate baseline risk and potential impact; relative measures can appear large when absolute differences are small. Statistical significance does not establish clinical importance, public-health relevance or causality. Interpretation should consider effect size, precision, study quality, applicability, harms, costs and patient or community priorities.

4.7 Screening and diagnostic-test concepts

Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the target condition who test positive; specificity is the proportion without it who test negative. Positive and negative predictive values depend on prevalence as well as test performance. Screening is intended for populations without recognised symptoms and requires evidence that benefits outweigh harms, including false positives, overdiagnosis and unnecessary procedures. These concepts support academic interpretation and do not guide an individual's diagnosis.

4.8 Evidence synthesis and research ethics

Systematic reviews use explicit methods to identify, appraise and synthesise studies. Meta-analysis statistically combines results when studies are sufficiently compatible. Heterogeneity, publication bias, selective reporting and differences in populations or methods can limit conclusions. Research ethics includes scientific validity, independent review, informed consent where applicable, privacy, fair participant selection and responsible data sharing. Clinical recommendations should be based on current, authoritative guidelines and qualified professional judgement.

  3. Explain selection bias, information bias and confounding.
  4. Interpret a confidence interval and a p-value without overclaiming.
  5. Why do predictive values depend on prevalence?
  6. Explain the difference between statistical and clinical significance.
