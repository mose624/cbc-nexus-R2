1. Advanced research design and methodological reasoning

1.1 From research question to inference

Advanced research design begins by specifying the inference the study intends to make. A descriptive question estimates or characterises a phenomenon. An associational question examines whether variables vary together. A causal question asks what would happen to an outcome under different exposure or intervention conditions. An interpretive question examines meaning, experience or social process. A methodological question evaluates how evidence is generated or analysed. The design, data and claims must match the intended inference.

Before selecting a method, define the unit of analysis, population or case, exposure or phenomenon, outcome or interpretive focus, timeframe and context. Specify what evidence would count as an answer. Identify plausible alternative explanations. A design should not be chosen because it is fashionable, familiar or easy to describe. It should be selected because it can answer the question within ethical and practical limits.

1.2 Causal reasoning and counterfactual thinking

Causal inference asks what would have happened to the same unit under an alternative condition. Because only one outcome is observed for a unit at a particular time, researchers use design and assumptions to estimate the missing counterfactual. Randomised experiments can create comparable groups in expectation, but they still require appropriate allocation, implementation, follow-up, outcome measurement and analysis. Non-compliance, contamination, attrition and measurement error can weaken inference.

Observational studies may estimate causal effects when assumptions are justified. Confounding occurs when a common cause of exposure and outcome distorts the relationship. Selection bias occurs when inclusion or retention depends on factors related to exposure and outcome. Reverse causation occurs when the outcome influences the apparent predictor. Measurement error may bias estimates. A causal diagram can help identify assumed relationships and clarify which variables should be adjusted for. Do not adjust mechanically for every measured variable: controlling for a mediator or collider can introduce bias.

1.3 Common quasi-experimental strategies

Difference-in-differences compares change over time in a group exposed to an intervention with change in a comparison group. Its interpretation depends on the plausibility of parallel trends in the absence of intervention, stable composition, no major differential co-interventions and appropriate handling of time effects. Inspect pre-intervention trends and explain why the comparison group is credible.

Interrupted time-series analysis examines repeated observations before and after an intervention. It can estimate changes in level or trend, but concurrent events, seasonality, changes in measurement and autocorrelation may affect results. Regression discontinuity uses a threshold that determines assignment and compares units close to the cutoff. It depends on continuity assumptions and the absence of precise manipulation around the threshold. Instrumental-variable methods require a relevant instrument that affects the exposure and influences the outcome only through that exposure, among other assumptions. These methods are not automatic solutions to confounding; each requires substantive justification.

1.4 Case selection and comparative research

Comparative research should explain why cases are selected and what the comparison can reveal. A most-similar-systems strategy selects cases that share important features but differ in an outcome or explanatory factor. A most-different-systems strategy examines cases that differ in many respects but share a pattern. These are reasoning strategies, not guarantees of causal identification. Researchers must account for case history, context, selection, measurement and alternative explanations.

In qualitative case study work, define the boundaries of the case: institution, programme, community, event, policy or period. Multiple sources can strengthen contextual understanding, but triangulation is not merely collecting many sources. Explain how each source contributes, where accounts conflict and how the analysis treats disagreement. Avoid claiming that one case represents every institution or population.

1.5 Mixed-methods integration at advanced level

Mixed methods should be planned around a rationale for integration. A quantitative strand may estimate a pattern while a qualitative strand explores mechanisms or meanings. A qualitative phase may develop a measurement instrument for later testing. Integration can occur through connecting samples, building one strand on another, merging datasets, embedding a strand or presenting a joint interpretation. State what happens if findings diverge. Contradiction may reveal different constructs, timeframes, sampling frames or experiences rather than an error to be hidden.

1.6 Researcher positionality and reflexivity

Positionality describes how a researcher's social, professional, cultural, institutional and intellectual location may shape access, relationships, questions and interpretation. Reflexivity is the ongoing examination of those influences. Researchers should not treat positionality statements as ritual paragraphs. Explain relevant relationships, assumptions, power differences, language, access and decisions. In community research, consider who defines the problem, who benefits, whose knowledge is treated as authoritative and how results will be returned to participants.

2. Systematic reviews, scoping reviews and evidence synthesis

2.1 Choosing the review type

A systematic review answers a focused question through explicit, reproducible procedures for searching, selecting, appraising and synthesising evidence. A scoping review maps the extent and characteristics of research and identifies concepts or gaps. A qualitative evidence synthesis integrates findings from qualitative studies. A meta-analysis statistically combines compatible quantitative estimates. A narrative review critically discusses a topic without claiming that all relevant evidence has been located. A rapid review uses streamlined procedures and must disclose the compromises made.

Choose the review type before searching. The review question, field, evidence base, available expertise and intended output determine the appropriate approach. Do not conduct a broad unsystematic search and label it a systematic review after the fact.

2.2 Protocol development

A review protocol should state the rationale, question, eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategy, screening process, data extraction, appraisal method, synthesis plan and procedures for resolving disagreements. Register or publish a protocol where suitable and available. Record amendments with dates and reasons. A protocol reduces selective decision-making and helps readers distinguish planned analyses from later exploratory work.

2.3 Search design and reproducibility

Translate the question into searchable concepts. Use controlled vocabulary where a database provides it, alongside free-text terms, synonyms and spelling variants. Adapt the syntax for each database rather than copying one search string everywhere. Search reference lists and relevant registries where appropriate. Record the platform, database, search date, exact query, limits and number of results. A search is not reproducible if the database and search terms are missing.

Do not apply arbitrary date or language restrictions without explanation. Search grey literature when the question requires policy reports, theses, working papers or evaluation documents. Assess the reliability and purpose of each source. Search updates may be required before submission if a long period has elapsed.

2.4 Screening and extraction

Apply eligibility criteria consistently. Where possible, use two independent reviewers for screening and resolve disagreements through discussion or a third reviewer. If a single student is conducting a dissertation review, explain the procedure and any quality-control checks. Record reasons for full-text exclusions. Use a standard extraction form that captures study design, context, participants, intervention or phenomenon, outcomes, methods, findings, limitations and funding or conflicts where relevant.

2.5 Risk of bias and certainty

Risk-of-bias appraisal asks whether study methods could systematically distort results. The appropriate tool depends on the design: randomised trials, non-randomised intervention studies, diagnostic accuracy studies and qualitative research require different appraisal frameworks. Do not use a generic numerical score as if all designs were interchangeable. Explain the domains assessed and how concerns affect interpretation.

Certainty of evidence considers how confident readers can be in the overall body of evidence. Factors may include risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. A large number of studies does not automatically create high certainty if the studies are weak or inconsistent.

2.6 Synthesis and reporting

Narrative synthesis should explain patterns, differences and limitations across studies. Group evidence using a rationale such as intervention type, context, outcome or design. Meta-analysis is appropriate only when studies are sufficiently comparable and statistical assumptions are defensible. Assess heterogeneity and avoid combining fundamentally different constructs simply to produce a pooled estimate. Report the model, effect measure, uncertainty and sensitivity analyses.

Publication bias may occur when the likelihood of publication depends on the direction or statistical significance of results. Funnel plots and statistical tests can be informative in suitable circumstances but have limitations, especially with few studies or substantial heterogeneity. Discuss selective reporting and missing evidence rather than treating a single diagnostic test as conclusive.

3. Advanced quantitative analysis and interpretation

3.1 Measurement scales and distributions

Nominal variables classify categories without order. Ordinal variables have an order but not necessarily equal distances between categories. Interval scales have equal units but no absolute zero; ratio scales have equal units and a meaningful zero. The scale affects which summaries and models are appropriate. Do not automatically treat ordinal survey responses as continuous without justification.

Inspect distributions using plots and summary statistics. Check skewness, ceiling and floor effects, outliers, multimodality and missingness. A normality test alone should not determine the entire analysis. Consider the study design, sample size, model residuals and robustness of the estimator. Use transformations only when they improve the model and can be interpreted sensibly.

3.2 Estimation, uncertainty and effect size

Report estimates with confidence intervals where appropriate. A confidence interval communicates the range of values compatible with the data and model at a stated confidence level; it is not a probability statement about a fixed parameter after the interval has been calculated. The width reflects information and uncertainty. A narrow interval may still be biased if the design is flawed.

Effect size expresses the magnitude of a relationship or difference. It may be a mean difference, risk ratio, odds ratio, correlation, standardised mean difference or another measure suited to the outcome. Interpret it in context, with units and baseline risk where relevant. Avoid relying on arbitrary labels such as small, medium and large without explaining practical meaning.

3.3 Regression and model specification

Linear regression estimates a conditional mean for a continuous outcome under model assumptions. Logistic regression models the probability or odds of a binary outcome. Count outcomes may use Poisson or negative-binomial models depending on dispersion and assumptions. Survival analysis examines time to an event while accounting for censoring. Multilevel models can represent nested data such as learners within classes or patients within hospitals. Time-series models address temporal dependence and trend.

Model specification should be theory-informed. State the outcome, predictors, reference categories, functional form, interactions and adjustment variables. Check residuals, influential observations, collinearity, model fit and assumptions. Avoid stepwise selection as a substitute for substantive reasoning. Do not interpret an adjusted association as causal without a credible causal design and assumptions.

3.4 Statistical power and precision

Power is the probability of detecting a specified effect under assumptions when that effect exists. It depends on sample size, effect size, variability, significance threshold, design and analysis. A post-hoc power calculation based on the observed effect is generally less informative than reporting the estimate and confidence interval. Plan sample size before data collection using a meaningful effect and defensible assumptions. Account for clustering, attrition, multiple outcomes and planned subgroup analyses.

3.5 Missing data and sensitivity analysis

Describe how much data are missing, which variables are affected and whether missingness differs across groups. Missing completely at random, missing at random and missing not at random are assumptions about the missingness process; they cannot be established by a simple test alone. Complete-case analysis, single imputation and multiple imputation have different consequences. State the assumptions, implementation and diagnostics. Sensitivity analysis examines whether conclusions change under plausible alternative assumptions or analytic decisions.

3.6 Reproducible computational analysis

Keep analysis scripts, data dictionaries, software versions, package versions and a log of decisions. Separate raw data, cleaned data, scripts and outputs. Use stable identifiers and avoid manual spreadsheet edits that cannot be audited. Test code on a small sample, inspect intermediate results and compare outputs with independent calculations where feasible. Protect data and credentials; never publish identifiable data or access keys.

4. Advanced qualitative research and interpretation

4.1 Sampling for depth and variation

Qualitative sampling is guided by the research purpose rather than a universal requirement for statistical representativeness. Purposive sampling selects participants or cases with relevant experience. Maximum-variation sampling seeks a range of perspectives. Criterion sampling selects cases meeting defined conditions. Theoretical sampling develops as concepts emerge in grounded theory. Snowball sampling may help reach populations that are difficult to access but can produce network-related selection bias.

Explain who was eligible, how participants were approached, who declined where known, how the sample developed and why the evidence was adequate for the analytic purpose. Avoid claiming that a small qualitative sample represents an entire population. Provide enough contextual detail for readers to judge transferability.

4.2 Interviewing and fieldwork quality

Develop an interview guide that maps to research questions without dictating expected answers. Pilot the guide, use open prompts and neutral probes, and allow participants to raise issues not anticipated by the researcher. Record contextual information, interruptions, setting and the researcher's role. Interviewing is relational: power, trust, language and perceived consequences may influence what participants disclose.

Translation and interpretation require careful documentation. Explain language choices, interpreter training, confidentiality arrangements, transcription conventions and how meaning was checked. Avoid treating translated words as perfectly equivalent across cultural contexts.

4.3 Coding and thematic development

Familiarisation involves repeated engagement with the dataset and initial analytic notes. Coding identifies segments relevant to the question. Codes may be semantic, describing explicit content, or latent, interpreting underlying assumptions and meaning. Codes can be developed inductively from the data or deductively from theory, with the rationale stated. A codebook should define codes and inclusion or exclusion rules when a structured approach is used.

Candidate themes are patterns of shared meaning that contribute to the research question. Review themes against coded extracts and the full dataset. Define what each theme captures and what it does not. Look for exceptions, tensions and negative cases. Do not treat every interview question as a theme or rely solely on word frequency. A theme should be analytically meaningful and supported by evidence.

4.4 Alternative analytic traditions

Grounded theory aims to develop an explanatory account through iterative data collection, constant comparison and theoretical sampling. Narrative analysis examines how stories are structured and how identity, time and context shape accounts. Discourse analysis investigates how language constructs meaning, identities and power relations. Framework analysis can support applied policy research with a structured matrix. Content analysis may quantify or interpret patterns in texts. Phenomenological approaches examine lived experience. These traditions have different assumptions; choose one that fits the research question and follow its analytic logic.

4.5 Rigour, reflexivity and representation

Credibility concerns whether interpretations are well supported by evidence and context. Dependability concerns transparent processes. Confirmability concerns grounding claims in the data rather than unsupported preference. Transferability requires contextual detail. Reflexivity examines how the researcher shapes the inquiry. An audit trail can record decisions, code changes, analytic memos and methodological adaptations.

Quotations should illustrate an analytic point, not replace analysis. Select excerpts that are relevant and contextually clear. Protect participants from deductive identification. Do not edit quotations to change meaning. Explain how divergent accounts were treated and avoid presenting one participant's statement as a universal experience.

5. Research integrity, publication ethics and data stewardship

5.1 Authorship and contribution

Authorship should reflect substantive scholarly contributions according to the relevant disciplinary and publisher standards. Discuss roles early and revisit them as the project evolves. Acknowledgement may be appropriate for technical assistance, access support or other contributions that do not meet authorship criteria. Seniority, supervision or funding alone does not automatically justify authorship. Disclose conflicts of interest and funding sources.

5.2 Fabrication, falsification and plagiarism

Fabrication is inventing data or results. Falsification is manipulating research materials, processes or data so the record is misleading. Plagiarism is presenting another person's work or ideas without proper acknowledgement. Other concerns include selective reporting, image manipulation, duplicate publication, undisclosed conflicts and inappropriate authorship. Keep original records and correct errors transparently. Never create fictitious survey responses, interviews, ethics approvals, quotations or references.

5.3 Open science and responsible sharing

Open science can improve transparency through protocols, preregistration, open materials, code, metadata and accessible publications. Sharing must respect consent, privacy, confidentiality, copyright, licensing and community expectations. Sensitive data may require controlled access or may not be shareable. Explain restrictions and consider whether de-identified data, synthetic examples, code or metadata can be shared safely.

5.4 Data retention and security

A data management plan should specify data types, formats, documentation, storage, access, backup, retention, sharing and disposal. Use approved institutional systems, role-based access and encryption where appropriate. Keep identity keys separately from research data. Test backups. Define file naming and version control. Record changes to datasets and analysis scripts. Apply the institution's retention schedule and applicable law.

5.5 Publication and predatory journals

Before submitting an article, examine the journal's aims, scope, editorial board, peer-review policy, indexing claims, fees, copyright terms, publication ethics and recent articles. Verify indexing directly with the indexing service rather than relying on a publisher's logo. Be cautious of guaranteed acceptance, implausibly short review times, aggressive invitations and unclear ownership. A legitimate open-access journal can charge fees, but the fee and review process should be transparent. Do not submit the same manuscript to multiple journals simultaneously unless policy explicitly permits it.

5.6 AI and academic authorship

AI tools may support language improvement, brainstorming, coding assistance or search-term development where allowed. They cannot take responsibility for the accuracy, originality, privacy or ethics of a research project. Verify generated references and claims at the source. Check code outputs and statistical interpretations. Do not upload confidential data to unapproved services. Follow the institution and publisher's current AI disclosure policy. AI detection scores should not be treated as definitive proof of misconduct.

6. Advanced thesis writing, examination and contribution

6.1 Building a coherent thesis argument

A thesis is a connected argument, not a set of independent chapters. The introduction defines the problem and research direction. The literature review positions the study in scholarly debates. The methodology justifies how evidence was generated. The findings present the evidence. The discussion interprets the findings in relation to literature and theory. The conclusion answers the questions and states the contribution. Chapter transitions should explain why the next stage follows from the previous one.

6.2 Master's and doctoral contribution

A master's contribution may be an applied evaluation, a context-specific test of theory, a new synthesis, a methodological refinement or an evidence-based solution to a defined professional problem. Doctoral work normally requires a more substantial and original contribution. It may introduce new empirical evidence, reinterpret a known phenomenon, develop or refine theory, create a method, challenge an established assumption or reveal a previously under-examined relationship. Originality must be demonstrated through the literature and the research itself, not asserted in a declaration.

6.3 Writing an effective abstract

A completed-study abstract commonly summarises background, problem, aim, design, data or participants, analysis, principal findings and contribution. A proposal abstract describes the planned method and anticipated value without presenting findings that do not yet exist. Use specific terms and avoid unsupported claims. Keywords should identify the discipline, core concepts, population, context and method. The abstract should match the final thesis, not an earlier draft.

6.4 Editing and technical quality assurance

Edit in stages: argument and structure first, alignment and evidence second, paragraph clarity third, grammar and formatting last. Check every heading, table, figure, caption, abbreviation, citation, reference and appendix. Ensure that each table or figure is mentioned in the text and has a title, units and explanatory notes. Confirm the required file type, page numbering, margins, line spacing, declaration, similarity report process and submission portal. Use the institution's template early.

6.5 Proposal defence and viva voce

A proposal presentation should explain the problem, gap, aim, objectives, literature, framework, design, sampling, instruments, analysis, ethics, feasibility and contribution. A viva tests whether the candidate understands and can defend the research. Prepare concise answers to why the topic matters, how the gap was established, why the design fits, how participants were protected, what assumptions underpin the analysis, which findings were unexpected, what the limitations are and what the work adds.

Answer the question directly, then explain the reasoning and evidence. If you do not know, acknowledge the limit and explain how you would investigate it. Do not invent an answer to satisfy an examiner. Record corrections, clarify deadlines and complete required post-defence revisions. A defence is an academic discussion, not a contest in which every criticism must be rejected.

6.6 Research dissemination and impact

Research may be communicated through a thesis repository, journal article, conference presentation, policy brief, technical report, dataset or community presentation. Select the format for the intended audience. A journal article requires a focused contribution and compliance with journal policy; a policy brief requires accessible language and feasible recommendations; a community report should respect participants and explain findings without unnecessary jargon.

Publication does not automatically create impact. Explain the pathway by which evidence may inform practice or policy, what stakeholders need to be involved and what additional evidence is required. Share data only where consent, ethics approval, law, licence and institutional policy permit.

6.7 Doctoral-level critical self-assessment

At advanced level, the researcher should be able to explain the study's epistemological assumptions, justify the design, assess alternative explanations, defend analytic decisions, identify the limits of transfer or generalisation and articulate the original contribution. A strong thesis does not claim certainty where evidence is limited. It explains why the findings are credible, what they change and what further work is necessary.

7. Advanced applied exercises and marking rubric

  1. Develop a causal diagram for a proposed intervention and identify confounders, mediators and possible colliders.
  2. Compare difference-in-differences, interrupted time series and regression discontinuity, stating assumptions and threats.
  3. Draft a systematic review protocol with eligibility criteria, search plan, screening and synthesis strategy.
  4. Critique a quantitative paper by examining sampling, measurement, effect size, uncertainty and missing data.
  5. Develop a qualitative codebook and show how candidate themes were reviewed against the full dataset.
  6. Design a mixed-methods joint display and explain how divergent evidence will be interpreted.
  7. Write a data management plan covering privacy, access, backup, retention and sharing.
  8. Prepare a viva presentation and a response to five challenging methodological questions.
