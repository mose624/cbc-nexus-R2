Conceptual workflow

1. Environmental biotechnology and microbial ecology

Environmental biotechnology applies organisms, enzymes and biological processes to pollution control, resource recovery and ecosystem management. Microorganisms occupy complex communities in soil, water, sediments and engineered treatment systems. Their activity depends on nutrients, electron donors and acceptors, temperature, pH, moisture, oxygen, toxicants and interactions with other organisms.

Microbial communities can perform complementary transformations. In wastewater treatment, aerobic organisms oxidise organic matter, nitrifiers convert ammonium to nitrite and nitrate, and denitrifiers can reduce nitrate under suitable anoxic conditions. Process performance depends on the whole community and reactor environment rather than one organism alone.

2. Biodegradation and bioremediation

Biodegradation is biological transformation of compounds; complete mineralisation converts organic material into inorganic end products such as carbon dioxide, water and mineral salts under suitable conditions. Bioremediation uses biological processes to reduce contaminant concentration, toxicity, mobility or risk. Natural attenuation, biostimulation and bioaugmentation are different strategies, each requiring evidence that the intended process occurs at a meaningful rate.

Contaminant disappearance does not necessarily mean detoxification. Transformation products may remain harmful, and pollutants may be transferred between water, soil, air and biomass. Baseline characterisation, control sites, mass balance and toxicity testing strengthen evaluation.

3. Wastewater treatment and resource recovery

Wastewater treatment commonly combines physical, chemical and biological steps. Primary treatment removes settleable solids; secondary biological treatment reduces biodegradable organic load; tertiary or advanced processes target nutrients, pathogens, salts or trace contaminants according to reuse or discharge requirements. Activated sludge, trickling filters, anaerobic digestion and constructed wetlands are examples of biological systems.

Performance is monitored through parameters such as biochemical oxygen demand, chemical oxygen demand, suspended solids, nutrients, pH, flow and microbiological indicators. Regulatory limits depend on receiving environment and intended reuse. Sludge management and safe resource recovery are part of the system boundary.

4. Phytoremediation and constructed wetlands

Phytoremediation uses plants and associated microorganisms to stabilise, extract, degrade or contain contaminants. Mechanisms include phytoextraction, phytostabilisation, rhizodegradation and phytovolatilisation. Suitability depends on contaminant chemistry, rooting depth, climate, biomass management and ecological risks. Plants may concentrate contaminants in harvestable tissues, creating a secondary waste stream that must be safely managed.

Constructed wetlands use vegetation, substrates and microbial communities to treat water through filtration, sorption, sedimentation and biological transformation. They can provide low-energy treatment in suitable settings, but require land, hydraulic design, maintenance and monitoring for seasonal performance and vector or pathogen risks.

5. Bioenergy and circular resource systems

Anaerobic digestion converts biodegradable organic matter into biogas, primarily methane and carbon dioxide, plus digestate. Feedstock composition, loading rate, retention time, temperature, pH and inhibitors influence stability. Biogas can provide energy, while digestate may return nutrients to land if it meets safety and quality requirements. Contamination by plastics, metals, pathogens or persistent chemicals can constrain use.

Life-cycle assessment compares energy, greenhouse-gas emissions, land, water, transport, leakage and displaced alternatives. A biological route is not automatically sustainable; the full system and counterfactual matter.

6. Environmental monitoring and risk assessment

Environmental biotechnology projects require baseline and follow-up monitoring, appropriate reference sites and clear endpoints. Chemical analysis should be paired with biological or ecotoxicological measures when transformation products or mixture effects are plausible. Sampling design accounts for spatial heterogeneity, seasonality, hydrology and detection limits.

Risk assessment identifies hazards, exposure pathways and receptors, then characterises likelihood and consequence with uncertainty. Community engagement, transparent reporting and compliance with environmental permits support legitimacy. Remediation targets should be based on site-specific risk and applicable standards rather than arbitrary percentage removal alone.

Worked example: calculating organic-load removal

Influent wastewater has a biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) of 240 mg/L and treated effluent has 36 mg/L. Percentage removal = [(240 − 36)/240] × 100 = 85%. This is a concentration-based comparison. If flow rates differ substantially, calculate mass loading as concentration × flow and compare loads, using consistent units and time periods.

An 85% reduction does not alone establish compliance or ecological safety. Check the discharge limit, flow, nutrient levels, pathogen indicators, toxicity, receiving-water sensitivity and sampling representativeness.

Case study: selecting a remediation strategy for contaminated soil

A site assessment identifies a petroleum-hydrocarbon mixture in soil near a former storage area. The team characterises contaminant fractions, concentration gradients, soil texture, groundwater depth, oxygen and nutrient status. Bench-scale treatability tests compare natural attenuation and managed biostimulation, while controls distinguish biodegradation from volatilisation or sorption.

Monitoring tracks parent compounds, relevant metabolites, soil gas, groundwater and ecotoxicity. A decline in one measured compound is not sufficient if more toxic intermediates form or contaminants migrate. Worker protection, excavation alternatives, community concerns, long-term land use and disposal of contaminated biomass are included in the decision.

Key technical glossary

Use these definitions as a starting point; some terms have narrower meanings in particular subdisciplines.

Bioremediation
Use of organisms or biological processes to reduce contaminant concentration, toxicity, mobility or risk.
Biodegradation
Biological transformation or breakdown of a substance.
Mineralisation
Conversion of organic compounds into inorganic end products through biological or chemical processes.
Biostimulation
Adjustment of environmental conditions or nutrients to stimulate indigenous biological activity.
Bioaugmentation
Addition of selected organisms to support a biological treatment process.
Activated sludge
Suspended microbial biomass used in aerobic wastewater treatment.
Biochemical oxygen demand
Measure of oxygen consumed by microorganisms during degradation of biodegradable material under defined conditions.
Chemical oxygen demand
Oxygen-equivalent measure of chemically oxidisable substances in a sample.
Nitrification
Biological oxidation of ammonium to nitrite and nitrate.
Denitrification
Biological reduction of nitrate or nitrite to gaseous nitrogen products under suitable conditions.
Phytoremediation
Use of plants and associated processes to remove, stabilise or transform contaminants.
Phytoextraction
Uptake and accumulation of contaminants in harvestable plant tissues.
Anaerobic digestion
Microbial conversion of organic matter without oxygen, producing biogas and digestate.
Ecotoxicology
Study of harmful effects of chemicals and other stressors on organisms and ecosystems.
Life-cycle assessment
Evaluation of environmental impacts across defined stages of a product or system.
