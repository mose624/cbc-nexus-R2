Core framework and conceptual foundations

Organ anatomy is best learned by tracing each structure from origin to termination, then relating its position to neighbouring organs, serosal spaces, vessels, nerves and clinically accessible surface landmarks. In the thorax, distinguish the pleural cavities from the mediastinum; the pericardial sac lies within the middle mediastinum. In the abdomen, distinguish intraperitoneal organs from retroperitoneal structures and remember that peritoneal relationships are not the same as embryological origin. The portal venous system carries blood from much of the gastrointestinal tract and spleen to the liver before it enters the systemic circulation. The biliary tree conveys bile from hepatic ducts through the common hepatic and common bile ducts; the cystic duct connects the gallbladder. The urinary tract conducts urine from renal papillae to minor and major calyces, renal pelvis, ureter, bladder and urethra. Pelvic anatomy should be studied in relation to pelvic floor support, fascial planes, autonomic pathways and the ureter's relationships to reproductive organs.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

For the heart, map the four chambers, valve positions, fibrous skeleton, coronary arteries and cardiac conduction pathway. The sinoatrial node normally initiates atrial depolarisation; conduction reaches the atrioventricular node, His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje network. Coronary dominance is defined by the artery giving rise to the posterior interventricular branch and varies between individuals. For the respiratory tract, follow air from nasal cavity to pharynx, larynx, trachea, main bronchi, lobar and segmental bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli. The right main bronchus is generally wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, influencing aspiration patterns. The diaphragm is supplied motorically by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5); central diaphragmatic irritation may refer pain to the shoulder through cervical spinal segments. In neuroanatomy, distinguish dura, arachnoid and pia; cerebrospinal fluid circulates from the lateral ventricles through the foramina of Monro, third ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, fourth ventricle and into subarachnoid spaces, with absorption chiefly through arachnoid granulations.

Integrated clinical case

A patient with right upper-quadrant pain after a fatty meal has tenderness near the right costal margin. Explain the anatomical relationships of the gallbladder, cystic duct, common hepatic duct, common bile duct, liver and duodenum. Discuss why the cystic artery and biliary anatomy must be identified carefully during surgery, and why anatomical variation matters.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.

Focused anatomical masterclass: regional relationships, neurovascular pathways and clinical application

Purpose of this focused module. This section replaces generic revision language with concrete regional anatomy. It is organised around a repeatable method: establish the region and its boundaries; identify the layers; map the contents; trace arterial supply, venous drainage, lymphatics and innervation; then explain how those relationships matter in examination, imaging and supervised clinical practice. Anatomical variation is common, so diagrams are learning models rather than substitutes for dissection, prosections or validated imaging.

1. Thoracic wall, pleura and mediastinum

The thoracic cage is formed by the thoracic vertebrae posteriorly, ribs and costal cartilages laterally, and sternum anteriorly. A typical rib articulates posteriorly with the bodies of adjacent vertebrae and the transverse process at its corresponding level. The intercostal spaces contain external, internal and innermost intercostal muscle layers, with neurovascular structures running in the costal groove along the inferior border of each rib. The main intercostal neurovascular bundle is arranged from superior to inferior as vein, artery and nerve (VAN). This relationship explains why procedures entering an intercostal space are planned close to the superior border of the rib below, while recognising that collateral vessels and anatomical variation still require appropriate technique and supervision.

The parietal pleura lines the thoracic wall, diaphragm and mediastinum; the visceral pleura closely invests the lung surface and enters the fissures. The pleural cavity is a potential space containing a thin film of fluid that permits low-friction movement. The costodiaphragmatic recess is a dependent pleural reflection that can accommodate lung expansion during deep inspiration and is a recognised site for fluid accumulation. The lung root connects each lung to the mediastinum and includes the main bronchus, pulmonary vessels, bronchial vessels, lymphatics and autonomic nerves. The pulmonary ligament is a pleural fold inferior to the root that allows movement of these structures.

The mediastinum is the central compartment between the pleural sacs. The superior mediastinum lies above the transverse thoracic plane, while the inferior mediastinum is divided into anterior, middle and posterior parts. The middle mediastinum contains the pericardium and heart; the posterior mediastinum contains structures including the oesophagus, thoracic aorta, thoracic duct and azygos system. A useful clinical anatomy exercise is to identify which compartment a mass or enlarged structure occupies before predicting displacement of adjacent organs. Do not infer a diagnosis from location alone.

2. Heart: chambers, valves, coronary circulation and conduction anatomy

The heart lies obliquely in the middle mediastinum within the pericardium. Its base is directed mainly posteriorly and is formed predominantly by the left atrium; its apex is formed by the left ventricle and projects towards the left fifth intercostal space near the midclavicular line in the typical adult. The right atrium receives systemic venous blood through the superior and inferior venae cavae and coronary sinus. Blood passes through the right atrioventricular valve (tricuspid valve) to the right ventricle, then through the pulmonary valve to the pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries. Oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, crosses the mitral valve into the left ventricle and exits through the aortic valve into the aorta.

The fibrous cardiac skeleton supports the valve openings, provides attachment for cardiac muscle and electrically insulates atrial myocardium from ventricular myocardium except through the specialised atrioventricular conduction pathway. The sinoatrial node is normally located near the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. The impulse spreads through atrial myocardium to the atrioventricular node, then through the atrioventricular bundle, right and left bundle branches and Purkinje fibres. The atrioventricular nodal delay allows ventricular filling before coordinated ventricular contraction. Coronary arteries arise from the ascending aorta. The right coronary artery commonly supplies the right atrium, much of the right ventricle and, in right-dominant circulation, the posterior interventricular region. The left coronary artery divides commonly into the anterior interventricular artery (left anterior descending) and circumflex artery. Coronary dominance is defined by the artery giving rise to the posterior interventricular branch; it varies between individuals.

Cardiac veins generally drain into the coronary sinus, which opens into the right atrium, although some veins drain directly into cardiac chambers. Autonomic fibres influence heart rate, conduction and contractility: sympathetic activity generally increases these functions, while parasympathetic activity through the vagus nerve reduces heart rate and slows atrioventricular conduction. In an examination answer, distinguish the anatomical conduction pathway from the electrocardiographic representation of electrical activity; an ECG is a surface recording, not a direct image of the conduction tissues.

3. Lungs, airways and respiratory tract anatomy

The right lung usually has three lobes separated by horizontal and oblique fissures; the left lung has two lobes separated by an oblique fissure and has a cardiac notch. Each lung is divided into bronchopulmonary segments, each supplied by a segmental (tertiary) bronchus and a corresponding branch of the pulmonary artery. Pulmonary veins usually run between segments. This segmental organisation is clinically important because a diseased segment may sometimes be removed while preserving adjacent functional lung, subject to specialist assessment.

The conducting portion extends from the trachea through bronchi and bronchioles to terminal bronchioles. The respiratory portion begins at respiratory bronchioles and includes alveolar ducts, sacs and alveoli. Type I pneumocytes form most of the thin alveolar gas-exchange surface; type II pneumocytes secrete pulmonary surfactant and can proliferate to replace damaged alveolar epithelial cells. Alveolar macrophages contribute to particulate clearance and innate defence. The blood-air barrier includes alveolar epithelium, fused or closely apposed basement membranes and capillary endothelium. Gas exchange depends not only on this barrier but also on ventilation, perfusion, diffusion gradients and haemoglobin transport.

The pulmonary arteries carry relatively deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs, whereas pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the left atrium. This is a useful reminder that artery and vein are defined by direction relative to the heart, not by oxygen content. Bronchial arteries provide systemic oxygenated blood to supporting tissues of the lungs and larger airways. Lymph drains towards pulmonary and bronchopulmonary nodes and then tracheobronchial nodes before reaching larger lymphatic pathways.

4. Diaphragm and mechanics-related anatomical relationships

The diaphragm is a musculotendinous partition between thoracic and abdominal cavities. Its peripheral muscle fibres arise from the xiphoid region, lower costal margin and lumbar crura and arcuate ligaments, converging on a central tendon. The right dome is often higher than the left because of the liver. The phrenic nerves (C3–C5) provide motor supply and sensory supply to the central diaphragmatic pleura and peritoneum and fibrous pericardium; peripheral sensory supply is also provided by lower intercostal nerves. The familiar mnemonic C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive is a memory aid, not a substitute for understanding the nerve's course.

Three major openings are conventionally described: the caval opening at approximately T8, the oesophageal hiatus at T10 and the aortic hiatus at T12. The inferior vena cava traverses the central tendon; the oesophagus passes through the muscular right crus with vagal trunks; the aorta passes posterior to the diaphragm beneath the median arcuate ligament, accompanied by the thoracic duct and often the azygos system. Vertebral levels are approximate teaching landmarks and can vary with posture and respiration.

5. Abdominal wall, peritoneum and abdominal spaces

The anterolateral abdominal wall is organised in layers: skin, superficial fascia, muscular and aponeurotic layers, transversalis fascia, extraperitoneal tissue and parietal peritoneum. The flat muscles are external oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis; the rectus abdominis lies within the rectus sheath. The linea alba is a midline fibrous raphe formed by interlacing aponeuroses. The arcuate line marks the inferior limit of the posterior rectus sheath in the usual description; below it, the aponeuroses pass anterior to rectus abdominis, leaving transversalis fascia posteriorly.

The peritoneum is a serous membrane with parietal and visceral layers. The peritoneal cavity is a potential space between them. Intraperitoneal organs are suspended by mesenteries and have a greater degree of peritoneal investment; retroperitoneal structures lie posterior to the parietal peritoneum. The kidneys, suprarenal glands, ureters, most of the pancreas and much of the duodenum are primarily or secondarily retroperitoneal. The greater sac communicates with the lesser sac (omental bursa) through the epiploic foramen. The greater omentum descends from the greater curvature of the stomach and drapes over the intestines; the lesser omentum connects the liver to the lesser curvature of the stomach and proximal duodenum.

Visceral pain is often poorly localised and may be referred to a somatic region sharing spinal cord segments. Parietal peritoneal irritation is generally sharper and better localised because the parietal layer receives somatic innervation. This distinction helps explain why the character and location of abdominal pain may change as inflammation extends from a visceral structure to adjacent parietal peritoneum. It is a conceptual pattern, not a diagnostic rule.

6. Gastrointestinal tract, liver, biliary tree and pancreas

The foregut, midgut and hindgut divisions are useful for understanding embryology, arterial supply and referred pain. The foregut is supplied principally by the coeliac trunk; the midgut by the superior mesenteric artery; and the hindgut by the inferior mesenteric artery. Their territories overlap near the junctions, and the exact arterial branching pattern varies. The coeliac trunk typically gives rise to left gastric, splenic and common hepatic arteries. The superior mesenteric artery supplies most of the small intestine and proximal large intestine; the inferior mesenteric artery supplies the distal transverse colon, descending colon, sigmoid colon and upper rectum.

The liver occupies much of the right upper quadrant and has functional right and left divisions based on vascular and biliary anatomy rather than simply its external lobes. The portal triad within the hepatoduodenal ligament comprises the proper hepatic artery, portal vein and common bile duct. In the usual arrangement, the bile duct lies to the right, hepatic artery to the left and portal vein posteriorly. The portal vein is formed mainly by the union of the superior mesenteric and splenic veins posterior to the neck of the pancreas. Portal blood carries absorbed nutrients from the gastrointestinal tract to the liver; hepatic veins drain the liver into the inferior vena cava.

Bile produced by hepatocytes passes through canaliculi and progressively larger ducts into right and left hepatic ducts, which form the common hepatic duct. The cystic duct connects the gallbladder; the common bile duct is formed after its union with the cystic duct. The common bile duct commonly joins the main pancreatic duct near the major duodenal papilla, but anatomical patterns vary. The pancreas is secondarily retroperitoneal except for its tail, which approaches the splenic hilum within the splenorenal ligament. Its exocrine acini secrete digestive enzymes, while endocrine islets release hormones including insulin and glucagon.

7. Kidneys, ureters, bladder and pelvic viscera

The kidneys are retroperitoneal organs, usually extending approximately from T12 to L3, with the right kidney often slightly lower because of the liver. The renal hilum is classically arranged from anterior to posterior as renal vein, renal artery and renal pelvis (V-A-P), although branching patterns vary. The renal cortex contains renal corpuscles and convoluted tubules; medullary pyramids contain loops of Henle and collecting ducts. Urine passes from papillary ducts into minor calyces, major calyces, renal pelvis and ureter.

The ureter descends on the psoas major muscle and enters the pelvis before passing obliquely through the bladder wall. Three commonly taught sites of relative narrowing are the pelvi-ureteric junction, crossing of the pelvic brim near the iliac vessels, and ureterovesical junction. These are useful anatomical landmarks in the study of ureteric calculi, but stone symptoms and passage depend on more than location alone. The bladder lies behind the pubic symphysis; its relationships change with filling. The trigone is a smooth triangular area between the two ureteric openings and the internal urethral opening.

Pelvic anatomy must be learned in relation to pelvic walls, floor, fascia, vessels, nerves and organs. The pelvic diaphragm is formed mainly by levator ani and coccygeus. Levator ani includes puborectalis, pubococcygeus and iliococcygeus components; the puborectalis sling contributes to the anorectal angle and continence mechanism. The pudendal nerve (S2–S4) supplies much of the perineum and external anal and urethral sphincter regions. The pelvic autonomic plexuses contain sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways that contribute to pelvic organ function.

8. Head, neck and nervous system: orientation before detail

For head and neck anatomy, use a compartment-based approach: cranial cavity, orbit, nasal cavity, oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, carotid sheath, posterior triangle and deep cervical spaces. The carotid sheath typically contains the common or internal carotid artery medially, internal jugular vein laterally and vagus nerve posteriorly between them. The sympathetic trunk lies posterior to the sheath rather than within its usual contents. The cervical plexus arises from anterior rami C1–C4; the brachial plexus is formed mainly by C5–T1 anterior rami. The recurrent laryngeal nerves ascend in the tracheoesophageal grooves in many individuals and supply most intrinsic laryngeal muscles; the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve supplies cricothyroid.

The brain is protected by skull, meninges and cerebrospinal fluid. From superficial to deep, the meninges are dura mater, arachnoid mater and pia mater. Cerebrospinal fluid is produced predominantly by choroid plexuses, flows through the lateral ventricles, interventricular foramina, third ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, fourth ventricle and exits through median and lateral apertures to the subarachnoid space. It is returned to venous circulation through arachnoid granulations and other drainage pathways. The spinal cord typically ends as the conus medullaris around L1–L2 in adults; the cauda equina consists of descending lumbar, sacral and coccygeal nerve roots below this level. Vertebral level and spinal cord segment are not interchangeable because the vertebral column grows more than the spinal cord.

9. Embryological integration and congenital anatomy

Embryology provides a developmental explanation for adult relationships. The primitive gut tube is divided into foregut, midgut and hindgut; the midgut undergoes physiological herniation and rotation during development before returning to the abdominal cavity. Abnormal rotation or fixation can produce malrotation and atypical mesenteric relationships. The heart develops from paired cardiogenic structures that fuse and undergo looping and septation; errors in septation or outflow tract development can produce congenital cardiac anomalies. The neural tube forms the central nervous system, while neural crest cells contribute to structures including peripheral ganglia and parts of the craniofacial skeleton. Developmental explanations should be linked to the specific anomaly being discussed rather than used as broad, unsupported causal claims.

10. Clinical anatomy: structured case reasoning

Case A — penetrating injury near a rib. A learner is asked to explain why the intercostal neurovascular bundle is relevant when considering the anatomy of an intercostal space. A high-quality answer identifies the costal groove, the usual VAN order, the collateral bundle near the superior border of the rib below, pleural reflections and the possibility of anatomical variation. The answer should not provide procedural instructions; invasive management belongs to trained clinicians using current protocols.

Case B — coronary territory. A teaching ECG and coronary angiogram are presented in a supervised session. The student first identifies the imaging modality and orientation, then names the coronary artery shown, traces its likely territory and relates the findings to myocardium supplied. The student distinguishes an anatomical territory from a definitive clinical diagnosis and recognises that dominance and branching patterns vary.

Case C — abdominal pain migration. A case describes early poorly localised abdominal discomfort followed by more localised right lower quadrant tenderness. The student explains the general difference between visceral afferent pain and somatic pain from parietal peritoneum, then discusses why this pattern may occur in appendiceal inflammation. The student must also state that pain patterns are variable and require full clinical assessment.

Case D — urinary tract anatomy. A radiology teaching image demonstrates a ureteric calculus. The student identifies the ureter, follows its course from renal pelvis to bladder, marks the three commonly taught narrowing sites and explains why the ureter's relationship to the pelvic brim matters. Image interpretation must be checked against the full study and clinical context.

11. Image interpretation protocol for this chapter

  1. Identify the modality: anatomical drawing, plain radiograph, ultrasound, CT, MRI, angiography or histology slide.
  2. Confirm orientation: determine patient side, plane, level and whether the image is viewed from the patient's feet or head where relevant.
  3. Locate stable landmarks: vertebral bodies, sternum, diaphragm, liver, kidneys, major vessels or other reliable structures.
  4. Trace relationships: identify what lies anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, superior and inferior to the target structure.
  5. Describe before interpreting: state only what is visible before proposing a mechanism or diagnosis.
  6. State limitations: image quality, incomplete views, normal variants and the need for a complete report or supervised interpretation.

13. Practical and self-directed learning activities

  • On a torso model, identify the pleural reflections, lung lobes, mediastinal compartments and diaphragm openings.
  • Draw the cardiac chambers and valves as a flow diagram; use different arrows for pulmonary and systemic circuits.
  • On an approved anatomy model or atlas, trace the coeliac trunk, superior mesenteric artery and inferior mesenteric artery territories.
  • Compare an axial CT image at the level of the renal hilum with a labelled anatomical cross-section; identify the psoas muscles, aorta, inferior vena cava and kidneys.
  • Use a de-identified, appropriately licensed teaching image to practise the six-step image interpretation protocol above.
  • Keep an error log: record the structure confused, the correct relationship, the source used to resolve it and a new retrieval question.
