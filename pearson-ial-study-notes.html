Biology

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Cell membranes and transport

Textbook explanation

Cell membranes follow the fluid-mosaic model: phospholipids form a bilayer with embedded proteins, cholesterol and carbohydrate-containing molecules. Selective permeability regulates exchange. Diffusion moves particles down a concentration gradient; facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins; active transport uses energy to move substances against a gradient; osmosis is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.

Worked application

A solute transported from low to high concentration requires an energy source and a suitable carrier protein. Compare its movement with diffusion, which proceeds down the concentration gradient.

Practice and reflection

Explain why active transport requires energy and describe two membrane adaptations.

Chapter 2: DNA, genes and protein synthesis

Textbook explanation

DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands joined by complementary base pairing: adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine with guanine. A gene is a DNA sequence that can be transcribed to RNA. During transcription, RNA polymerase builds a complementary RNA strand; during translation, ribosomes read codons and assemble amino acids using tRNA.

Worked application

If a DNA template triplet is TAC, the complementary mRNA codon is AUG. Codons are read in groups of three and specify amino acids or stop signals.

Practice and reflection

Describe transcription and translation, and explain how a base substitution may affect a protein.

Chapter 3: Energy transfer: respiration and photosynthesis

Textbook explanation

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in organic molecules. The light-dependent reactions occur in thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and reduced NADP; carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle uses carbon dioxide to build triose phosphate. Respiration transfers energy from organic molecules to ATP through glycolysis, link reaction, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

Worked application

A limiting factor is a factor in shortest supply relative to demand. Increasing light intensity raises photosynthetic rate only until another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, limits the process.

Practice and reflection

Compare the roles of ATP in photosynthesis and respiration. Explain how to investigate a limiting factor.

Chapter 4: Inheritance, selection and populations

Textbook explanation

Alleles segregate during meiosis and combine at fertilisation. Independent assortment and crossing over generate genetic variation. Natural selection changes allele frequencies when heritable traits affect survival and reproductive success. Population genetics can be used to examine allele frequencies under stated assumptions.

Worked application

For a simple dominant/recessive monohybrid cross Aa × Aa, expected genotypes are 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, assuming equal segregation and random fertilisation.

Practice and reflection

Use a genetic cross to predict genotype proportions and explain how selection can change a population.

Chemistry

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Atomic structure, bonding and periodicity

Textbook explanation

Electrons occupy quantised energy levels and orbitals. The arrangement of electrons explains periodic trends and chemical behaviour. Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; covalent bonding involves shared electron pairs; metallic bonding involves positive ions attracted to delocalised electrons.

Worked application

A covalent bond forms when orbitals overlap and electron density is shared between nuclei. Bond polarity depends on the difference in electronegativity and molecular geometry.

Practice and reflection

Explain how electron configuration relates to periodicity and compare ionic and covalent bonding.

Chapter 2: Energetics and reaction kinetics

Textbook explanation

Enthalpy change measures heat energy transferred at constant pressure. Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. Reaction rate depends on successful collision frequency and activation energy. Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

Worked application

Estimate reaction enthalpy using mean bond enthalpies: ΔH ≈ sum of bonds broken − sum of bonds formed. State clearly that this is an estimate because mean bond enthalpies are averaged values.

Practice and reflection

Calculate an enthalpy change from bond enthalpies and explain how a catalyst changes a rate profile.

Chapter 3: Equilibria, acids and redox

Textbook explanation

Dynamic equilibrium occurs in a closed system when forward and reverse reaction rates are equal. Equilibrium position responds to concentration, pressure and temperature changes. Acids donate protons; bases accept protons. Oxidation involves electron loss or an increase in oxidation state; reduction involves electron gain or a decrease in oxidation state.

Worked application

For a gaseous equilibrium, increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas, provided the gaseous mole numbers differ. A catalyst changes the time to reach equilibrium, not the equilibrium constant.

Practice and reflection

Predict an equilibrium shift and identify oxidation and reduction using oxidation states.

Chapter 4: Organic chemistry and analytical methods

Textbook explanation

Organic compounds are classified by functional groups and reaction patterns. Isomerism may be structural or stereoisomeric. Reaction mechanisms describe electron-pair movement and help explain substitution, addition, elimination and oxidation. Analytical methods include chromatography, infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry.

Worked application

In proton NMR, chemical shift gives information about the chemical environment of hydrogen atoms; integration estimates relative proton numbers and splitting gives information about neighbouring hydrogens.

Practice and reflection

Identify a functional group from a structure and explain how one analytical technique supports identification.

Physics

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Mechanics: motion, forces and energy

Textbook explanation

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are vector quantities. The gradient of a displacement-time graph gives velocity; the gradient of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration; the area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement. Newton's laws connect resultant force, mass and acceleration.

Worked application

For constant acceleration, v = u + at and s = ut + ½at². Select an equation containing the known quantities and the required unknown, and use consistent SI units.

Practice and reflection

A vehicle changes speed from 8 m s⁻¹ to 20 m s⁻¹ in 4 s. Calculate acceleration and describe the resultant force direction.

Chapter 2: Waves, superposition and optics

Textbook explanation

A progressive wave transfers energy without net transfer of matter. Wave speed is v = fλ. Superposition occurs when waves overlap; coherent sources have a constant phase difference and can produce stable interference. Refraction occurs when wave speed changes at a boundary.

Worked application

For a wave of frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 0.40 m, speed = 50 × 0.40 = 20 m s⁻¹. In a double-slit experiment, fringe spacing depends on wavelength, screen distance and slit separation.

Practice and reflection

Explain constructive and destructive interference and calculate wave speed from frequency and wavelength.

Chapter 3: Electricity, fields and electromagnetism

Textbook explanation

Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V = W/Q. Resistance is the ratio V/I for an ohmic conductor at constant physical conditions. Electric and magnetic fields describe forces acting at a distance.

Worked application

For a resistor with potential difference 12 V and current 0.50 A, R = V/I = 24 Ω. Electrical power may be calculated using P = IV, I²R or V²/R.

Practice and reflection

Explain the relationship between current, potential difference and resistance and describe energy transfer in a circuit.

Chapter 4: Materials, nuclear physics and quantum ideas

Textbook explanation

Material behaviour depends on microscopic structure and bonding. Nuclear decay is random for individual nuclei but predictable statistically for large samples. Activity is the rate of decay; half-life is the time for activity or number of undecayed nuclei to halve. Quantum models describe energy transfer in discrete packets.

Worked application

After three half-lives, the remaining undecayed fraction is (1/2)³ = 1/8, or 12.5% of the original amount.

Practice and reflection

Calculate remaining activity after several half-lives and explain why radioactive decay is described as random.

Mathematics

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Algebra, functions and mathematical proof

Textbook explanation

Algebraic methods include expansion, factorisation, completing the square and solving equations. A function maps each permitted input to exactly one output. Domain restrictions may arise from denominators, roots or context. Mathematical proof uses logically connected statements to establish a result for all permitted cases.

Worked application

For f(x)=x²−4x+1, completing the square gives f(x)=(x−2)²−3. The minimum value is −3 at x=2.

Practice and reflection

Complete the square for a quadratic and state its turning point. Explain the domain of a rational function.

Chapter 2: Trigonometry, exponentials and logarithms

Textbook explanation

Trigonometric functions model periodic behaviour and satisfy identities such as sin²x + cos²x = 1. Exponential functions model proportional growth or decay. Logarithms are inverse operations to exponentiation and obey laws derived from index rules.

Worked application

If 3ˣ = 20, take logarithms: x = log(20)/log(3). Check the value by substitution into the original equation.

Practice and reflection

Solve an exponential equation using logarithms and use a trigonometric identity to simplify an expression.

Chapter 3: Differentiation, integration and applications

Textbook explanation

Differentiation gives the gradient function and rates of change. Stationary points occur where the derivative is zero. Integration reverses differentiation and can calculate areas or accumulated change. Constants of integration are required for indefinite integrals.

Worked application

If y = 3x² − 4x + 7, then dy/dx = 6x − 4. At a stationary point, 6x − 4 = 0, so x = 2/3.

Practice and reflection

Differentiate a polynomial, find stationary points and explain how the second derivative classifies a turning point.

Chapter 4: Statistics, probability and mechanics

Textbook explanation

Probability models uncertainty using events and distributions. Conditional probability updates a probability when information is known. Statistical inference uses sample data to estimate or test claims about a population. Mechanics applies mathematical models to forces and motion.

Worked application

For independent events A and B, P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). For dependent events, use P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B|A). Always check whether independence is justified.

Practice and reflection

Distinguish independent and mutually exclusive events and set up a simple mechanics model with a force diagram.

Chapter 1: The purpose and structure of financial accounting

Core learning notes

Financial accounting records, classifies and summarises transactions so users can assess performance and financial position. The accounting equation is Assets = Capital + Liabilities. Every transaction has equal debit and credit effects under double-entry bookkeeping. Source documents provide evidence; ledgers classify entries; the trial balance checks arithmetic agreement but cannot detect every error.

Worked example / application

A business buys equipment for cash, £2,000. Debit equipment (asset) £2,000 and credit cash (asset) £2,000. Total assets are unchanged, but their composition changes.

Examination practice

Explain why a balanced trial balance does not prove that every transaction has been recorded correctly.

Chapter 2: Income statements and financial performance

Core learning notes

An income statement reports income and expenses over a period. Revenue less cost of sales gives gross profit; gross profit less operating expenses gives operating profit. Accruals match income and expenses to the period in which they are earned or incurred, rather than simply when cash moves.

Worked example / application

Revenue £80,000; opening inventory £8,000; purchases £35,000; closing inventory £10,000. Cost of sales = 8,000 + 35,000 − 10,000 = £33,000; gross profit = £47,000.

Examination practice

Distinguish gross profit from operating profit and explain how depreciation affects profit.

Chapter 3: Statements of financial position and interpretation

Core learning notes

A statement of financial position presents assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date. Current assets and current liabilities help assess liquidity; non-current assets support longer-term operations. Ratios such as current ratio and gross profit margin support comparison, but must be interpreted in context.

Worked example / application

Current assets £30,000 and current liabilities £15,000 give a current ratio of 2:1. This indicates £2 of current assets for each £1 of short-term liabilities; inventory quality and collection times still matter.

Examination practice

Calculate the current ratio from supplied data and evaluate two limitations of ratio analysis.

Chapter 1: Arabic script, sounds and reading fluency

Core learning notes

Modern Standard Arabic is written from right to left. Letters often change shape according to their position in a word, and short vowels may be omitted in ordinary texts. Accurate reading depends on recognising letter forms, long and short vowels, consonant patterns and context. Practise connected script rather than isolated letter names alone.

Worked example / application

Read كَتَبَ (kataba, he wrote) and كِتاب (kitāb, book). The shared consonantal root k-t-b relates to writing, while vowel patterns and added letters distinguish meaning.

Examination practice

Explain how positional letter forms and vowel markings support accurate reading.

Chapter 2: Grammar, sentence structure and morphology

Core learning notes

Arabic grammar distinguishes nouns, verbs and particles. Noun phrases show features such as definiteness, gender, number and case; verbal sentences commonly begin with a verb, while nominal sentences can begin with a noun. Root-and-pattern morphology creates related words and meanings.

Worked example / application

In الطالبُ مجتهدٌ (the student is diligent), الطالبُ is the definite subject and مجتهدٌ is the predicate. In a fully vocalised formal context, case endings provide grammatical information.

Examination practice

Identify the subject and predicate in a nominal sentence and explain the role of definiteness.

Chapter 3: Communication, translation and textual analysis

Core learning notes

Effective communication requires register, audience awareness and accurate vocabulary. Translation should preserve meaning, tone and grammatical relationships rather than substitute words mechanically. Literary and non-fiction analysis considers purpose, structure, imagery, argument and cultural context.

Worked example / application

Translate a short passage by identifying the main clause first, then resolving pronouns and time references before polishing natural English or Arabic phrasing.

Examination practice

Analyse how an author’s word choice and sentence structure shape tone in an unseen passage.

Business — IAL Online Notes

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Business objectives, stakeholders and the external environment

Core learning notes

Businesses combine resources to create goods and services. Objectives may include survival, profit, growth, market share and social responsibility. Stakeholders include owners, employees, customers, suppliers, government and communities; their interests may conflict. PESTLE analysis examines political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental influences.

Worked example / application

A rise in interest rates may increase loan repayments, reduce consumer borrowing and weaken demand. The effect depends on the firm's debt structure and product market.

Examination practice

Evaluate how a change in the external environment could affect two stakeholder groups.

Chapter 2: Marketing, market research and strategy

Core learning notes

Marketing identifies and satisfies customer needs. Primary research gathers new data; secondary research uses existing data. Segmentation divides markets into groups; targeting selects customers; positioning shapes the intended brand image. The marketing mix commonly considers product, price, place and promotion.

Worked example / application

A premium product may use selective distribution and value-based pricing, but the business must demonstrate quality that customers recognise and are willing to pay for.

Examination practice

Recommend a suitable marketing mix for a new product and justify each decision.

Chapter 3: Operations, finance and human resources

Core learning notes

Operations transform inputs into outputs through processes, capacity and quality management. Finance decisions concern sources and uses of funds, cash flow and profitability. Human-resource management covers recruitment, training, motivation and performance. Functional decisions should support the overall strategy.

Worked example / application

If fixed costs are £12,000, selling price £20 and variable cost £8, contribution is £12 per unit. Break-even output = 12,000 ÷ 12 = 1,000 units.

Examination practice

Calculate break-even output and assess one way to improve operational efficiency.

Economics — IAL Online Notes

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Scarcity, opportunity cost and market systems

Core learning notes

Scarcity exists because resources are limited relative to wants. Choosing one option means sacrificing the next-best alternative: opportunity cost. A production possibility frontier illustrates maximum attainable combinations given resources and technology. Markets coordinate decisions through prices, incentives and competition.

Worked example / application

If a government allocates funds to a new railway rather than hospitals, the opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone hospital services, not necessarily the full monetary budget.

Examination practice

Use a production possibility diagram to explain opportunity cost and economic growth.

Chapter 2: Demand, supply and price determination

Core learning notes

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices; supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell. A movement along a curve follows a price change; a shift reflects another determinant. Equilibrium occurs where planned demand equals planned supply.

Worked example / application

An increase in consumer income may shift demand for a normal good to the right. If supply is unchanged, equilibrium price and quantity tend to rise.

Examination practice

Distinguish a movement along demand from a shift in demand and explain the likely market outcome.

Chapter 3: Macroeconomic objectives and policy

Core learning notes

Governments commonly pursue sustainable growth, low and stable inflation, low unemployment and external balance. Fiscal policy changes taxation and government spending; monetary policy changes interest rates and monetary conditions. Supply-side policies aim to raise productive capacity. Policy effects depend on timing and economic conditions.

Worked example / application

A cut in policy interest rates may encourage consumption and investment, shifting aggregate demand right. If the economy is near capacity, inflationary pressure may increase.

Examination practice

Evaluate the likely effectiveness of monetary policy during a recession.

Chapter 1: Language, context and representation

Core learning notes

Language choices are shaped by audience, purpose, genre, mode and context. Analyse lexis, grammar, syntax, discourse structure and graphology, linking evidence to meanings and effects. Representation is not neutral: naming, agency, modality and evaluation can position readers to view people or events in particular ways.

Worked example / application

Compare 'The minister announced the policy' with 'The policy was announced'. The active construction foregrounds the agent; the passive construction backgrounds or omits the agent.

Examination practice

Analyse how grammatical choices construct responsibility in two short texts.

Chapter 2: Spoken language, interaction and variation

Core learning notes

Spoken interaction includes turn-taking, adjacency pairs, repair, overlap, pauses, prosody and non-verbal cues. Language varies by region, social group, age, occupation and situation. Variation should be described objectively and interpreted in relation to identity and context, not ranked as inherently superior or inferior.

Worked example / application

A speaker's use of specialist terminology may signal occupational identity and shared knowledge, while a shift to informal vocabulary can build solidarity with a familiar audience.

Examination practice

Use transcript evidence to explain how speakers manage power and cooperation.

Chapter 3: Writing, discourse and investigation

Core learning notes

Effective writing controls register, cohesion, paragraphing and rhetorical structure. Language investigation begins with a focused research question, a justified sample and transparent methods. Ethical practice protects participants and avoids unsupported generalisations.

Worked example / application

To investigate pronoun use in public speeches, define a sampling period, select comparable speeches, code first-person plural forms and interpret patterns alongside audience and purpose.

Examination practice

Design a small language investigation and explain one sampling limitation.

Literary interpretation begins with precise textual evidence. Analyse imagery, diction, syntax, narrative voice, rhythm, dramatic form and structural development. A strong argument explains how methods create effects and how meanings may remain ambiguous.

Genre shapes reader expectations through conventions such as tragedy, comedy, satire, realism or modernism. Historical and cultural contexts inform production and reception, but context should illuminate textual choices rather than replace close analysis.

A tragic protagonist's recognition and reversal can be examined through dramatic structure, audience knowledge and the consequences of choices.

Compare how two writers adapt a shared genre convention for different purposes.

Comparison considers similarities, contrasts and meaningful differences in theme, form, voice and context. Critical interpretations can challenge assumptions, but claims must be supported by textual evidence and a clear line of reasoning.

Examination practice

Develop a comparative thesis and evaluate an alternative interpretation.

Chapter 1: Physical systems and landscape processes

Core learning notes

Physical geography examines interacting systems such as atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere. Weathering, erosion, transport and deposition shape landscapes. Systems concepts include inputs, outputs, stores, transfers, feedback and thresholds.

Worked example / application

A river transports sediment when flow energy is sufficient; when velocity falls, competence and capacity decrease and deposition may occur.

Explain how changes in discharge influence erosion, transport and deposition along a river.

Chapter 2: Human geography, place and development

Core learning notes

Human geography studies population, migration, urbanisation, economic activity, inequality and place identity. Development is multidimensional and may be measured using income, health, education and composite indices. Spatial patterns reflect historical, political, economic and environmental processes.

Worked example / application

A city may experience suburbanisation as households and firms move outward. This can alter commuting, land values, service demand and inner-city investment.

Examination practice

Evaluate one strategy for reducing inequality within a rapidly growing city.

Chapter 3: Geographical enquiry, data and fieldwork

Core learning notes

Geographical investigations require a clear question, sampling strategy, risk assessment, ethical practice and suitable data presentation. Quantitative methods include averages, correlation and statistical tests; qualitative methods include interviews and observation. Conclusions must acknowledge uncertainty and limitations.

Worked example / application

A systematic river study samples at fixed intervals along a reach, measures channel width and depth, and records velocity using a consistent method.

Examination practice

Design a fieldwork method to test how channel characteristics vary downstream.

German nouns have grammatical gender and case; articles and adjectives change accordingly. Main clauses generally place the finite verb in second position, while subordinate clauses commonly place it at the end. Verb forms, separable prefixes and word order are central to accurate expression.

Chapter 3: Society, culture and contemporary issues

Core learning notes

Discuss themes such as education, work, environment, technology, family, culture and social change. Effective responses compare viewpoints, use accurate specialist vocabulary and distinguish fact from opinion. Cultural knowledge should be specific and avoid stereotypes.

A balanced response on renewable energy can compare investment costs, energy security, emissions and regional employment effects.

Modern Greek uses a 24-letter alphabet and writes from left to right. Accurate reading depends on letter-sound correspondences, vowel combinations, stress marks and the distinction between similar consonants. Build vocabulary through roots, word families and context.

Worked example / application

In Το βιβλίο είναι ενδιαφέρον (The book is interesting), the article and noun form a neuter singular noun phrase, followed by a matching predicate adjective.

Identify case and agreement features in a short Greek sentence and explain their function.

Chapter 3: Translation, interpretation and cultural texts

When translating an idiom, first establish its communicative function, then choose a natural target-language equivalent rather than a literal word-for-word rendering.

History — IAL Online Notes

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Historical evidence and source evaluation

Core learning notes

Historical knowledge is built from primary and secondary evidence. Evaluate provenance, purpose, audience, context, content and limitations. A source is not simply reliable or unreliable: its value depends on the question being investigated and the claims it can support.

Worked example / application

A government speech may reveal official priorities and persuasive strategy, but may conceal disagreement or implementation problems. Compare it with private correspondence or independent records.

Examination practice

Assess the value and limitations of two sources for investigating a historical controversy.

Chapter 2: Causation, change and continuity

Core learning notes

Historical explanations distinguish long-term conditions, structural causes, short-term triggers and individual decisions. Change may be uneven; continuity can persist beneath political or social transformation. Avoid monocausal explanations and rank factors using evidence.

Worked example / application

A revolution may arise from fiscal crisis, social inequality, political exclusion and immediate events. Explain how these factors interacted rather than listing them separately.

Examination practice

Write a reasoned judgement about the relative importance of two causes of a historical event.

Chapter 3: Interpretations, significance and historical argument

Core learning notes

Historians construct interpretations by selecting evidence and applying concepts. Significance can be assessed through scale, duration, consequences and contemporary meaning. Essays need a clear thesis, precise evidence, counterargument and a supported conclusion.

Worked example / application

To evaluate whether a reform was transformative, compare conditions before and after, identify groups affected and consider whether later developments reversed its effects.

Examination practice

Compare two historical interpretations and explain why they differ.

Information Technology — IAL Online Notes

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Information systems, data and digital representation

Core learning notes

Information systems collect, process, store and communicate data to support decisions. Data quality includes accuracy, completeness, consistency, validity and timeliness. Digital systems encode information using binary representations; files and databases organise information for retrieval and use.

Worked example / application

A decimal integer is converted to binary by repeated division by two and reading remainders in reverse order. For example, 13 decimal is 1101 binary.

Examination practice

Explain why data validation and verification are different and give an example of each.

Chapter 2: Networks, cybersecurity and responsible use

Core learning notes

Networks connect devices through communication protocols and hardware. Security controls include authentication, access permissions, encryption, backups, patching and user awareness. Threats include phishing, malware, social engineering and unauthorised access. Legal and ethical duties govern personal data and digital conduct.

Worked example / application

A strong access-control design applies least privilege: a user receives only the permissions needed for their role. Multi-factor authentication adds another independent proof of identity.

Examination practice

Assess a security plan for a small organisation and prioritise two controls.

Chapter 3: Systems development, databases and project design

Core learning notes

Systems development includes requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment and maintenance. Relational databases organise data into tables linked by keys; normalisation reduces unnecessary duplication and update anomalies. Testing checks whether requirements are met.

Worked example / application

A customer table can use CustomerID as a primary key; an order table stores CustomerID as a foreign key to link each order to its customer.

Examination practice

Design a simple relational data model for a school booking system and identify primary and foreign keys.

Chapter 1: Legal systems, sources and precedent

Core learning notes

Legal systems derive rules from sources such as legislation, judicial precedent and constitutional principles. In common-law systems, courts interpret statutes and apply binding decisions according to hierarchy and the doctrine of precedent. Distinguish ratio decidendi from obiter dicta.

Worked example / application

A lower court generally follows the relevant ratio of a binding higher-court decision where the material facts and legal issue are sufficiently similar.

Examination practice

Explain how precedent promotes consistency while allowing legal development.

Chapter 2: Contract formation and remedies

Core learning notes

A contract generally requires offer, acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations and sufficient certainty, subject to jurisdiction-specific rules. Terms may be express or implied. Breach can lead to remedies such as damages, specific performance or rescission where available.

Worked example / application

If a supplier fails to deliver goods by an agreed date, assess the contractual term, causation, loss and mitigation before considering damages.

Examination practice

Apply contract principles to a scenario and evaluate the most suitable remedy.

Chapter 3: Criminal liability, evidence and justice

Core learning notes

Criminal liability commonly requires proof of prohibited conduct (actus reus) and the required mental element (mens rea), subject to offences and defences defined by law. Procedural safeguards, standards of proof and admissibility rules protect fairness.

Worked example / application

For a basic offence, identify the conduct element, the required fault element and whether the evidence supports each element beyond the applicable standard.

Examination practice

Analyse a hypothetical criminal problem by separating actus reus, mens rea, defences and evidential issues.

Psychology — IAL Online Notes

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Research methods and scientific reasoning

Core learning notes

Psychology uses experiments, observations, surveys, interviews and case studies to investigate behaviour and mental processes. Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether a method measures what it claims. Sampling, operationalisation, controls and ethics affect the strength of conclusions.

Worked example / application

An experiment testing memory should operationalise recall, standardise instructions and control distractions. Random allocation can reduce systematic participant differences between conditions.

Examination practice

Evaluate the validity and reliability of a proposed memory experiment.

Chapter 2: Biological, cognitive and learning approaches

Core learning notes

Biological explanations examine the brain, nervous system, hormones and genetics. Cognitive approaches study processes such as attention, memory and perception. Learning approaches explain behaviour through conditioning, reinforcement, punishment and observational learning. Each approach has strengths and limitations.

Worked example / application

In classical conditioning, a neutral stimulus becomes associated with an unconditioned stimulus and can eventually elicit a conditioned response. Identify each component in a real-life example.

Examination practice

Compare two psychological approaches to explaining an observed behaviour.

Chapter 3: Social, developmental and individual differences

Core learning notes

Social psychology examines influence, conformity, obedience and group processes. Developmental psychology considers change across the lifespan. Individual-differences research explores variation in personality, intelligence and mental health. Interpret findings with attention to culture, context and ethical limits.

Worked example / application

A conformity study should consider group size, unanimity, task ambiguity and participant awareness when interpreting behaviour.

Examination practice

Discuss how culture and situational factors may limit the generalisability of a psychological finding.

Independent study notes with explanations, applied examples and examination practice.

Chapter 1: Spanish grammar and accurate communication

Core learning notes

Spanish nouns and adjectives agree in gender and number. Verbs are conjugated for person, number, tense and mood. Distinguish ser and estar, preterite and imperfect, and the indicative and subjunctive. Pronouns and connectors help create precise, coherent communication.

Worked example / application

Ayer estudié durante dos horas uses the preterite for a completed event. Cuando era niño, leía mucho uses the imperfect for a habitual past situation.

Examination practice

Choose between preterite and imperfect in a short narrative and justify each choice.

Chapter 2: Listening, reading and translation

Core learning notes

Comprehension requires identifying main ideas, specific information, inference, opinion and tone. Use context, cognates and grammatical signals without assuming every similar-looking word has the same meaning. Translation should preserve meaning and register in natural Spanish.

Worked example / application

Translate 'If I had more time, I would travel more' using a correct conditional structure: Si tuviera más tiempo, viajaría más.

Examination practice

Summarise the argument of a Spanish article and identify evidence of the writer's attitude.

Chapter 3: Speaking, writing and Hispanic societies

Core learning notes

Develop arguments using reasons, examples, contrasts and conclusions. Themes may include education, employment, environment, technology, equality, culture and regional identities across the Spanish-speaking world. Avoid treating diverse countries and communities as culturally uniform.

Worked example / application

A balanced response about tourism can weigh employment and cultural exchange against housing pressure, environmental impact and seasonal work.

Examination practice

Prepare an oral presentation in Spanish evaluating a contemporary issue with a clear conclusion.
