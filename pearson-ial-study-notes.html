Biology

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Cell membranes and transport

Textbook explanation

Cell membranes follow the fluid-mosaic model: phospholipids form a bilayer with embedded proteins, cholesterol and carbohydrate-containing molecules. Selective permeability regulates exchange. Diffusion moves particles down a concentration gradient; facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins; active transport uses energy to move substances against a gradient; osmosis is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.

Worked application

A solute transported from low to high concentration requires an energy source and a suitable carrier protein. Compare its movement with diffusion, which proceeds down the concentration gradient.

Practice and reflection

Explain why active transport requires energy and describe two membrane adaptations.

Chapter 2: DNA, genes and protein synthesis

Textbook explanation

DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands joined by complementary base pairing: adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine with guanine. A gene is a DNA sequence that can be transcribed to RNA. During transcription, RNA polymerase builds a complementary RNA strand; during translation, ribosomes read codons and assemble amino acids using tRNA.

Worked application

If a DNA template triplet is TAC, the complementary mRNA codon is AUG. Codons are read in groups of three and specify amino acids or stop signals.

Practice and reflection

Describe transcription and translation, and explain how a base substitution may affect a protein.

Chapter 3: Energy transfer: respiration and photosynthesis

Textbook explanation

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in organic molecules. The light-dependent reactions occur in thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and reduced NADP; carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle uses carbon dioxide to build triose phosphate. Respiration transfers energy from organic molecules to ATP through glycolysis, link reaction, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

Worked application

A limiting factor is a factor in shortest supply relative to demand. Increasing light intensity raises photosynthetic rate only until another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, limits the process.

Practice and reflection

Compare the roles of ATP in photosynthesis and respiration. Explain how to investigate a limiting factor.

Chapter 4: Inheritance, selection and populations

Textbook explanation

Alleles segregate during meiosis and combine at fertilisation. Independent assortment and crossing over generate genetic variation. Natural selection changes allele frequencies when heritable traits affect survival and reproductive success. Population genetics can be used to examine allele frequencies under stated assumptions.

Worked application

For a simple dominant/recessive monohybrid cross Aa × Aa, expected genotypes are 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, assuming equal segregation and random fertilisation.

Practice and reflection

Use a genetic cross to predict genotype proportions and explain how selection can change a population.

Chemistry

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Atomic structure, bonding and periodicity

Textbook explanation

Electrons occupy quantised energy levels and orbitals. The arrangement of electrons explains periodic trends and chemical behaviour. Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; covalent bonding involves shared electron pairs; metallic bonding involves positive ions attracted to delocalised electrons.

Worked application

A covalent bond forms when orbitals overlap and electron density is shared between nuclei. Bond polarity depends on the difference in electronegativity and molecular geometry.

Practice and reflection

Explain how electron configuration relates to periodicity and compare ionic and covalent bonding.

Chapter 2: Energetics and reaction kinetics

Textbook explanation

Enthalpy change measures heat energy transferred at constant pressure. Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. Reaction rate depends on successful collision frequency and activation energy. Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

Worked application

Estimate reaction enthalpy using mean bond enthalpies: ΔH ≈ sum of bonds broken − sum of bonds formed. State clearly that this is an estimate because mean bond enthalpies are averaged values.

Practice and reflection

Calculate an enthalpy change from bond enthalpies and explain how a catalyst changes a rate profile.

Chapter 3: Equilibria, acids and redox

Textbook explanation

Dynamic equilibrium occurs in a closed system when forward and reverse reaction rates are equal. Equilibrium position responds to concentration, pressure and temperature changes. Acids donate protons; bases accept protons. Oxidation involves electron loss or an increase in oxidation state; reduction involves electron gain or a decrease in oxidation state.

Worked application

For a gaseous equilibrium, increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas, provided the gaseous mole numbers differ. A catalyst changes the time to reach equilibrium, not the equilibrium constant.

Practice and reflection

Predict an equilibrium shift and identify oxidation and reduction using oxidation states.

Chapter 4: Organic chemistry and analytical methods

Textbook explanation

Organic compounds are classified by functional groups and reaction patterns. Isomerism may be structural or stereoisomeric. Reaction mechanisms describe electron-pair movement and help explain substitution, addition, elimination and oxidation. Analytical methods include chromatography, infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry.

Worked application

In proton NMR, chemical shift gives information about the chemical environment of hydrogen atoms; integration estimates relative proton numbers and splitting gives information about neighbouring hydrogens.

Practice and reflection

Identify a functional group from a structure and explain how one analytical technique supports identification.

Physics

Open a chapter to study the core ideas and practise applying them.

Chapter 1: Mechanics: motion, forces and energy

Textbook explanation

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are vector quantities. The gradient of a displacement-time graph gives velocity; the gradient of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration; the area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement. Newton's laws connect resultant force, mass and acceleration.

Worked application

For constant acceleration, v = u + at and s = ut + ½at². Select an equation containing the known quantities and the required unknown, and use consistent SI units.

Practice and reflection

A vehicle changes speed from 8 m s⁻¹ to 20 m s⁻¹ in 4 s. Calculate acceleration and describe the resultant force direction.

Chapter 2: Waves, superposition and optics

Textbook explanation

A progressive wave transfers energy without net transfer of matter. Wave speed is v = fλ. Superposition occurs when waves overlap; coherent sources have a constant phase difference and can produce stable interference. Refraction occurs when wave speed changes at a boundary.

Worked application

For a wave of frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 0.40 m, speed = 50 × 0.40 = 20 m s⁻¹. In a double-slit experiment, fringe spacing depends on wavelength, screen distance and slit separation.

Practice and reflection

Explain constructive and destructive interference and calculate wave speed from frequency and wavelength.

Chapter 3: Electricity, fields and electromagnetism

Textbook explanation

Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V = W/Q. Resistance is the ratio V/I for an ohmic conductor at constant physical conditions. Electric and magnetic fields describe forces acting at a distance.

Worked application

For a resistor with potential difference 12 V and current 0.50 A, R = V/I = 24 Ω. Electrical power may be calculated using P = IV, I²R or V²/R.

Practice and reflection

Explain the relationship between current, potential difference and resistance and describe energy transfer in a circuit.

Chapter 4: Materials, nuclear physics and quantum ideas

Textbook explanation

Material behaviour depends on microscopic structure and bonding. Nuclear decay is random for individual nuclei but predictable statistically for large samples. Activity is the rate of decay; half-life is the time for activity or number of undecayed nuclei to halve. Quantum models describe energy transfer in discrete packets.

Worked application

After three half-lives, the remaining undecayed fraction is (1/2)³ = 1/8, or 12.5% of the original amount.

Practice and reflection

Calculate remaining activity after several half-lives and explain why radioactive decay is described as random.

Mathematics

Chapter 1: Algebra, functions and mathematical proof

Textbook explanation

Algebraic methods include expansion, factorisation, completing the square and solving equations. A function maps each permitted input to exactly one output. Domain restrictions may arise from denominators, roots or context. Mathematical proof uses logically connected statements to establish a result for all permitted cases.

Worked application

For f(x)=x²−4x+1, completing the square gives f(x)=(x−2)²−3. The minimum value is −3 at x=2.

Practice and reflection

Complete the square for a quadratic and state its turning point. Explain the domain of a rational function.

Chapter 2: Trigonometry, exponentials and logarithms

Textbook explanation

Trigonometric functions model periodic behaviour and satisfy identities such as sin²x + cos²x = 1. Exponential functions model proportional growth or decay. Logarithms are inverse operations to exponentiation and obey laws derived from index rules.

Worked application

If 3ˣ = 20, take logarithms: x = log(20)/log(3). Check the value by substitution into the original equation.

Practice and reflection

Solve an exponential equation using logarithms and use a trigonometric identity to simplify an expression.

Chapter 3: Differentiation, integration and applications

Textbook explanation

Differentiation gives the gradient function and rates of change. Stationary points occur where the derivative is zero. Integration reverses differentiation and can calculate areas or accumulated change. Constants of integration are required for indefinite integrals.

Worked application

If y = 3x² − 4x + 7, then dy/dx = 6x − 4. At a stationary point, 6x − 4 = 0, so x = 2/3.

Practice and reflection

Differentiate a polynomial, find stationary points and explain how the second derivative classifies a turning point.

Chapter 4: Statistics, probability and mechanics

Textbook explanation

Probability models uncertainty using events and distributions. Conditional probability updates a probability when information is known. Statistical inference uses sample data to estimate or test claims about a population. Mechanics applies mathematical models to forces and motion.

Worked application

For independent events A and B, P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). For dependent events, use P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B|A). Always check whether independence is justified.

Practice and reflection

Distinguish independent and mutually exclusive events and set up a simple mechanics model with a force diagram.
