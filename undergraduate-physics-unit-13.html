1. Quantum foundations and Planck's hypothesis

Classical physics treats energy exchange as continuous. Blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect exposed failures of this assumption at microscopic scales. Planck proposed that an oscillator of frequency ν exchanges energy in packets hν. Einstein extended the quantum idea to light, with photon energy E=hν and momentum p=h/λ. The threshold frequency in the photoelectric effect follows from Kmax=hν−φ. Intensity controls photon flux, whereas frequency controls energy per photon. The Planck constant is h=6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J s and ħ=h/(2π).

The de Broglie relation λ=h/p assigns a wavelength to material particles. For non-relativistic kinetic energy K, p=√(2mK), hence λ=h/√(2mK). Electron diffraction confirms that matter waves are experimentally real. At relativistic energies use pc=√(K²+2Kmc²), not the non-relativistic momentum expression.

2. Wavefunction and Born probability

A pure state is represented by |ψ⟩ or a wavefunction ψ(r,t). The Born rule identifies |ψ|² as probability density. The probability in a region Ω is PΩ=∫Ω|ψ|²dτ. A bound-state wavefunction must be square-integrable and normalised so ∫all space|ψ|²dτ=1. A physically acceptable wavefunction is single-valued and finite; at a finite potential discontinuity, ψ and its first derivative are continuous. At an infinite wall, ψ vanishes.

The wavefunction is a probability amplitude, not a material displacement. Complex phase matters in interference: amplitudes are added before taking the modulus squared. A global phase e^(iα) does not change measurement probabilities, but relative phase between components can alter interference. If ψ is not normalised, choose N such that |N|²∫|ψ|²dτ=1.

3. Superposition and Hilbert-space structure

Quantum states form a complex vector space with inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩=∫φ*ψdτ. If ψ₁ and ψ₂ are allowed states, c₁ψ₁+c₂ψ₂ is also allowed for a linear theory. Orthonormal states satisfy ⟨φm|φn⟩=δmn. Expansion in a complete orthonormal basis gives ψ=Σcnφn with cn=⟨φn|ψ⟩. For a normalised state, Σ|cn|²=1 and |cn|² is the probability of the corresponding basis outcome.

The cross term in |ψ₁+ψ₂|² produces interference. This is why probabilities cannot generally be added as though the alternatives were distinguishable classical events. Orthogonality expresses zero inner-product overlap, and completeness means the basis can represent every state in the relevant space.

4. Operators and observables

An observable is represented by a Hermitian operator on an appropriate domain. Hermiticity ensures real eigenvalues. Position is x̂=x; momentum in position representation is p̂=−iħ∇; the non-relativistic Hamiltonian is Ĥ=−(ħ²/2m)∇²+V. An eigenstate satisfies Â|a⟩=a|a⟩. The expectation value is ⟨A⟩=⟨ψ|Â|ψ⟩ for a normalised state, and the variance is (ΔA)²=⟨A²⟩−⟨A⟩².

The expectation value is the statistical mean of many measurements on identically prepared systems; it need not be an outcome in one measurement. A state with zero variance in A is an eigenstate of A. Differential operators also require boundary conditions and domains; formal integration by parts alone does not establish self-adjointness.

5. Time-dependent Schrödinger equation

The fundamental non-relativistic evolution equation is iħ∂ψ/∂t=Ĥψ. For a time-independent Hamiltonian, separation ψ(r,t)=φ(r)T(t) gives T=exp(−iEt/ħ) and Ĥφ=Eφ. In one dimension, −(ħ²/2m)d²φ/dx²+V(x)φ=Eφ. The equation is first order in time and second order in position. It determines evolution from an initial state when the Hamiltonian and suitable boundary conditions are specified.

The probability density ρ=|ψ|² obeys ∂ρ/∂t+∇·j=0, where j=(ħ/2mi)(ψ*∇ψ−ψ∇ψ*). This continuity equation expresses conservation of total probability. The scalar Schrödinger equation does not intrinsically include spin and is not relativistically invariant.

6. Infinite square well: complete derivation

For a well with V=0 on 0<x<L and infinite potential outside, the stationary equation inside is φ''+k²φ=0, where k²=2mE/ħ². The general solution is A sin(kx)+B cos(kx). The boundary condition φ(0)=0 gives B=0. The second boundary φ(L)=0 requires sin(kL)=0, so kL=nπ with n=1,2,…. Normalisation ∫₀ᴸ|φ|²dx=1 gives φn=√(2/L)sin(nπx/L). Substitution yields En=n²π²ħ²/(2mL²).

The spectrum is discrete because only standing waves satisfying both boundaries are allowed. The ground-state energy is non-zero, and En scales as 1/L². The nth state has n−1 internal nodes. Adjacent gaps are not equal: E(n+1)−En=(2n+1)E1. For every stationary state, symmetry gives ⟨x⟩=L/2.

7. Finite wells and quantum tunnelling

A finite well allows wavefunction penetration into classically forbidden regions. For a rectangular barrier of height V₀ and particle energy E<V₀, the decay constant is κ=√(2m(V₀−E))/ħ. For a sufficiently thick barrier, transmission scales approximately as exp(−2κa), where a is barrier width. Tunnelling explains alpha decay, scanning tunnelling microscopy, tunnel diodes and sub-barrier nuclear fusion.

At finite interfaces, match ψ and dψ/dx. Bound-state energies in finite wells are roots of transcendental equations. A semi-infinite forbidden region has an evanescent wave and no transmitted current; a finite barrier can support a non-zero transmitted current. The exponential dependence makes transmission very sensitive to width and barrier height.

8. Quantum harmonic oscillator

For V=½mω²x², Ĥ=p̂²/(2m)+½mω²x². Define ladder operators a and a† so that Ĥ=ħω(a†a+½), with [a,a†]=1. The energies are En=ħω(n+½), n=0,1,2,…. The ground-state energy ħω/2 is the zero-point energy. The ladder operators act as a|n⟩=√n|n−1⟩ and a†|n⟩=√(n+1)|n+1⟩.

The ground-state wavefunction is φ₀=(mω/πħ)^(1/4)exp[−mωx²/(2ħ)]. Excited states contain Hermite polynomials and have n nodes. The oscillator approximates motion near a stable equilibrium and is central to molecular vibrations, lattice phonons and field modes.

9. Measurement and state projection

For an observable with eigenstates |an⟩, the probability of outcome an in state |ψ⟩ is |⟨an|ψ⟩|². An ideal measurement yielding an projects the state into that eigenspace. For a degenerate eigenvalue, projection is onto the full eigenspace. Repeated ideal measurements of the same observable on the resulting eigenstate return the same value with probability one.

Distinguish the pre-measurement state, the measured operator, the possible eigenvalues, their probabilities and the post-measurement state. Measurement theory predicts statistical outcomes; interpretive questions about what constitutes a measurement go beyond the calculation rules used in this unit.

10. Commutators and uncertainty

11. Scattering at a potential step

For a step from V=0 to V=V₁ with E>V₁, k₁=√(2mE)/ħ and k₂=√[2m(E−V₁)]/ħ. Matching ψ and its derivative gives r=(k₁−k₂)/(k₁+k₂) and t=2k₁/(k₁+k₂). Reflection probability is R=|r|². Transmission probability is the ratio of transmitted to incident probability currents, T=(k₂/k₁)|t|². Current conservation gives R+T=1.

When E<V₁ the transmitted solution is evanescent. For a finite barrier the decaying and growing solutions within the barrier must both be included before matching at the second boundary. Using |t|² alone as T is a common error when the wave numbers differ.

12. Angular momentum

Orbital angular momentum L̂=r̂×p̂ obeys [Lx,Ly]=iħLz and cyclic permutations. L² and Lz commute and have simultaneous eigenvalues l(l+1)ħ² and mħ. Here l=0,1,2,… and m=−l,…,+l. The angular eigenfunctions are spherical harmonics Ylᵐ. For fixed l there are 2l+1 allowed m values. Ladder operators L±=Lx±iLy raise or lower m without changing l.

The direction of angular momentum is not fully specified by simultaneous values of L² and one component. Angular momentum algebra is essential for central potentials, atomic spectra, selection rules and spin coupling.

13. Hydrogen atom

For a hydrogen-like ion, V(r)=−Ze²/(4πε₀r). Separation in spherical coordinates gives radial and angular equations. The bound-state energies are En=−μZ²e⁴/[2(4πε₀)²ħ²n²], where μ is the electron–nucleus reduced mass. For hydrogen the ground-state energy is approximately −13.6 eV. Quantum numbers n, l and m arise from normalisability and regularity.

The energy depends on n in the ideal Coulomb model, producing degeneracy. The radial probability distribution includes the radial volume factor; the most probable radius is not generally equal to the expectation value ⟨r⟩. Spectral photon energy satisfies |ΔE|=hν.

14. Spin and Pauli matrices

Spin is intrinsic angular momentum, not a literal classical rotation of an extended particle. A spin-½ particle has two outcomes ±ħ/2 for a chosen component. The Pauli matrices are σx=[[0,1],[1,0]], σy=[[0,−i],[i,0]], σz=[[1,0],[0,−1]], and Ŝ=(ħ/2)σ. A magnetic field couples to the magnetic moment and produces Zeeman splitting.

The Stern–Gerlach experiment separates a beam into discrete components in a non-uniform magnetic field. A spin state is a two-component spinor. Rotations act on spinors differently from ordinary three-dimensional vectors, a feature with important consequences for quantum measurement and magnetic resonance.

15. Identical particles and exchange symmetry

Interchanging identical particles cannot produce a physically distinguishable state. Bosonic states are symmetric under exchange; fermionic states are antisymmetric. The Pauli exclusion principle follows for identical fermions: two fermions cannot occupy the same complete one-particle state. For electrons, the total state combines spatial and spin parts so that the full state is antisymmetric.

A Slater determinant constructs an antisymmetric many-fermion state. Symmetrised products are used for bosons. Exchange symmetry is foundational to atomic shell structure, chemical bonding, Fermi pressure and quantum statistics.

16. Time-independent perturbation theory

Write Ĥ=Ĥ₀+λV̂, with λ small. For a non-degenerate unperturbed state |n⁰⟩, the first-order energy correction is E_n^(1)=⟨n⁰|V̂|n⁰⟩. The second-order correction is Σ_{m≠n}|⟨m⁰|V̂|n⁰⟩|²/(E_n⁰−E_m⁰). The method is useful for weak fields, small potential distortions and fine-structure corrections.

Near degeneracy, denominators become small and non-degenerate perturbation theory fails. Diagonalise the perturbation within the degenerate subspace. Compare corrections with the unperturbed energy scale and state why the perturbation is small.

17. Variational method

For a normalised trial state ψ, ⟨ψ|Ĥ|ψ⟩ is not below the exact ground-state energy. Choose a trial function with adjustable parameters, calculate the expectation energy, and minimise it. The resulting value is an upper bound. A useful trial function respects symmetry, boundary behaviour and the correct limiting form.

The method is valuable when exact differential equations cannot be solved. A more flexible trial family can improve the upper bound, but a good ground-state estimate does not automatically provide accurate excited-state energies.

18. Time-dependent transitions

A weak time-dependent perturbation induces transitions between stationary states. To first order, the amplitude from i to f contains the time integral of ⟨f|V̂(t)|i⟩exp(iωfi t)/(iħ), where ωfi=(Ef−Ei)/ħ. The probability is the modulus squared. Periodic driving produces resonance near the transition frequency. Fermi's golden rule relates transition rate to squared matrix element and density of final states.

Selection rules arise when symmetry forces a matrix element to vanish. Electric-dipole transitions have angular momentum and parity constraints. The weak-perturbation approximation is valid only while transition probabilities remain sufficiently small.

19. Laboratory and computational quantum physics

Quantum phenomena are measured through spectra, diffraction patterns, tunnelling currents, detector counts and coincidence statistics. Photon counting often begins with a Poisson model, for which standard deviation is approximately √N for N independent counts. Background counts, dead time, detector efficiency, finite resolution and calibration drift must be considered.

A finite-difference calculation discretises the Hamiltonian and solves a matrix eigenvalue problem. Verify normalisation, orthogonality, boundary conditions and convergence under grid refinement. Numerical eigenvectors have arbitrary global phase, so compare phase-invariant quantities or align phases before comparing wavefunctions.

Worked numerical examples

Use guard digits during calculation and round only the final result. Each result should be checked for units and physical scale.

Example 1. Electron accelerated through 150 V

K=eV=2.403×10⁻¹⁷ J. λ=h/√(2mₑK)≈1.00×10⁻¹⁰ m. The wavelength is comparable to atomic lattice spacings, making electron diffraction observable.

Example 2. Particle in a 1.00 nm electron well

E₁=h²/(8mₑL²)=6.02×10⁻²⁰ J≈0.376 eV. If L is halved, E₁ increases by a factor of four because E₁∝L⁻².

Example 3. Third level of the same well

E₃=9E₁≈3.38 eV. The gap E₃−E₂=(9−4)E₁=1.88 eV.

Example 4. Photon at 500 nm

E=hc/λ≈3.97×10⁻¹⁹ J=2.48 eV. A flux of 10¹⁵ photons per second carries approximately 3.97×10⁻⁴ W.

Example 5. Photoelectric stopping energy

For φ=2.0 eV and photon energy 3.2 eV, Kmax=1.2 eV. Increasing intensity at fixed frequency raises emission rate, not maximum electron energy.

Example 6. Uncertainty estimate

If Δx=0.10 nm, Δp≥ħ/(2Δx)=5.27×10⁻²⁵ kg m s⁻¹. For an electron this corresponds to Δv≥5.79×10⁵ m s⁻¹.

Example 7. Oscillator level separation

For ν=5.0×10¹³ Hz, ΔE=hν=3.31×10⁻²⁰ J=0.207 eV. The gap is independent of the quantum number.

Example 8. Barrier decay factor

For an electron and V₀−E=1.0 eV, κ≈5.12 nm⁻¹. At a=0.50 nm, exp(−2κa)≈0.0059, showing strong width sensitivity.

Example 9. Normalising a constant state

For ψ=A on 0≤x≤L, |A|²L=1, so A=1/√L after choosing a real positive global phase.

Example 10. Hydrogen transition photon

For a transition n=3 to n=2, ΔE=13.6(1/4−1/9) eV≈1.89 eV. The emitted wavelength is λ=hc/ΔE≈656 nm.

Laboratory and computational investigations

Electron diffraction

Use an electron diffraction tube, calibrated accelerating supply and polycrystalline graphite target. Record ring diameters for several accelerating voltages. Convert radii to scattering angles using the screen geometry, then apply Bragg's law 2d sinθ=nλ and compare with λ=h/√(2mₑeV). Plot inferred wavelength against 1/√V. Account for uncertainty in ring centre, screen distance, accelerating voltage and graphite lattice spacing. Relativistic correction is required as accelerating voltage increases.

Photoelectric effect

Measure stopping potential for several known frequencies. Plot stopping potential against frequency; the gradient is h/e and the intercept is −φ/e. Use regression, report the uncertainty in gradient and intercept, and check residuals. Control stray light, contact potentials, wavelength calibration and photocathode condition. Intensity changes at fixed frequency test emission rate, not the energy-frequency gradient.

Numerical eigenvalue calculation

Discretise the one-dimensional Hamiltonian using ψ''(xᵢ)≈[ψᵢ₊₁−2ψᵢ+ψᵢ₋₁]/Δx². Construct the tridiagonal kinetic-energy matrix and add the potential on the diagonal. Impose boundary conditions, diagonalise, normalise each eigenvector with the grid measure and compare eigenvalues for successively smaller Δx. Check orthogonality and domain-size convergence. Compare the infinite-well numerical energies with the analytic n² scaling.

Uncertainty and reporting

For y=f(x₁,…,xₙ), independent standard uncertainties propagate as uᵧ²=Σ(∂f/∂xᵢ)²uᵢ². Include covariance terms for correlated variables. Separate statistical scatter from calibration and model uncertainty. Record raw observations, instrument resolution, fitting method, residuals, repeat count and justified exclusions. Never modify a result simply to make it agree with theory.

Safety

High-voltage electron tubes require enclosed terminals, interlocks and supervised use. Never open energised equipment. Laser work requires appropriate wavelength-rated eye protection, beam stops and controlled beam paths. Follow institutional electrical, laser and radiation procedures; never bypass shielding or safety interlocks.

Common misconceptions and revision checklist

  • ψ is not the probability density; |ψ|² is.
  • Complex conjugation is required in inner products.
  • Amplitudes are combined before squaring.
  • Not every observable has a discrete spectrum.
  • Transmission probability requires probability current.
  • Spin is not classical rotation of a small sphere.
  • Boundary conditions are part of the eigenvalue problem.
  • Degenerate perturbation theory requires diagonalisation within the degenerate subspace.
  • Numerical results require convergence, normalisation and orthogonality checks.

University examination questions with model answers

In a formal examination, show intermediate steps, define symbols, state assumptions and include units.

1. State Born's rule.

Model answer: The probability density is |ψ|²; integrate it over the region of interest. A normalised state integrates to one over all space.

2. Why is a bound state normalisable?

Model answer: Because |ψ|² is a probability density and its total integral must be finite and set to unity.

3. Derive the infinite-well spectrum.

Model answer: Solve φ''+k²φ=0, impose φ(0)=φ(L)=0 to get k=nπ/L, then use E=ħ²k²/(2m).

4. Why is the ground-state energy non-zero?

Model answer: The boundary conditions require a non-zero standing-wave number; a constant zero-energy state cannot satisfy both walls.

5. Distinguish amplitude and probability.

Model answer: Amplitudes may be complex; probability is the modulus squared. Add amplitudes before squaring to retain interference.

6. Write the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

Model answer: iħ∂ψ/∂t=Ĥψ, where ħ is the reduced Planck constant and Ĥ is the Hamiltonian.

7. What is an observable operator?

Model answer: A Hermitian operator representing a measurable quantity, with real eigenvalues on its appropriate domain.

8. Explain tunnelling.

Model answer: A wavefunction penetrates a finite classically forbidden barrier, producing a non-zero transmission probability.

9. State the uncertainty relation.

Model answer: ΔxΔp≥ħ/2, which follows from [x̂,p̂]=iħ.

10. What is a stationary state?

Model answer: A state whose probability density is time-independent; its time dependence is a global phase for an energy eigenstate.

11. Define degeneracy.

Model answer: Two or more linearly independent eigenstates share the same eigenvalue.

12. What is zero-point energy?

Model answer: The oscillator ground-state energy ħω/2, which cannot be reduced to zero.

13. What are bosons and fermions?

Model answer: Bosons have symmetric exchange states; fermions have antisymmetric exchange states and obey exclusion.

14. What does a commutator express?

Model answer: The failure of operator products to commute; non-zero commutators underpin uncertainty relations.

15. Give the first-order energy correction.

Model answer: E_n^(1)=⟨n⁰|V̂|n⁰⟩ for a non-degenerate unperturbed level.

16. What is the variational principle?

Model answer: The expectation energy of any normalised trial state is an upper bound to the exact ground-state energy.

17. Why use probability current in scattering?

Model answer: Because incident and transmitted wave numbers may differ, so |t|² alone need not equal transmission probability.

18. What is spin?

Model answer: Intrinsic angular momentum represented for spin one-half by a two-component spinor and Pauli matrices.

19. What did Stern–Gerlach demonstrate?

Model answer: Discrete outcomes for a spin component in a non-uniform magnetic field.

20. Why does perturbation theory fail near degeneracy?

Model answer: Small energy denominators make corrections large; diagonalise within the degenerate subspace first.

21. What is a selection rule?

Model answer: A symmetry-derived condition that determines whether a transition matrix element can be non-zero.

22. How do you validate a numerical eigenstate?

Model answer: Check normalisation, orthogonality, boundary conditions and convergence under grid or basis refinement.

23. Explain exchange antisymmetry for electrons.

Model answer: Swapping electron labels changes the sign of the total state; identical complete one-particle states make the antisymmetric determinant vanish.

24. Why does intensity not raise photoelectron Kmax at fixed frequency?

Model answer: Each photon still carries hν; intensity changes photon arrival rate, not energy per photon.

25. Give two limitations of Schrödinger theory.
