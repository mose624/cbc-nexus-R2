Role of research in nursing

Nursing research develops, tests and applies knowledge to improve patient outcomes, service quality, education and professional practice. It can explore experiences, describe populations, identify associations, test interventions or evaluate service changes. Research should address relevant questions and use methods suited to the purpose.

Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research evidence with clinical expertise, patient values and preferences, and the local care context. Evidence does not replace professional judgement or shared decision-making. Nurses should recognise uncertainty and avoid treating one study as a universal rule.

Developing a researchable question

A research question identifies the population or phenomenon, the issue of interest and, where relevant, the intervention, comparator and outcome. PICO is commonly used for focused intervention questions: Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome. Qualitative questions may instead explore experiences, meanings or processes.

A good question is clear, focused, feasible, ethical and significant. Define terms and outcomes before selecting methods. Consider whether the question can be answered with available time, skills, access and resources, and whether the proposed study duplicates existing evidence without a meaningful reason.

Literature searching and evidence hierarchy

A literature search uses structured concepts, synonyms, subject headings and appropriate databases. Record search dates, databases, terms, limits and inclusion criteria so the process can be understood and repeated. Search strategy should be broad enough to identify relevant evidence while remaining focused on the question.

Evidence hierarchies vary by question type. Systematic reviews of high-quality studies may be useful for intervention questions, while qualitative synthesis, diagnostic accuracy studies, cohort studies or guidelines may be more appropriate for other questions. Study design alone does not guarantee quality; assess bias, relevance and certainty.

Research designs and methodology

Quantitative research measures variables numerically and may use experimental, quasi-experimental, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional or descriptive designs. Qualitative research explores experience, meaning and social processes through approaches such as interviews, focus groups, observation and document analysis. Mixed-methods research integrates both approaches.

Choose a design that answers the question and addresses threats to validity or credibility. Randomisation can reduce confounding in trials, but feasibility and ethics may limit its use. Qualitative sampling aims for relevant depth and variation rather than statistical representativeness. Describe methods transparently.

Sampling, variables and measurement

A population is the group a study seeks to understand; a sample is the subset from which data are collected. Probability sampling gives eligible members a known selection chance, while non-probability approaches include convenience, purposive and snowball sampling. Sampling affects representativeness and transferability.

Variables may be categorical or numerical, independent or dependent, and measured on nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio scales. Validity concerns whether a tool measures what it is intended to measure; reliability concerns consistency. Check language adaptation, calibration, scoring instructions and suitability for the target population.

Research ethics and governance

Ethical research respects autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, privacy and scientific integrity. Participants need understandable information about purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation and withdrawal. Additional safeguards may be needed for children, people with impaired decision-making capacity or other vulnerable groups.

Obtain approval from the appropriate research ethics committee and institutional or regulatory authorities before recruitment or data collection where required. Consent does not remove the researcher's duty to minimise harm. Protect identifiable data, disclose conflicts of interest, report adverse events and avoid fabrication, falsification or plagiarism.

Data collection and data management

Data collection methods include structured questionnaires, interviews, observation, clinical records, physiological measurements and validated scales. Standard operating procedures and staff training reduce variation. Pilot testing can identify unclear questions, workflow problems and missing response options before the main study.

Create a data dictionary, define coding rules, restrict access and maintain secure backups according to approved governance. De-identify data where possible and retain linkage keys separately. Document missing data, corrections and exclusions transparently; never alter values to produce a preferred result.
