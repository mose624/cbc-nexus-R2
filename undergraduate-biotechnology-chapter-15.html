Conceptual workflow

1. Responsible biotechnology and governance

Biotechnology operates within scientific, legal, ethical and social systems. Governance includes laws, regulations, institutional policies, professional standards, public participation and international agreements. Requirements differ by product, organism, intended use and jurisdiction. A research method permitted in one setting may require additional authorisation or be prohibited in another.

Responsible innovation considers anticipated benefits, uncertainty, distribution of risks, reversibility, stakeholder perspectives and alternatives. It is not a final approval step added after development; it should shape research questions, design choices, communication and monitoring from the beginning.

2. Biosafety and risk assessment

Biosafety aims to prevent unintended exposure to biological agents or their accidental release. Risk assessment identifies hazards, exposure routes, likelihood, consequences and uncertainties, then selects proportionate controls. Relevant factors include agent properties, concentration, volume, procedures, aerosols, sharps, host susceptibility, facility design, waste streams and staff competence.

Controls follow a hierarchy: eliminate or substitute hazards where feasible, use engineering controls, establish administrative procedures and training, and use personal protective equipment as an additional layer. Containment level is not selected by organism name alone; the activity-specific risk assessment and competent institutional authority determine appropriate controls.

3. Biosecurity and dual-use considerations

Biosecurity addresses prevention of loss, theft, misuse, diversion or unauthorised access to biological materials, equipment, technology and sensitive information. Measures may include inventory control, access management, secure storage, personnel responsibilities, transport procedures and incident reporting. Biosecurity complements but is distinct from biosafety.

Dual-use research can generate knowledge, products or technologies with both beneficial and harmful potential. Responsible review considers plausible misuse pathways, benefits, alternatives, information sensitivity and proportionate mitigation. The objective is not to suppress legitimate science indiscriminately but to identify and manage specific risks through accountable governance.

4. Regulation of products and clinical translation

Regulatory pathways depend on whether a biotechnology output is a medicine, vaccine, diagnostic, food, feed, agricultural organism, environmental intervention or research tool. Evidence may include identity, quality, manufacturing consistency, safety, efficacy or performance, environmental assessment and post-market monitoring. Classification and requirements differ across countries and agencies.

Clinical translation progresses through staged evidence generation and independent review. Ethical approval, informed consent, participant protection, trial registration where required, good clinical practice, data integrity and transparent reporting are essential. Research success does not imply marketing authorisation or professional endorsement.

5. Research ethics, consent and data governance

Research ethics addresses respect for persons, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, privacy and scientific integrity. Human biospecimens and genomic data can reveal sensitive information about individuals and relatives. Consent should be understandable and appropriately scoped; secondary use, withdrawal limits, data sharing and incidental findings require governance and communication plans.

Animal research requires justification, refinement, reduction and replacement where feasible, alongside competent oversight and humane endpoints. Community engagement is important where research affects local resources, traditional knowledge or collective interests. Benefits and burdens should not be distributed unfairly.

6. Intellectual property, access and public trust

Biotechnology intellectual property may include patents, trade secrets, copyright, plant variety rights and contractual know-how. Patentability, ownership, research exemptions, licensing and benefit-sharing vary by law and treaty. Intellectual property can support investment but may also affect access, follow-on research and affordability. Technology transfer should include training, quality systems, local capacity and sustainable maintenance, not only transfer of a patent or piece of equipment.

Public trust depends on honesty about evidence, uncertainty, conflicts of interest, failures and limitations. Risk communication should be two-way, culturally appropriate and responsive to legitimate concerns. Avoid absolute claims such as completely safe, risk-free or guaranteed.

Worked example: structuring a proportionate risk assessment

Consider a hypothetical teaching laboratory planning a non-clinical assay using a biological sample. A defensible assessment records the activity, biological material, potential hazards, exposure routes, people and environment at risk, existing controls, residual risk, further controls, responsible person and review date. The assessor checks whether aerosols, sharps, centrifugation, spills, transport or waste handling change the exposure profile.

The result is not a universal risk score detached from context. Controls must be reviewed by the institution's competent biosafety function and comply with applicable law. If the organism, scale, procedure or facility changes, reassess before work proceeds.

Case study: governance review of a proposed gene-editing project

A university team proposes a gene-editing study with potential agricultural application. The review identifies the intended organism and trait, research purpose, containment needs, possible off-target or unintended outcomes, environmental exposure routes, material transfer, data security, stakeholder concerns and relevant national rules. The team distinguishes contained laboratory research from field release and commercial deployment, which may have different approval requirements.

The governance plan assigns responsibilities, defines incident reporting, records materials, describes independent oversight and establishes decision gates. It includes transparent public communication and a plan to monitor unexpected outcomes. The project proceeds only when the appropriate institutional and regulatory approvals are in place.

Key technical glossary

Use these definitions as a starting point; some terms have narrower meanings in particular subdisciplines.

Biosafety
Principles, technologies and practices used to prevent unintentional exposure to biological agents or their accidental release.
Biosecurity
Measures that prevent loss, theft, misuse, diversion or unauthorised access to biological materials and related assets.
Hazard
Source or situation with potential to cause harm.
Risk
Combination of likelihood and consequence of a harmful event, considered with uncertainty and exposure.
Containment
Physical and procedural measures that restrict exposure or release.
Risk assessment
Structured process to identify hazards, evaluate risks and select proportionate controls.
Dual-use research
Research with potential for both beneficial applications and harmful misuse.
Informed consent
Voluntary agreement based on adequate understanding of relevant information and choices.
Beneficence
Ethical obligation to promote benefits and well-being.
Non-maleficence
Ethical obligation to avoid or minimise harm.
Justice
Fair distribution of benefits, burdens, opportunities and access.
Good clinical practice
Ethical and scientific quality standard for designing, conducting, recording and reporting clinical trials.
Regulatory authorisation
Formal permission by a competent authority for a regulated activity or product, where required.
Technology transfer
Process of transferring knowledge, skills, methods or rights to enable use in another setting.
Benefit-sharing
Arrangements to distribute benefits arising from use of genetic resources or associated knowledge, as applicable.
