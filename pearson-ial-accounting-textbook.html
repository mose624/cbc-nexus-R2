Chapter 1: The purpose and users of accounting

1.1 What accounting does

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarising, analysing and communicating financial information. It converts a large number of individual economic events into reports that help people make decisions. Bookkeeping is an important part of this process: it records transactions in an organised way. Accounting goes further by interpreting the records, preparing statements and communicating what the information means.

A business may make hundreds of transactions in a month. A sales invoice, a supplier bill, a wage payment and a bank receipt are individual events. If they are recorded consistently, they can be grouped into meaningful categories such as revenue, cost of sales, wages, cash, receivables and payables. At the end of a reporting period, these records support financial statements.

1.2 Internal and external users

Owners and shareholders want to assess profitability, financial position and the return on their investment. Managers use accounting information to plan operations, control costs, set prices, monitor departments and decide whether to expand. Employees may consider an organisation's stability when assessing job security and pay prospects. Lenders assess the ability of a borrower to repay principal and interest. Suppliers consider whether customers can pay on time. Customers may be interested in continuity of supply, while governments use reported information for taxation, regulation and economic statistics.

Different users ask different questions. A bank considering a five-year loan may focus on cash generation, gearing and security. A manager deciding whether to discontinue a product needs relevant future costs and revenues, not simply last year's total profit. A shareholder may compare return on capital employed with alternative investments. Therefore, useful accounting information must be relevant to the decision being made.

1.3 Qualities of useful information

Relevance means information can influence a decision, often because it has predictive or confirmatory value. Faithful representation means the information depicts the economic substance of transactions completely, neutrally and without material error. Comparability allows users to identify similarities and differences between periods or entities. Verifiability means knowledgeable independent observers can reach reasonable agreement that the information faithfully represents what it claims to represent. Timeliness means information is available early enough to affect a decision. Understandability requires clear classification and presentation without hiding complexity that is important.

These qualities involve trade-offs. A very detailed report may be accurate but arrive too late to help management. A simplified report may be easy to read but omit a material risk. Accounting judgement is needed to balance relevance, faithful representation, cost and clarity.

1.4 Accounting concepts and assumptions

The business entity concept treats the business as separate from its owner for accounting purposes. If an owner takes cash for personal use, the transaction is drawings rather than a business expense. The going concern assumption presumes the entity will continue operating for the foreseeable future unless evidence indicates otherwise. The accrual basis records income when earned and expenses when incurred, rather than only when cash is received or paid. The consistency concept supports use of accounting policies from period to period, while allowing a justified change when it improves reporting. Materiality focuses attention on information whose omission or misstatement could influence users.

Prudence requires caution when making judgements under uncertainty; it does not justify deliberate understatement of assets or profit. Substance over form means the economic reality of a transaction should be considered rather than relying only on its legal label. The historical cost basis records many assets initially at transaction cost, although applicable reporting standards may require other measurement bases in particular circumstances.

Applied example: owner and business are separate

The owner pays a personal electricity bill of £180 from the business bank account. The business has paid cash, but the payment is not a business electricity expense. It is recorded as drawings or an amount due from the owner, according to the entity's accounting system. Treating it as an operating expense would understate business profit.

1.5 Limitations of accounting information

Financial statements are based partly on estimates, such as useful lives of non-current assets and expected credit losses. Historical cost may not equal current market value. Reports summarise past performance and may not predict future outcomes. Inflation can reduce comparability across time if figures are not adjusted. Different accounting policies and estimates can reduce comparability between businesses. Non-financial factors such as staff expertise, customer satisfaction, product quality, environmental effects and reputation may be important but are not fully captured by monetary statements.

Check your understanding
  1. Explain two differences between bookkeeping and accounting.
  2. Identify four external users and explain one decision each might make.
  3. Explain why prudence does not mean intentionally understating profit.
  4. Discuss two limitations of using published financial statements to judge management performance.

Chapter 2: The accounting equation and double entry

2.1 The accounting equation

The resources controlled by a business are its assets. Obligations to other parties are liabilities. The residual interest belonging to the owner is equity or capital. The fundamental relationship is:

Assets = Equity + Liabilities

The equation remains in balance after every correctly recorded transaction. A business starts when its owner invests £12,000 cash. Assets increase by £12,000 and equity increases by £12,000. If it then borrows £5,000, cash and liabilities both increase by £5,000. If it buys equipment for £3,000 cash, one asset increases while another asset decreases; total assets do not change.

2.2 Debit and credit

Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction in at least two accounts, with total debits equal to total credits. Debit means the left side of an account and credit means the right side; neither word inherently means increase or decrease. The effect depends on the type of account.

Account typeIncreaseDecreaseNormal balance
AssetsDebitCreditDebit
ExpensesDebitCreditDebit
DrawingsDebitCreditDebit
LiabilitiesCreditDebitCredit
Capital / equityCreditDebitCredit
Income / revenueCreditDebitCredit

A useful reasoning method is to identify the two accounts affected, decide whether each account increases or decreases, and apply the debit-credit rules. Avoid memorising entries without understanding the underlying change.

2.3 Recording common transactions

When goods are sold for cash, cash increases and sales revenue increases: debit cash, credit sales. When goods are sold on credit, trade receivables increase: debit trade receivables, credit sales. When inventory is bought on credit, purchases or inventory increases and trade payables increase: debit purchases/inventory, credit trade payables. When a customer pays an outstanding invoice, cash increases and receivables decrease: debit bank, credit trade receivables. When a supplier is paid, payables decrease and cash decreases: debit trade payables, credit bank.

In a perpetual inventory system, a sale may require a second entry to recognise the cost of inventory sold: debit cost of sales and credit inventory. In a periodic system, purchases and closing inventory are dealt with through period-end procedures. Follow the method and terminology specified in the question.

Worked example: a sequence of transactions

A new business receives £10,000 capital in cash, buys equipment for £2,400 cash, purchases goods on credit for £1,600, and pays rent of £500.

  1. Dr Bank £10,000; Cr Capital £10,000.
  2. Dr Equipment £2,400; Cr Bank £2,400.
  3. Dr Purchases £1,600; Cr Trade payables £1,600.
  4. Dr Rent expense £500; Cr Bank £500.

Bank balance is £7,100; equipment is £2,400; purchases are £1,600; rent expense is £500; payables are £1,600; capital is £10,000. The equation balances: assets £9,500 = equity £7,900 (capital less loss/expense and assuming no sales) + liabilities £1,600.

2.4 Capital and revenue expenditure

Capital expenditure creates or improves a resource expected to provide benefits over more than one accounting period, such as purchasing a delivery vehicle or installing production machinery. Revenue expenditure is incurred in day-to-day operations, such as routine repairs, fuel and wages. Classification matters because capital expenditure is generally recognised as an asset and charged to profit over time through depreciation, whereas revenue expenditure is normally charged to the period in which it is incurred.

A major improvement that increases capacity or extends useful life may be capital in nature; routine maintenance that keeps an asset operating as intended is usually revenue expenditure. The substance and material facts matter, not merely the size of the invoice.

Practice
  1. State the debit and credit for a credit sale of £750.
  2. Explain why debit does not always mean an increase in every account.
  3. Classify the purchase of a delivery van, routine servicing, and an extension that doubles warehouse capacity.
  4. Prepare double entries for owner capital, cash purchases, credit purchases, customer receipts and supplier payments.

Chapter 3: Books of prime entry and ledger accounts

3.1 Source documents

Source documents provide evidence that a transaction occurred and support accurate recording. Examples include sales invoices, purchase invoices, credit notes, debit notes, receipts, remittance advices, bank statements, petty cash vouchers and till rolls. A good accounting system checks dates, amounts, supplier or customer identity, authorisation, tax treatment and whether the transaction has already been recorded. Sequential numbering and reconciliations help identify missing or duplicated records.

3.2 Books of prime entry

Books of prime entry collect transactions of a similar type before they are posted to ledger accounts. The sales journal records credit sales; the purchases journal records credit purchases; the sales returns journal records credit notes issued to customers; and the purchases returns journal records credit notes received from suppliers. The cash book records receipts and payments through bank and cash. The petty cash book records small cash payments. A general journal records transactions that do not fit conveniently into another specialised book, including opening entries, corrections and some year-end adjustments.

Cash sales are generally recorded through the cash book or sales records rather than the credit sales journal. A credit note reduces the amount owed by a customer or to a supplier. A remittance advice is a document sent with payment information; it is not itself proof that funds have cleared the bank.

3.3 Ledger accounts and balancing

The ledger groups transactions by account. A trade receivables control account summarises amounts owed by credit customers; individual customer accounts provide detail. A trade payables control account summarises amounts owed to credit suppliers. Control accounts help check the completeness and accuracy of the personal ledgers, but a difference must be investigated rather than automatically written off.

At the end of a period, the two sides of a ledger account are totalled. The difference is the balance carried down (c/d) to make the totals equal; it is then brought down (b/d) on the opposite side as the opening balance for the next period. Asset and expense accounts normally carry debit balances, while liability, capital and income accounts normally carry credit balances.

Worked example: trade receivables control

Opening receivables £4,200; credit sales £9,800; cash received £8,100; sales returns £300; irrecoverable debts written off £200. Closing receivables = 4,200 + 9,800 − 8,100 − 300 − 200 = £5,400. The control account should reconcile to the total of individual customer balances.

3.4 Discounts and settlement

A trade discount reduces the list price before the transaction is recorded; accounts are usually recorded at the net invoice amount. A cash discount (settlement discount) is offered to encourage prompt payment. If a customer owes £1,000 and is allowed a 2% settlement discount, the customer pays £980. The seller records discount allowed of £20 and receives £980 cash, clearing the £1,000 receivable. The buyer records discount received.

3.5 Bank and cash book

A bank column in the cash book records money paid into and out of the bank account. The cash book is part of the double-entry system and can be updated for bank charges, direct debits, standing orders and interest identified on the bank statement. A bank reconciliation explains differences between the cash book balance and the bank statement balance, such as unpresented cheques and outstanding lodgements. These timing differences do not necessarily indicate errors.

Practice
  1. Distinguish a sales invoice from a credit note.
  2. Explain the purpose of a control account.
  3. Calculate closing receivables from opening balance £3,600, credit sales £12,400, receipts £10,700, returns £450 and write-offs £150.
  4. Explain two reasons why a bank statement balance may differ from the cash book.

Chapter 4: Trial balance and correction of errors

4.1 Purpose and limitations

A trial balance is a list of ledger account balances at a particular date, arranged into debit and credit columns. If the double entries have been posted and balanced correctly, total debits should equal total credits. This is an arithmetic check, not proof that the records are completely correct. Errors that preserve equality can remain undetected.

4.2 Types of error

An error of omission occurs when a transaction is not recorded at all. An error of commission occurs when the correct type of account is used but the wrong personal account is selected. An error of principle occurs when a transaction is entered in the wrong class of account, such as recording equipment as an ordinary expense. An error of original entry occurs when the wrong amount is entered in both accounts. A reversal of entries occurs when the debit and credit are swapped. A compensating error occurs when two errors offset each other. A transposition error occurs when digits are reversed, for example 63 entered as 36.

Some errors affect the trial balance agreement and others do not. A one-sided posting, or unequal amounts on debit and credit sides, usually causes a difference. Complete omission, posting to the wrong customer account, or a full reversal may leave totals equal.

4.3 Suspense accounts and correction journals

If a trial balance difference is temporarily placed in a suspense account, the account is a holding account until the cause is found. It is not a permanent solution. Each identified error is corrected with a journal entry that records the missing or incorrect debit and credit. After all relevant errors are corrected, the suspense account should close to nil unless an unresolved difference remains.

Worked example: correcting an error of principle

Equipment costing £1,200 was incorrectly debited to repairs expense. The original credit to bank was correct. To correct the classification, debit equipment £1,200 and credit repairs expense £1,200. The correction increases non-current assets and increases profit relative to the incorrect records, before depreciation and any other adjustments.

4.4 Suspense account illustration

A credit purchase of £460 was correctly credited to trade payables but omitted from purchases. The correction is debit purchases £460 and credit suspense £460 if the original trial balance difference had been placed in suspense due to the missing debit. Always identify which side of the trial balance was short before deciding the suspense entry; do not apply a memorised direction without checking the facts.

Practice
  1. Explain why a trial balance can agree when a transaction has been completely omitted.
  2. Classify an error where office equipment is recorded as stationery expense.
  3. Correct a £540 payment to a supplier that was debited correctly to payables but credited to bank as £450.
  4. Explain the role and limitation of a suspense account.

Chapter 5: Accrual accounting and year-end adjustments

5.1 Accruals and prepayments

The accrual basis matches expenses to the period in which the benefit is consumed and recognises income when earned. An accrued expense is an expense incurred but not yet paid or invoiced at the reporting date. A prepayment is cash paid in advance for a future period. Accruals increase the expense for the current period and create a liability; prepayments reduce the current-period expense and create an asset.

Worked example: insurance prepayment

A business pays £1,200 for 12 months of insurance on 1 October. Its year ends on 31 December. Three months relate to the current year: expense = £1,200 × 3/12 = £300. The remaining £900 is a prepayment asset. The adjustment is debit prepayments £900 and credit insurance expense £900 if the full payment was initially recorded as an expense.

Worked example: accrued electricity

Electricity used during the year is estimated at £740 but will be invoiced next month. Record electricity expense £740 and an accrual liability £740. When the invoice is paid later, clear the liability and record any difference in the appropriate period.

5.2 Depreciation

Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount of a tangible non-current asset over its estimated useful life. It is not a valuation exercise and does not normally set the asset's market value. Depreciable amount is cost (or another applicable carrying amount) less residual value. The estimate of useful life, residual value and method should reflect the expected pattern of consumption of economic benefits and be reviewed when required by the applicable framework.

The straight-line method charges an equal amount each full year:

Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life

The reducing-balance method applies a fixed percentage to the carrying amount at the start of each period, resulting in a higher charge in earlier years. The method chosen should be systematic and appropriate to the asset's use.

Worked example: straight-line depreciation

Machine cost £24,000, estimated residual value £4,000 and useful life 5 years. Annual depreciation = (£24,000 − £4,000) ÷ 5 = £4,000. After two full years, accumulated depreciation is £8,000 and carrying amount is £16,000, before any impairment or disposal.

5.3 Irrecoverable debts and allowance for receivables

An irrecoverable debt is written off when there is no reasonable expectation of collection. The receivable is removed and an expense is recognised. An allowance for receivables estimates expected credit losses on the remaining receivables. It is an estimate, not a list of specific debts that have already been written off. If an allowance increases, the increase is normally charged as an expense; if it decreases, the reduction is credited to profit or loss, subject to the relevant reporting requirements.

Worked example: allowance adjustment

Trade receivables are £20,000 after writing off known irrecoverable balances. The required closing allowance is 4% = £800. The existing allowance is £650. Increase required = £150, which is charged as an expense. The statement of financial position presents receivables net of the allowance, subject to the required presentation.

5.4 Inventory valuation

Inventory is generally measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value under common financial reporting frameworks. Cost includes purchase costs and other costs incurred in bringing inventory to its present location and condition. Net realisable value is the estimated selling price less costs to complete and sell. Damaged, obsolete or slow-moving items may need write-downs. FIFO and weighted average are common cost formulas; the selected formula should be applied consistently to inventory of similar nature and use, subject to the applicable standards. LIFO is not permitted under IFRS.

5.5 Capital and revenue adjustments

Year-end adjustments ensure that assets, liabilities, income and expenses are recognised in the correct period. A business must not treat a capital purchase as an expense simply because cash was paid, nor omit depreciation because it is non-cash. Adjustments affect both profit and the statement of financial position. A useful examination habit is to state the effect on profit and the effect on assets or liabilities for every adjustment.

Practice
  1. Calculate a rent accrual when £5,400 was paid during the year and £600 remains unpaid at year end.
  2. Calculate annual straight-line depreciation on an asset costing £18,000 with residual value £3,000 and a five-year life.
  3. Explain the difference between writing off an irrecoverable debt and maintaining an allowance.
  4. Explain why inventory is not always valued at its purchase cost.

Chapter 6: Financial statements for a sole trader

6.1 Income statement structure

An income statement reports financial performance over a period. A typical trading section starts with revenue, deducts sales returns to calculate net revenue, and deducts cost of sales to calculate gross profit. Operating expenses are then deducted to calculate operating profit. Finance costs and tax may be shown separately where relevant. The exact format depends on the reporting framework and the information requested.

Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Net purchases + Direct costs − Closing inventory

Net purchases are purchases less purchase returns, with carriage inwards included where appropriate. Carriage outwards is normally a distribution expense, not part of the cost of bringing inventory to the business.

Worked example: gross profit and operating profit

Revenue £96,000; sales returns £2,000; opening inventory £11,000; purchases £42,000; purchase returns £1,500; carriage inwards £500; closing inventory £9,000; operating expenses £21,000.

Net revenue = £94,000. Net purchases = £40,500. Cost of sales = 11,000 + 40,500 + 500 − 9,000 = £43,000. Gross profit = £51,000. Operating profit = £51,000 − £21,000 = £30,000.

6.2 Statement of financial position

The statement of financial position reports assets, liabilities and equity at a specific date. Non-current assets are held for continuing use. Current assets are expected to be realised, sold or consumed in the normal operating cycle or within the relevant current classification period. Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled in the normal operating cycle or within the relevant period. Equity represents the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets.

A sole trader's closing capital is affected by additional capital introduced, profit or loss and drawings. A simplified relationship is: closing capital = opening capital + additional capital + profit − drawings. If there is a loss, subtract it rather than add it.

6.3 Distinguishing profit from cash

Profit is calculated using accrual accounting; cash flow records receipts and payments. A credit sale increases revenue and receivables before cash is collected. Depreciation reduces profit but does not require a cash payment in the period. Purchasing a non-current asset uses cash but is not normally charged fully as an expense immediately. Loan proceeds increase cash and liabilities but are not revenue. These differences explain why a profitable business can experience cash shortages.

Practice
  1. Prepare a trading account from supplied opening inventory, purchases, returns and closing inventory.
  2. Explain why drawings are not an expense in the income statement.
  3. Explain three reasons why profit and cash generated may differ.
  4. Prepare a closing capital calculation using opening capital, profit, drawings and additional capital.

Chapter 7: Financial statements for companies

7.1 Company ownership and equity

A company is a separate legal entity from its shareholders. Share capital represents amounts contributed by shareholders in exchange for shares. Retained earnings represent accumulated profits not distributed as dividends, adjusted for losses and other relevant movements. A dividend is a distribution to shareholders; it is not an operating expense incurred to generate revenue. A company may also have reserves arising from other transactions or reporting requirements.

7.2 Statement of profit or loss

Company performance reporting distinguishes revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit, finance costs and tax expense where relevant. The presentation should make significant items understandable and comparable. Finance costs arise from financing arrangements and should not be confused with operating expenses such as distribution or administration costs.

7.3 Statement of financial position and financing

Equity and liabilities finance the company's assets. Equity finance includes share capital and retained earnings. Debt finance includes bank loans, debentures and other borrowings. Debt can provide funding without diluting ownership, but interest and repayment obligations increase financial risk. Share issues can strengthen the capital base but may dilute existing shareholders' ownership and control.

7.4 Dividends and retained earnings

Retained earnings increase when profits are retained and decrease when losses or distributions are recognised. A company must consider legal requirements, distributable reserves, liquidity, investment plans and shareholder expectations before declaring dividends. A high dividend may please investors in the short term but reduce funds available for expansion and resilience.

7.5 Ethical and governance considerations

Financial reporting involves judgement and may create incentives to manipulate results. Examples include recognising revenue too early, delaying expense recognition, overstating inventory or using unrealistic depreciation estimates. Ethical reporting requires integrity, objectivity, professional competence, confidentiality and professional behaviour. Effective governance includes oversight, internal controls, independent scrutiny and clear accountability.

Practice
  1. Distinguish share capital from retained earnings.
  2. Explain why dividends are not treated as operating expenses.
  3. Evaluate one advantage and one disadvantage of debt finance compared with issuing shares.
  4. Discuss how weak internal controls can increase the risk of misstated financial statements.

Chapter 8: Cash flow and working capital

8.1 Why cash flow matters

Cash is needed to pay employees, suppliers, taxes, interest and loan instalments. A business can report profit but fail because customers pay late or inventory absorbs too much cash. Cash flow analysis distinguishes operating, investing and financing activities. Operating cash flows arise from principal revenue-producing activities; investing cash flows relate to acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and investments; financing cash flows arise from changes in equity and borrowings.

8.2 Working capital

Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Working capital supports day-to-day operations. Too little can cause payment difficulties and interrupt production. Too much may indicate inefficient use of resources, excessive inventory or slow collection of receivables. The appropriate level depends on the business model, seasonality, supplier terms and reliability of cash inflows.

8.3 Managing the cash conversion cycle

Inventory days estimate how long inventory is held; receivables days estimate how long customers take to pay; payables days estimate how long the business takes to pay suppliers. The cash conversion cycle is often approximated as inventory days plus receivables days less payables days. A shorter cycle can release cash, but excessively tight credit terms or very low inventory may damage customer relationships or cause stock-outs.

Worked example: cash conversion cycle

Inventory days are 50, receivables days are 35 and payables days are 30. Approximate cash conversion cycle = 50 + 35 − 30 = 55 days. The business must finance approximately 55 days between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers, subject to the assumptions behind the ratios.

8.4 Cash budgets

A cash budget forecasts receipts and payments over future periods. It begins with opening cash, adds expected cash receipts and deducts expected cash payments to calculate closing cash. Only cash movements are included. Depreciation is excluded because it is non-cash; credit sales are included when cash is expected to be received, not necessarily when the sale is made. Loan receipts and capital expenditure are included when the cash moves.

Practice
  1. Calculate working capital from current assets of £48,000 and current liabilities of £31,000.
  2. Calculate the cash conversion cycle from inventory days 42, receivables days 28 and payables days 25.
  3. Explain why depreciation is excluded from a cash budget.
  4. Recommend two ways a business could improve cash flow without simply taking out another loan.

Chapter 9: Financial statement analysis and ratios

9.1 Profitability ratios

Ratios turn financial statement figures into relationships that support comparison. Gross profit margin measures gross profit as a proportion of revenue. Operating profit margin measures operating profit relative to revenue. Return on capital employed (ROCE) compares operating profit with capital employed and indicates how effectively long-term finance is used to generate operating returns. Definitions can vary by course or reporting convention, so use the formula supplied or required in the question.

Gross profit margin = Gross profit ÷ Revenue × 100%
Operating profit margin = Operating profit ÷ Revenue × 100%
ROCE = Operating profit ÷ Capital employed × 100%

9.2 Liquidity ratios

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Acid-test ratio = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities

Liquidity ratios assess the ability to meet short-term obligations. A current ratio above 1 does not automatically prove strong liquidity: inventory may be obsolete, receivables may be overdue, or cash may be restricted. A very high ratio can suggest idle resources or poor working-capital management. Industry norms and operating cycles matter.

9.3 Efficiency ratios

Inventory turnover and inventory days indicate how quickly stock is sold or used. Receivables days indicate the average collection period, while payables days indicate the average time taken to pay suppliers. Asset turnover measures revenue generated per unit of capital employed. When calculating days, use average balances where appropriate and ensure numerator and denominator are consistent.

9.4 Gearing and investor ratios

Gearing measures the extent to which a business relies on debt finance. Different formulae exist, such as debt compared with equity or debt compared with total capital. Higher gearing can increase financial risk because interest and repayment commitments remain even when trading conditions deteriorate. Investor measures may include earnings per share, dividend per share, dividend yield and price/earnings ratio. Interpret each in context and avoid treating one ratio as a complete verdict.

9.5 How to evaluate performance

A sound evaluation compares ratios over time, with competitors and against industry conditions. It explains the direction and size of change, identifies possible causes, considers the effects on stakeholders and acknowledges limitations. A margin may fall because input costs rose, prices were reduced, product mix changed or inefficiency increased. The ratio alone does not identify which explanation is correct; further evidence is needed.

Worked example: margin analysis

Revenue is £250,000, cost of sales is £150,000 and operating expenses are £65,000. Gross profit = £100,000; gross profit margin = 100,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = 40%. Operating profit = £35,000; operating profit margin = 35,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = 14%. The difference between the margins reflects operating expenses as well as trading performance.

Practice
  1. Calculate gross profit margin where revenue is £180,000 and gross profit is £63,000.
  2. Calculate the acid-test ratio where current assets are £72,000, inventory £24,000 and current liabilities £32,000.
  3. Explain why a high current ratio may not guarantee liquidity.
  4. Evaluate the usefulness of comparing a company's ROCE with a competitor's ROCE.

Chapter 10: Cost classification and costing methods

10.1 Direct and indirect costs

A direct cost can be traced economically to a particular cost unit or cost centre, such as the material used to make a product or wages paid to a worker directly making it. An indirect cost cannot be traced conveniently to one cost unit and is treated as overhead, such as factory rent or general supervision. A cost unit is the unit of output for which costs are measured; a cost centre is a location, function or person for which costs are collected.

10.2 Fixed, variable and semi-variable costs

Within a relevant range and time period, total fixed cost remains constant as activity changes, although fixed cost per unit falls as output rises. Total variable cost changes with activity, while variable cost per unit is assumed constant under a simple linear model. Semi-variable costs contain fixed and variable elements. Step-fixed costs remain constant over a band of activity and then rise when additional capacity is required.

10.3 Absorption costing

Absorption costing assigns direct costs and a share of production overhead to each unit. A predetermined overhead absorption rate may be calculated using machine hours, labour hours or units, depending on the cost driver. Under- or over-absorption occurs when actual overhead differs from the amount absorbed. The difference must be treated according to the costing method and policy being used.

Worked example: overhead absorption

Budgeted production overhead is £120,000 and budgeted machine hours are 30,000. Overhead absorption rate = £120,000 ÷ 30,000 = £4 per machine hour. A product requiring 2.5 machine hours absorbs £10 of overhead per unit.

10.4 Marginal costing and contribution

Marginal costing separates variable costs from fixed costs. Contribution is sales revenue less variable costs. Contribution first covers fixed costs; any remaining contribution is profit. Contribution per unit is useful for short-term decisions where fixed costs are unchanged and capacity is available. It should not be confused with profit.

Contribution per unit = Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit
Break-even output = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit
Margin of safety = Actual or budgeted output − Break-even output
Worked example: break-even

Selling price £25; variable cost £15; fixed costs £20,000. Contribution per unit = £10. Break-even output = £20,000 ÷ £10 = 2,000 units. At output of 2,600 units, margin of safety is 600 units, or 23.1% of output.

10.5 Limitations of simple break-even analysis

Traditional break-even analysis assumes a constant selling price and variable cost per unit, fixed costs remain constant within the relevant range, all output is sold, and sales mix remains stable. In reality, discounts, inflation, overtime, capacity constraints, waste, changing product mix and demand uncertainty may alter the result. Use break-even as a planning model, not as a guaranteed forecast.

Practice
  1. Classify factory rent, direct materials, sales commission and factory manager salary.
  2. Calculate contribution and break-even output for price £40, variable cost £24 and fixed costs £48,000.
  3. Explain the difference between absorption and marginal costing.
  4. Discuss two limitations of break-even analysis.

Chapter 11: Budgeting and variance analysis

11.1 Purpose of budgets

A budget is a quantified plan for a future period. Budgets coordinate departments, communicate targets, allocate resources, support cash planning and provide a basis for control. A participative budget involves managers who are responsible for delivering it; this can improve ownership and use local knowledge, although it may encourage budgetary slack. A top-down budget can be quicker and align with strategic priorities but may ignore operational realities.

11.2 Functional budgets and the master budget

Functional budgets may include sales, production, materials, labour, overhead, administration, capital expenditure and cash. The sales budget often drives other budgets. Production requirements depend on expected sales and desired closing inventory. Materials purchases depend on production needs and inventory policies. The master budget consolidates functional plans into budgeted financial statements and cash forecasts.

Worked example: production budget

Forecast sales are 8,000 units. Desired closing finished goods inventory is 1,200 units and opening inventory is 900 units. Production required = sales + closing inventory − opening inventory = 8,000 + 1,200 − 900 = 8,300 units.

11.3 Flexible budgets

A fixed budget is prepared for one activity level. A flexible budget adjusts variable costs to the actual level of activity while keeping fixed costs constant within the relevant range. Comparing actual results with a flexible budget helps separate the effect of activity volume from spending performance. Without flexing, a business may wrongly label a higher variable cost as adverse when it simply produced more units.

11.4 Variances

A variance is the difference between a budgeted and actual result. A favourable variance improves profit relative to budget; an adverse variance reduces it. For revenue, actual revenue above budget is normally favourable. For costs, actual cost below budget is normally favourable. Variance analysis should identify causes, not just labels. Price, rate, usage, efficiency, volume and mix can be investigated depending on the data and syllabus approach.

For a simple material cost variance, compare actual material cost with the flexible-budget cost allowed for actual output. A price variance may arise from supplier price changes or bulk discounts. A usage variance may arise from waste, poor-quality inputs, machine problems or inaccurate standards. Responsibility should be assigned carefully because external factors may be outside a manager's control.

11.5 Behavioural and practical limitations

Budgets can motivate but may also encourage short-term behaviour, excessive cost cutting, manipulation of forecasts or conflict between departments. Standards can become outdated when technology, prices or processes change. A useful control system combines financial and non-financial indicators, regular review and constructive investigation rather than punishing every adverse variance.

Practice
  1. Explain three purposes of preparing budgets.
  2. Calculate production required when sales are 12,500 units, desired closing inventory is 1,800 and opening inventory is 1,300.
  3. Explain why a flexible budget is more useful than a fixed budget when actual output differs from plan.
  4. Give possible causes of an adverse material usage variance.

Chapter 12: Decision-making and performance

12.1 Relevant costs and revenues

Relevant costs are future cash flows that differ between decision alternatives. Sunk costs have already been incurred and cannot be changed by the decision, so they are normally irrelevant. Committed costs may be unavoidable in the short term. Opportunity cost is the benefit sacrificed by choosing one alternative instead of the next best alternative. Avoidable fixed costs may be relevant if they can genuinely be eliminated; allocated overheads are not automatically relevant simply because they appear in a report.

Worked example: special order

A business has spare capacity. A customer offers £18 per unit for 1,000 units. Variable cost is £12 per unit and additional packaging costs £1 per unit. No extra fixed cost is incurred and normal sales are unaffected. Incremental contribution = (£18 − £12 − £1) × 1,000 = £5,000. On the stated assumptions, the order increases profit by £5,000. If capacity were full, the opportunity cost of displaced sales would need to be included.

12.2 Make or buy

A make-or-buy decision compares relevant internal production costs with the supplier's purchase price and any additional costs. Consider quality, reliability, confidentiality, delivery times, supplier dependency, workforce effects and the use of released capacity. A supplier quote below the full absorption cost does not automatically make buying preferable if much of the internal fixed overhead will remain.

12.3 Limiting factors

A limiting factor is a scarce resource that restricts output, such as skilled labour hours, machine time or raw material. Where one limiting factor exists and products are otherwise comparable, rank products by contribution per unit of the scarce resource. Allocate the resource in descending order until demand or capacity is met. If there are multiple constraints, a more advanced optimisation method may be needed.

Worked example: scarce machine hours

Product A earns contribution £30 and uses 3 machine hours: £10 contribution per machine hour. Product B earns £24 and uses 2 machine hours: £12 per machine hour. If machine hours are the only constraint, prioritise B first, subject to demand and other constraints.

12.4 Non-financial considerations and balanced performance

Decisions should not be based only on short-term financial calculations. Quality, customer loyalty, staff morale, environmental impact, legal compliance, brand reputation and strategic fit can change the long-term outcome. A balanced performance framework may combine financial measures with customer, internal-process, learning and growth indicators. Measures should be linked to strategy and should not reward behaviour that undermines other objectives.

12.5 Professional judgement

Accounting information supports decisions but does not replace judgement. State assumptions, identify uncertainty, distinguish relevant from irrelevant data and test how sensitive the result is to changes in price, volume and cost. A recommendation should be conditional where important facts are missing. For example, accept a special order only if spare capacity exists, quality can be maintained, the order does not damage normal pricing and incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost plus any opportunity cost.

Practice
  1. Explain why sunk costs are normally excluded from a make-or-buy decision.
  2. Calculate contribution per limiting machine hour for two products and rank them.
  3. Evaluate a special order using both financial and non-financial factors.
  4. Explain how a balanced set of performance indicators can improve management decisions.

Integrated revision and examination practice

How to approach calculation questions

  1. Read the requirement first and identify the output requested: journal, ledger, statement, ratio, budget or recommendation.
  2. Underline dates and reporting periods. Many errors arise from applying a full-year amount when only part of the year belongs to the period.
  3. Extract relevant figures and label each one. Separate revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, cash flows and non-financial information.
  4. Show workings in a logical sequence. Use brackets and units; round only at the final stage unless instructed otherwise.
  5. Check reasonableness. A gross profit greater than revenue, a negative asset balance without explanation or an impossible ratio may indicate an error.
  6. For written evaluation, make a point, explain its effect, apply it to the figures or scenario, and reach a justified conclusion.

Question 1 — Double entry (8 marks)

On 1 March, a sole trader introduced £15,000 cash. During the month the business bought equipment for £3,600 cash, purchased goods on credit for £2,400, sold goods on credit for £4,100 and paid rent of £700. Prepare the journal entries and identify the accounts affected.

Question 2 — Adjustments (10 marks)

A business paid insurance of £2,400 on 1 July for 12 months. Its year ends on 31 December. It also owes wages of £950 at year end. Calculate the insurance expense and prepayment, and state the wages adjustment.

Revenue is £320,000; sales returns £5,000; opening inventory £28,000; purchases £142,000; purchase returns £4,000; carriage inwards £2,000; closing inventory £31,000; operating expenses £76,000. Calculate net revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating profit and gross profit margin.

Question 4 — Ratio interpretation (10 marks)

A company's current ratio has fallen from 2.1:1 to 1.3:1 while its acid-test ratio has fallen from 1.2:1 to 0.7:1. Explain what these changes may indicate, identify two limitations of the ratios and recommend further information that should be examined.

Question 5 — Costing and decision-making (10 marks)

A product sells for £52. Variable cost is £31 per unit and annual fixed costs are £84,000. Calculate contribution per unit and break-even output. The business is offered a one-off order of 900 units at £39 each. Additional variable packaging is £2 per unit. There is spare capacity and no extra fixed cost. Evaluate the order on the stated facts and identify two non-financial considerations.

Q1: Dr Cash/Bank £15,000, Cr Capital £15,000; Dr Equipment £3,600, Cr Cash/Bank £3,600; Dr Purchases £2,400, Cr Trade payables £2,400; Dr Trade receivables £4,100, Cr Sales £4,100; Dr Rent expense £700, Cr Cash/Bank £700. Award method marks for identifying both accounts and correct debit-credit direction.

Q2: Six months of insurance have expired: £2,400 × 6/12 = £1,200 expense. Prepayment = £1,200. Wages expense must include the £950 accrued amount, with a corresponding current liability. Award marks for period allocation and correct asset/liability treatment.

Q3: Net revenue = £315,000. Net purchases = £138,000. Cost of sales = £28,000 + £138,000 + £2,000 − £31,000 = £137,000. Gross profit = £178,000. Operating profit = £102,000. Gross profit margin = £178,000 ÷ £315,000 × 100 = approximately 56.5%. Show the sequence and formula for method credit.

Q4: The lower current ratio suggests reduced current-asset cover of current liabilities. The acid-test fall indicates weaker immediate liquidity after excluding inventory. Q5: Contribution per unit = £52 − £31 = £21. Break-even output = £84,000 ÷ £21 = 4,000 units. Special-order incremental contribution = (£39 − £31 − £2) × 900 = £5,400. Marking principles for extended responses

High-quality responses use accurate accounting terminology, apply concepts to the scenario, show calculations clearly, explain the effect on profit and financial position, and distinguish evidence from assumption. For evaluation, develop both sides of an argument, consider stakeholder consequences and finish with a conclusion that follows from the information. A list of unexplained points is weaker than a smaller number of developed points.

Glossary of essential terms

Accrual: an expense incurred or income earned but not yet settled in cash. Asset: a present economic resource controlled by an entity. Capital expenditure: expenditure recognised as an asset because it provides future economic benefits. Contribution: revenue less variable costs. Depreciation: systematic allocation of an asset's depreciable amount over its useful life. Drawings: resources withdrawn by an owner for personal use. Equity: residual interest in assets after deducting liabilities. Going concern: assumption that the entity will continue operating for the foreseeable future. Liability: a present obligation to transfer an economic resource. Prepayment: payment made before the related expense is recognised. Receivable: amount owed to the entity. Retained earnings: accumulated profits retained in the company after distributions and other relevant movements. Trial balance: list of ledger balances used to check debit-credit arithmetic equality.

Chapter 13: Partnership accounts and changes in ownership

13.1 Partnership agreements and capital accounts

A partnership is an arrangement in which two or more people carry on a business and share its results. Partners may contribute different amounts of capital, perform different roles and accept different levels of risk. A written partnership agreement should explain the profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital and drawings, partner salaries, decision-making powers, admission and retirement procedures, dispute resolution and dissolution. Where the agreement is silent, local partnership law may provide default rules. Accounting records must reflect the agreement actually in force.

Under a fixed capital system, each partner has a capital account for long-term capital introduced or withdrawn and a current account for recurring appropriations and personal transactions. The current account may record salary, interest on capital, share of profit, drawings and interest on drawings. Under a fluctuating capital system, these movements are recorded in one capital account. The method should be clearly identified and consistently applied. A debit current-account balance may indicate that drawings and charges exceed the partner's credits; it is not automatically a cash debt due immediately without considering the agreement.

13.2 Profit appropriation

The income statement first establishes the partnership's profit for the period. The appropriation account then allocates profit according to the partnership agreement. Partner salaries and interest on capital are usually appropriations between owners rather than operating expenses when they represent distributions of profit. Interest on drawings is credited to the appropriation account because it compensates the partnership for the use of its resources. The residual profit is divided using the agreed ratio. Students should distinguish an appropriation from an expense incurred to earn revenue.

Worked example: sharing residual profit

Partners A and B share residual profit 3:2. Net profit is £60,000. A receives an agreed salary of £8,000 and B £6,000. Interest on capital is £2,000 for A and £1,000 for B. Interest on drawings is £500 for A and £300 for B. Net appropriations before residual profit are £16,200 (£8,000 + £6,000 + £2,000 + £1,000 − £500 − £300). Residual profit is £43,800. A receives £26,280 and B £17,520. Their net allocations are £35,780 and £24,520, totalling £60,300 because interest on drawings adds £800 to partnership income. A properly balanced appropriation account shows that total credits, including interest on drawings, reconcile with the profit available for allocation.

13.3 Admission, retirement and dissolution

When a new partner joins, the old partners may surrender part of their profit share. Calculate the new ratio from the agreement and then identify each partner's gain or sacrifice. Goodwill may arise from reputation, customer relationships, location, trained staff or other advantages not fully represented by identifiable net assets. The treatment depends on the accounting framework and agreement. When a partner retires, settlement may include capital and current balances, profit to the retirement date, revaluation gains or losses, goodwill adjustments and agreed payments. Immediate settlement can create a cash shortage, so the remaining partners may need new capital or finance.

On dissolution, assets are realised, liabilities are settled and any surplus is distributed after considering partners' balances and the required settlement order. A revaluation account may be used to record changes in asset and liability values before a change in ownership. A revaluation gain is not cash received: an increase in an asset's value does not itself provide funds to pay a retiring partner. Keep calculations separate for asset realisation, liability settlement, partner loans and capital balances.

  2. Distinguish fixed and fluctuating capital accounts.
  3. Explain how a new profit-sharing ratio is calculated when a partner is admitted.
  4. Discuss two liquidity risks arising when a partner retires.

Chapter 14: Company equity, share capital and reserves

14.1 Company ownership and sources of finance

A company is a separate legal entity from its shareholders. Shareholders provide equity finance and may receive dividends if a distribution is declared and legally permitted. A company may also finance operations through retained earnings, bank loans, bonds or other instruments. Equity is not the same as cash: an issue of shares may increase cash, but subsequent expenditure may convert cash into inventory, equipment or operating costs. The statement of financial position reports the remaining assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date.

Ordinary shares commonly carry voting rights and a residual claim on distributions, although the rights depend on the share terms and company law. Preference shares may have priority for dividends or capital repayment. Classification of a financial instrument as equity or a liability depends on its contractual substance and the applicable reporting framework, not simply the name assigned by management.

14.2 Share issues and share premium

If shares are issued above nominal value, the excess may be recorded in a share premium or equivalent reserve, subject to law and the applicable standards. For example, 50,000 shares with a nominal value of £1 issued at £1.40 generate proceeds of £70,000: share capital is £50,000 and share premium is £20,000 before any required treatment of issue costs. Nominal value is not necessarily the market value. Students should show the number of shares, nominal value, issue price and total proceeds separately.

14.3 Retained earnings, dividends and reserves

Retained earnings represent accumulated profits retained after distributions and other relevant movements. A profitable year may increase retained earnings, but the increase will not necessarily equal cash generated. Profit includes accruals, depreciation and estimates. A dividend is a distribution to owners, not an operating expense. Distinguish the proposal of a dividend from a valid declaration that creates an obligation under the relevant legal and reporting framework. Reserves may arise from retained profits, revaluation, share premium or other transactions. They are components of equity, not necessarily separate bank accounts, and some may be restricted by law.

Worked example: statement of changes in equity

Opening share capital is £100,000 and retained earnings £42,000. During the year, shares of £20,000 nominal value are issued at par, profit is £18,000 and dividends recognised are £5,000. Closing share capital is £120,000. Closing retained earnings are £42,000 + £18,000 − £5,000 = £55,000. Closing total equity is £175,000, assuming no other movements. The statement explains how opening balances became closing balances.

14.4 Governance, stewardship and audit

Directors are responsible for stewardship, oversight and reporting within the legal framework. Governance may include an independent board, audit committee, internal controls, risk management and external audit. An external auditor provides an independent opinion on whether financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, under the applicable framework. An audit does not guarantee future profitability or prove that every fraud has been detected. Users should understand materiality, sampling, estimates and the limits of assurance.

Practice
  1. Calculate share capital and share premium for 80,000 £1 shares issued at £1.25.
  2. Explain why retained earnings are not equivalent to cash.
  3. Distinguish ordinary shares from preference shares.
  4. Evaluate how audit committees and internal controls support accountability.

Chapter 15: Inventory valuation, depreciation and impairment

15.1 Inventory measurement

Inventory is generally measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value under international inventory principles. Cost may include purchase costs, conversion costs and other costs incurred to bring goods to their present location and condition. Net realisable value is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less estimated completion costs and costs necessary to make the sale. Damaged, obsolete or slow-moving goods may require a write-down. Management should support estimates with evidence such as recent selling prices, condition reports and post-year-end sales.

Common cost formulas include first-in, first-out (FIFO) and weighted average, where permitted by the applicable standard. FIFO allocates the earliest purchase costs to units sold first; weighted average calculates an average cost per unit. The physical movement of goods does not have to match the cost formula. A formula should be appropriate to the nature and use of inventory and applied consistently to similar items.

Worked example: weighted average and write-down

A retailer has 100 units at £8 and buys 150 at £10. Total cost is £2,300 for 250 units. Weighted average cost is £9.20. If 180 units are sold, 70 remain at £644. If net realisable value is £8.70 per unit, closing inventory is reduced to £609 and the £35 write-down is recognised in the appropriate expense or cost-of-sales line. Explain both the calculation and the reason for the lower measurement.

15.2 Depreciation and disposal

Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount of a tangible non-current asset over its useful life. The depreciable amount is cost or another appropriate measurement less residual value. Straight-line depreciation produces an equal annual charge when assumptions are unchanged. Reducing-balance depreciation applies a constant percentage to the carrying amount, producing a declining charge. The method should reflect the pattern in which future economic benefits are consumed, not be chosen merely to achieve a preferred profit figure.

On disposal, update depreciation to the disposal date if required, remove the asset's cost and accumulated depreciation, recognise proceeds and compare proceeds with carrying amount to calculate a gain or loss. A disposal gain is not ordinary sales revenue. Show cost, accumulated depreciation, carrying amount and proceeds separately to make the calculation auditable.

15.3 Impairment, receivables and provisions

An asset may be impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount under the relevant framework. Indicators include physical damage, obsolescence, adverse market changes or plans to discontinue an operation. For receivables, expected credit loss approaches may require an allowance based on historical experience, current conditions and reasonable forward-looking information. A balance should not be treated as fully recoverable merely because it remains legally due.

A provision is recognised when the applicable criteria are met, commonly including a present obligation from a past event, a probable outflow and a reliable estimate. Certain warranty obligations and legal claims may qualify. A possible obligation that does not meet recognition criteria may require contingent-liability disclosure. A provision is not a general reserve for future operating losses. Document the evidence, assumptions and range of possible outcomes.

Practice
  1. Explain the lower-of-cost-and-net-realisable-value principle.
  2. Compare FIFO and weighted average when purchase prices are rising.
  3. Calculate annual straight-line depreciation on equipment costing £48,000 with residual value £3,000 and a five-year useful life.
  4. Explain why impairment information matters to lenders and investors.
  5. Distinguish a provision from a contingent liability.

Chapter 16: Cash-flow statements and cash management

16.1 Why profit differs from cash

Accrual accounting recognises income when earned and expenses when incurred, while a cash-flow statement records cash receipts and payments. A credit sale increases revenue and receivables before cash is collected. Depreciation reduces profit but is not a cash payment in the period. Purchasing equipment uses cash but is normally capitalised rather than charged fully as an operating expense immediately. These differences explain why a profitable business can experience a cash crisis and why a temporary cash surplus does not necessarily indicate sustainable profitability.

16.2 Operating, investing and financing activities

Operating activities relate to principal revenue-producing activities and other activities that are not investing or financing. Investing activities include acquiring and disposing of long-term assets and investments. Financing activities change the size and composition of contributed equity and borrowings. Classification details depend on the reporting framework and the nature of the entity. Operating cash flow helps assess whether core operations generate cash; investing outflows may indicate expansion rather than weakness; financing inflows may support growth but increase future repayment or dividend expectations.

16.3 Indirect-method adjustments

The indirect method begins with a profit measure and adjusts for non-cash items, items classified elsewhere and working-capital movements. Depreciation is added back because it reduced accounting profit without using cash in the period. A gain on disposal is deducted from operating profit in the reconciliation when disposal proceeds are shown in investing activities. An increase in inventory generally uses cash. An increase in receivables generally means revenue has been recognised before collection and reduces operating cash relative to profit. An increase in payables generally delays cash payment and increases operating cash relative to profit.

Worked example: working-capital movements

Profit before working-capital changes is £52,000. Inventory increases by £4,500, receivables decrease by £3,000 and payables increase by £1,700. Net adjustment is −£4,500 + £3,000 + £1,700 = £200. Operating cash flow before any further interest and tax adjustments is £52,200. Always follow the question's specified starting profit and reporting framework.

16.4 Cash budgets and forecasting

A cash budget forecasts receipts and payments by period. It should include opening cash, customer collections, cash sales, supplier payments, wages, tax, capital expenditure, loan repayments and other material items. Credit sales should be included in the period when cash is expected, not automatically when the sale is recorded. The closing balance becomes the next period's opening balance. A cash budget differs from a profit budget because timing is central to cash planning.

To respond to a forecast deficit, a business might accelerate collection, review credit checks, reduce slow-moving inventory, negotiate supplier terms, postpone non-essential capital expenditure or arrange committed finance. Every action has trade-offs: aggressive collection may damage customer relationships, excessive stock reduction may cause stock-outs, and delayed supplier payment may harm reliability. Prepare downside scenarios, including late payment by a major customer, cost inflation or a fall in demand.

16.5 Cash conversion cycle

The cash conversion cycle estimates the time between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers. A common approximation is inventory days plus receivables days less payables days. A long cycle means funds are tied up for longer. However, the shortest possible cycle is not always optimal: a manufacturer may need safety stock, and generous credit may support valuable customer relationships. Compare trends, industry practice and operational risks before recommending changes.

Practice

  2. Classify machinery purchases, share issues and customer receipts.
  3. Explain the cash-flow effect of increases in inventory, receivables and payables.
  4. Prepare a three-month cash forecast using opening cash, credit terms and payment dates.
  5. Evaluate two responses to a forecast cash deficit.

Chapter 17: Accounting information systems, ethics and sustainability

17.1 Accounting information systems and audit trails

An accounting information system captures, processes, stores and reports financial transactions. It may include sales, purchases, payroll, inventory, general ledger and bank interfaces. A reliable system produces accurate, timely, relevant and understandable information and protects it from unauthorised access. Source documents such as invoices, credit notes, receipts, bank statements and approved purchase orders provide evidence. Transactions should be coded to the correct accounts and accounting periods. An audit trail allows a reviewer to trace a reported figure back to the original transaction, supporting document and approval.

Reconciliations compare independent records, such as the cash book and bank statement. They help identify omissions, duplicate entries, timing differences and unusual transactions. Exception reports can flag unusual values, repeated supplier invoices or transactions outside normal approval limits. Automation can reduce repetitive data entry, but it cannot replace review, control design and professional judgement.

17.2 Internal controls and risk

Internal controls safeguard assets, support reliable reporting, promote operational effectiveness and encourage compliance. Examples include segregation of duties, authorisation limits, restricted system access, sequential document numbering, physical safeguards, independent reconciliations and periodic stock counts. Where a small organisation cannot fully separate duties, compensating controls may include owner review of bank statements, independent payment approval and external bookkeeping checks.

Controls should be proportionate to risk. Too few controls increase the possibility of fraud and error; unnecessarily complex controls can delay work and encourage staff to bypass procedures. Management should identify vulnerable assets and processes, assess likelihood and impact, design controls and monitor whether they operate. A control is useful only when it is implemented, understood and reviewed.

17.3 Ethics and professional behaviour

Ethical accounting requires integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. Pressure to manipulate estimates, defer expenses, recognise revenue prematurely or conceal liabilities can undermine trust. An accountant should challenge unsupported assumptions, retain evidence, follow professional guidance and use appropriate escalation channels. Confidentiality does not mean concealing wrongdoing where disclosure is required by law or professional obligations.

Ethical threats may arise from self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity or intimidation. Safeguards can include independent review, disclosure of conflicts, rotation of responsibilities, consultation with a senior professional and refusal to participate in misleading reporting. The appropriate response depends on the facts, law and professional code. A strong examination answer identifies the threat, explains who may be harmed and recommends a practical safeguard.

17.4 Sustainability and integrated decision-making

Businesses increasingly report environmental, social and governance information. Relevant measures may include energy use, emissions, waste, water consumption, workforce safety, supply-chain conditions and community impact. Sustainability risks can affect future cash flows through regulation, resource costs, insurance, customer demand, access to finance and reputation. Reporting should use clear boundaries, consistent methods and evidence. Unsupported environmental claims create greenwashing risk.

Financial and non-financial measures should be considered together. A low-cost supplier may increase the risk of labour abuses or unreliable delivery. An energy-efficient process may require capital expenditure now but reduce future operating costs and emissions. Management should identify short- and long-term effects, assess uncertainty and explain trade-offs. Material sustainability matters may influence investors, employees, customers, lenders and regulators.

17.5 Cybersecurity and emerging technology

Cloud accounting, automated invoice processing, data analytics and machine learning can improve speed and visibility. Risks include inaccurate source data, duplicate transactions, inappropriate access, outages, cyberattacks, supplier dependence and opaque automated decisions. Good governance includes multi-factor authentication, least-privilege access, encryption, secure backups, change management, supplier due diligence and tested recovery plans. Sensitive payroll, banking and customer data should be handled lawfully and only for legitimate purposes.

Automation should be tested against realistic business scenarios. Reconcile system outputs to source records, investigate exceptions and retain human approval for high-risk transactions. A model that classifies transactions correctly most of the time can still create material errors in unusual cases. Digital literacy must complement core knowledge of double entry, reporting standards and ethics.

Practice
  1. Describe an audit trail and explain why it matters.
  2. Recommend controls where one employee receives cash, records sales and prepares bank reconciliations.
  3. Explain two ethical threats created by pressure to overstate profit.
  4. Evaluate how sustainability risks may affect financial performance.
  5. Discuss benefits and risks of automated accounting systems.

Applied case study: Northstar Components Ltd

Business background and diagnosis

Northstar Components Ltd manufactures replacement parts for industrial equipment. It sells to domestic and overseas customers, offers 30-day credit to established buyers and holds raw materials to reduce production delays. During the year, sales increased by 14%, but operating cash flow fell and the overdraft rose. The finance director believes higher sales demonstrate strong performance. The operations manager reports increased waste, customer returns and slower collection of receivables. The board requests a balanced analysis.

Separate profitability, liquidity, efficiency and risk. Higher sales do not prove higher profit: gross margin may have fallen because input costs rose or selling prices were discounted. Higher profit does not prove stronger cash flow: receivables may have grown faster than sales, inventory may have accumulated or suppliers may have shortened credit terms. Customer returns may indicate quality problems affecting revenue, warranty costs and reputation. Compare several periods and investigate the timing of changes.

Ratio analysis

Assume revenue increased from £2.0 million to £2.28 million, while gross profit increased from £620,000 to £638,400. Gross profit margin changed from 31% (£620,000 ÷ £2,000,000) to 28% (£638,400 ÷ £2,280,000). Gross profit rose in absolute terms, but margin declined by three percentage points. Possible explanations include supplier inflation, production waste, product mix changes, discounting or inventory write-downs. The ratio alone cannot identify the cause.

Assume receivables increased from £220,000 to £360,000 and credit sales are approximately £2.1 million. Closing receivables days are £360,000 ÷ £2,100,000 × 365 = approximately 62.6 days. If the prior-year estimate was 38 days, collection has slowed. This estimate uses closing receivables; average balances and actual credit sales would improve reliability. The finance team should examine aged debt, disputed invoices, customer credit limits and collection procedures.

Inventory and cash conversion

Suppose inventory rises from £260,000 to £410,000 while cost of sales is £1.64 million. Closing inventory days are approximately £410,000 ÷ £1,640,000 × 365 = 91.2 days. This may reflect deliberate protection against supply delays, but it may also indicate slow-moving goods, inaccurate forecasts or poor production planning. Analyse inventory by product code and age, compare quantities with confirmed orders and review damaged or obsolete goods.

If inventory days are 91, receivables days 63 and payables days 45, the approximate cash conversion cycle is 91 + 63 − 45 = 109 days. Funds are committed for a long period before cash is recovered. Reducing the cycle may release cash, but management should avoid harming service levels or supplier relationships. Possible actions include demand-based purchasing, tighter credit approval, targeted collection, and sustainable supplier-term negotiations.

Recommendations and performance measures

Recommendations need owners, measurable targets and review dates. The credit controller could review overdue balances weekly and contact customers before invoices fall due. The operations manager could investigate scrap rates by production line and introduce quality checks where defects arise. Purchasing could classify inventory by value, usage and lead time, then set evidence-based reorder levels. The finance director could prepare a rolling 13-week cash forecast and stress-test a major customer's payment arriving 30 days late.

Targets should not encourage harmful behaviour. Reducing receivables days should be balanced against customer retention and bad-debt risk. Reducing inventory should be balanced against production continuity. Improving gross margin should not undermine quality or competitiveness. Monitor leading indicators such as overdue orders and defect rates alongside lagging indicators such as gross margin and operating cash flow.

Case-study questions and indicative marking guidance

  1. Calculate the change in gross profit margin and explain two possible causes.
  2. Calculate closing receivables days and explain one limitation.
  3. Calculate the cash conversion cycle.
  4. Recommend three actions to improve cash flow without unnecessarily damaging customer relationships.
  5. Evaluate the claim that higher sales demonstrate strong performance.

Strong answers calculate accurately, show formulas and interpret movements rather than merely restating them. For gross margin, identify 31%, 28% and a three-percentage-point fall. For receivables days, calculate approximately 63 days and recognise the limitation of using closing rather than average balances. For the cash conversion cycle, show 91 + 63 − 45 = 109 days. Recommendations should connect to the case: aged-debt follow-up, credit control, inventory analysis, supplier negotiation, waste reduction and cash forecasting. Evaluation should recognise that sales growth may be positive but is insufficient evidence without margin, cash, quality and customer information.

Independent study, revision and accounting research skills

Connect topics rather than memorising isolated rules

Double entry creates the ledger balances used in the trial balance. Adjustments convert incomplete cash records into accrual-based results. Financial statements summarise performance and position. Ratios interpret those statements, while cash-flow analysis explains why profit and cash may diverge. Costing and budgeting support internal decisions, and ethical controls protect information reliability. When revising a topic, identify the earlier concept on which it depends and the later decision it informs.

Calculation technique

Write the formula, substitute figures, show arithmetic and state the unit or interpretation. A margin is a percentage of revenue; a liquidity ratio is a relationship such as 1.5:1; a break-even point is an output quantity or sales value, not a profit figure. Label each amount as a closing balance, average balance, annual amount or monthly amount. Keep workings in a logical order and round only at the final stage unless instructed otherwise. Check whether the answer is plausible and whether the sign of each adjustment is correct.

Evaluate evidence and assumptions

Financial statements depend on estimates, policies and reporting dates. Ratios may be affected by seasonality, accounting-policy differences, inflation, one-off transactions and industry characteristics. Comparisons are most meaningful when entities have similar activities and comparable accounting treatments. A single ratio is not a definitive judgement. Combine trend analysis, common-size statements, cash-flow information, operational measures and qualitative evidence. State where information is missing and explain how that limits the conclusion.

Revision cycle and examination command words

  1. Read the chapter objectives and explain the main ideas from memory.
  2. Rework each worked example without looking at the solution.
  3. Complete short questions under timed conditions and check formulas and debit-credit directions.
  4. Attempt an integrated case linking at least three topics.
  5. Record errors as knowledge, method, interpretation or time-management errors and redo the question several days later.
  6. Use the current Pearson specification and assessment materials to confirm coverage for the examination series.

Define requires a precise meaning. State normally requires a brief fact. Calculate requires workings and a final answer. Explain requires a reason and its effect. Analyse requires developed links between evidence and consequences. Discuss usually requires more than one perspective. Evaluate requires weighing evidence, recognising limitations and reaching a justified conclusion. Check the current specification and mark scheme for the precise expectations.

Academic integrity and use of company information

When using published financial statements, identify the company, reporting period, reporting framework and source. Distinguish audited figures from management commentary, alternative performance measures and forecasts. Cite the source of data and explain calculations performed. Do not present generated examples as verified company facts or copy another learner's work. Where data is missing, state the limitation rather than inventing a figure. Use the official specification to guide examination preparation and the relevant accounting standards to understand reporting treatments.
