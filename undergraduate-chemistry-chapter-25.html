Key equations and fully worked calculations

Photon energy: E = hc/λ. Beer–Lambert: A = εlc. Quantum yield: Φ = number of specified events / photons absorbed.

Single-state lifetime: τ = 1/(kr + knr), where radiative and non-radiative first-order decay pathways are included.

Worked calculation A: wavelength to photon energy

At 400 nm, E = hc/λ = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J photon⁻¹ = 3.10 eV photon⁻¹. The molar photon energy is 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.022 × 10²³ = 299 kJ mol⁻¹.

Worked calculation B: fluorescence lifetime

If kr = 2.0 × 10⁷ s⁻¹ and knr = 8.0 × 10⁷ s⁻¹, τ = 1/(1.0 × 10⁸) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ s = 10 ns.

For this simple model, ΦF = kr/(kr + knr) = 0.20. Increasing non-radiative decay shortens lifetime and lowers fluorescence yield.

Worked calculation C: quantum yield

If 350 molecules react after 1000 photons are absorbed, Φreaction = 350/1000 = 0.350. State the reaction monitored and ensure the denominator is absorbed, not merely incident, photons.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Photochemistry: excited-state kinetics and energy transfer

1. Einstein relation and absorption

A photon carries E=hν=hc/λ. Absorption occurs when the photon energy matches an allowed energy difference and the transition dipole is non-zero. Beer–Lambert law A=εlc applies to a homogeneous, non-scattering sample within the instrument's linear range. Absorbed photon flux, not merely incident power, is needed for quantum-yield calculations.

2. Excited-state rate equation

For an excited state X* that decays by radiative and non-radiative first-order pathways, d[X*]/dt=−(kr+knr)[X*]. Integration gives [X*]t=[X*]0e^(−t/τ), where τ=1/(kr+knr). The fluorescence quantum yield is ΦF=kr/(kr+knr) for this simple model. Additional quenching or energy-transfer pathways add rate constants to the denominator.

3. Stern–Volmer quenching

For dynamic collisional quenching, F0/F=τ0/τ=1+KSV[Q], where KSV=kqτ0. A linear plot is consistent with a simple dynamic model. Static complex formation, heterogeneous environments, inner-filter effects and combined quenching can produce deviations; lifetime measurements help distinguish mechanisms.

4. Energy transfer and photoredox cycles

Triplet energy transfer is favourable when donor excited-state energy exceeds the acceptor triplet energy and orbital/spin constraints permit transfer. In photoredox catalysis, an excited photocatalyst can act as a stronger oxidant or reductant than its ground state. Construct a catalytic cycle by tracking oxidation state, electron balance and catalyst regeneration at every step.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Photon energy and molar energy

Problem: Calculate energy for 450 nm light in J photon⁻¹ and kJ mol⁻¹.

Solution: E=hc/λ=4.42×10⁻¹⁹ J photon⁻¹. Multiply by NA: 266 kJ mol⁻¹. This is blue visible light.

Worked numerical problem 2: Beer–Lambert concentration

Problem: A=0.750, ε=2.50×10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and l=1.00 cm. Find c.

Solution: c=A/(εl)=0.750/(2.50×10⁴)=3.00×10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹.

Worked numerical problem 3: Excited-state lifetime and yield

Problem: kr=4.0×10⁷ s⁻¹ and knr=6.0×10⁷ s⁻¹. Find τ and ΦF.

Solution: τ=1/(1.0×10⁸)=10 ns. ΦF=kr/(kr+knr)=0.40. Forty percent of excited molecules decay by fluorescence in this two-pathway model.

Worked numerical problem 4: Stern–Volmer quenching

Problem: KSV=12.0 L mol⁻¹ and quencher concentration 0.050 M. Find F0/F.

Solution: F0/F=1+KSV[Q]=1+12.0×0.050=1.60. Therefore F/F0=0.625, assuming simple dynamic quenching.

Worked numerical problem 5: Photochemical quantum yield

Problem: A sample absorbs 2.0×10¹⁸ photons and forms 3.0×10¹⁷ molecules of product. Find Φ.

Solution: Φ=3.0×10¹⁷/2.0×10¹⁸=0.150 molecule per absorbed photon. State whether chain amplification or multiple products per photon are possible.

Worked numerical problem 6: Energy-transfer feasibility

Problem: A donor triplet lies at 1.80 eV and an acceptor triplet at 1.45 eV. Is triplet energy transfer energetically plausible?

Solution: The donor has 0.35 eV more triplet energy, so transfer is energetically downhill by 0.35 eV. Spectral overlap, distance, coupling and spin constraints still determine the rate.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Distinguish fluorescence from phosphorescence.

Answer: Fluorescence commonly occurs from an excited singlet to a singlet ground state and is prompt. Phosphorescence commonly occurs from a triplet to singlet ground state and is slower because it is spin-forbidden.

Model answer 2

Question: What is the Stokes shift?

Answer: It is the separation between absorption and emission maxima, commonly caused by vibrational relaxation and solvent or structural reorganisation before emission.

Model answer 3

Question: Why must absorbed photon flux be used in quantum-yield calculations?

Answer: Photons that are not absorbed cannot initiate the measured photochemical event. Incident flux overestimates the number of photons available to the molecule.

Model answer 4

Question: How can dynamic and static quenching be distinguished?

Answer: Dynamic quenching reduces excited-state lifetime; pure static quenching reduces emitting population but does not necessarily change the lifetime of the remaining emissive molecules.

Model answer 5

Question: Explain why oxygen often quenches triplet states.

Answer: Molecular oxygen has a triplet ground state and can efficiently accept energy or participate in electron-transfer processes, shortening excited-state lifetimes and sometimes generating reactive oxygen species.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: Calculate the Stokes shift if absorption maximum is 360 nm and emission maximum is 420 nm.

Answer: In wavelength terms the separation is 60 nm. For a rigorous energy shift, convert each wavelength to wavenumber or energy because energy is proportional to 1/λ.

Model answer 7

Question: Why can a rigid molecular environment increase fluorescence?

Answer: Restricting molecular rotations and vibrations can suppress non-radiative relaxation pathways, increasing the fraction of excited states that decay radiatively.

Model answer 8

Question: What is intersystem crossing?

Answer: It is a radiationless transition between states of different spin multiplicity, commonly singlet to triplet, enabled by spin–orbit coupling.

Model answer 9

Question: Why are actinometers used?

Answer: They are calibrated photochemical systems with known quantum yield, used to determine photon flux reaching a reaction vessel under defined geometry and wavelength conditions.

Model answer 10

Question: How can photoredox catalyst regeneration be verified?

Answer: Track electron balance and oxidation state through the catalytic cycle, and use spectroscopic or electrochemical measurements to detect catalyst intermediates and confirm turnover rather than stoichiometric consumption.
