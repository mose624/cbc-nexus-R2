How to read and explain the Jablonski diagram

A Jablonski diagram is an energy-level map of the electronic states of a molecule and the pathways by which an excited molecule loses energy or emits light. The vertical direction represents energy: higher horizontal lines represent higher electronic energy. The horizontal spacing is used to separate states for clarity; it is not a molecular distance or time axis.

  1. Ground state S₀: Most molecules initially occupy the lowest electronic singlet state, S₀. Several closely spaced vibrational levels exist within each electronic state.
  2. Absorption (upward straight arrow): A photon is absorbed when its energy matches an allowed energy gap. The molecule is promoted rapidly from S₀ to a vibrational level of S₁ or S₂. The Franck–Condon principle explains why the transition is effectively vertical on a nuclear-coordinate diagram: electrons move much faster than nuclei.
  3. Vibrational relaxation (short downward steps within a state): The excited molecule loses excess vibrational energy to its surroundings without changing electronic state. It usually reaches the lowest vibrational level of the excited electronic state before emission.
  4. Internal conversion (IC): A radiationless transition between electronic states of the same spin multiplicity, for example S₂ to S₁ or S₁ to S₀. Electronic energy is converted into vibrational energy and ultimately heat.
  5. Fluorescence (S₁ → S₀): A radiative transition between states of the same spin multiplicity. It is commonly prompt because the singlet excited-state lifetime is relatively short. Since some energy has already been lost by vibrational relaxation, fluorescence often occurs at a longer wavelength than the absorption band.
  6. Intersystem crossing (ISC; S₁ → T₁): A radiationless transition between states of different spin multiplicity. Spin–orbit coupling can mix singlet and triplet character and make this pathway possible.
  7. Phosphorescence (T₁ → S₀): A radiative triplet-to-singlet transition. It is spin-forbidden in the simple spin-selection-rule picture and is therefore often much slower than fluorescence. Emission may continue after the excitation source is switched off.
  8. Competing non-radiative decay: Internal conversion, collisional quenching, energy transfer and chemical reaction can compete with fluorescence and phosphorescence. The observed emission yield depends on the relative rate constants.

Key equations linked to the diagram

Photon energy: E = hν = hc/λ. For parallel first-order excited-state decay channels, the total decay rate is ktotal = kr + knr + kq + … and the lifetime is τ = 1/ktotal. In the simple two-channel fluorescence model, ΦF = kr/(kr + knr). A longer lifetime does not automatically mean a higher fluorescence yield: both radiative and non-radiative rates must be considered.

Common examination mistakes

  • Do not label internal conversion as emission: it is radiationless.
  • Do not confuse intersystem crossing with internal conversion: ISC changes spin multiplicity.
  • Do not say fluorescence is always exactly instantaneous; it has a finite excited-state lifetime.
  • Do not interpret the diagram's horizontal spacing as a time scale.
  • Do not assume every excited molecule fluoresces; competing pathways may dominate.

How to draw it in an examination: Draw S₀ and S₁ with several vibrational sublevels, add T₁ to one side, then use a straight upward arrow for absorption, short downward wavy arrows for vibrational relaxation and internal conversion, a downward solid arrow for fluorescence, a crossing dashed arrow for ISC, and a downward arrow from T₁ for phosphorescence. Label each pathway and state whether it is radiative or radiationless.

Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Photon energy is E=hν=hc/λ. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon. Molecular absorption requires an allowed transition whose energy matches the photon; selection rules and transition dipole moments govern intensity. UV-visible absorption often excites valence electrons.

Beer–Lambert law A=εlc links absorbance to concentration for a homogeneous, non-scattering sample. Deviations can arise from high concentration, chemical association, stray light, scattering, polychromatic radiation or detector limitations. Use a blank and a validated linear range.

Electronic excitation can create singlet or triplet states. In a singlet state, paired electrons have opposite spins and total spin S=0; a triplet state has S=1 with three spin sublevels. Spin multiplicity influences radiative transition probabilities and lifetimes.

A Jablonski diagram shows absorption, vibrational relaxation, internal conversion, intersystem crossing, fluorescence and phosphorescence. Absorption is usually very fast; vibrational cooling and internal conversion can occur before emission. The diagram is an energy-level map, not a time-scale plot.

Fluorescence is emission from an excited singlet state to a lower singlet state. The emitted photon often has lower energy and longer wavelength than the absorbed photon because of relaxation before emission. The difference is the Stokes shift.

Phosphorescence commonly involves emission from a triplet state to a singlet ground state. The transition is spin-forbidden and can be slow. Oxygen often quenches triplet states; rigid matrices and low temperature can reduce non-radiative pathways and reveal phosphorescence.

Quantum yield is the number of defined events divided by photons absorbed. For simple first-order excited-state decay, τ=1/(kr+knr), and ΦF=kr/(kr+knr). Additional quenching pathways add to the total decay constant and alter both lifetime and emission yield.

Internal conversion is radiationless transition between electronic states of the same spin multiplicity. Intersystem crossing changes multiplicity and is promoted by spin–orbit coupling. Both compete with radiative emission and influence the population of reactive excited states.

Primary photochemical events include bond cleavage, electron transfer, isomerisation, hydrogen abstraction and energy transfer. The excited-state potential-energy surface can differ from the ground-state surface, allowing transformations that are thermally inaccessible or follow different selectivity.

Photochemical rate depends on absorbed photon flux and quantum yield. Incident light is not equivalent to absorbed light because reflection, transmission and scattering matter. Actinometry estimates photon flux; vessel geometry, optical path and changing absorbance should be reported.

A sensitiser absorbs light and transfers energy or an electron to another species. Triplet energy transfer requires favourable excited-state energetics and sufficient electronic coupling. Electron transfer depends on redox potentials, solvent reorganisation and the excited-state lifetime.

Photoredox catalysts absorb visible light to form excited states with altered redox potentials. A catalytic cycle should track catalyst oxidation state and electron balance through quenching, substrate conversion and catalyst regeneration. Oxygen, light penetration and back-electron transfer can affect efficiency.

Environmental photochemistry includes direct photolysis and indirect reactions initiated by sensitised species such as hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen. Rates depend on solar spectrum, depth, turbidity, dissolved organic matter and oxygen. Laboratory lamp studies need comparable photon flux and spectral distribution.

Photovoltaic devices convert absorbed photons into separated charge carriers; photoelectrochemical systems couple light absorption to redox reactions at interfaces. Efficient devices require absorption, charge separation, transport, catalytic turnover and stability against corrosion or photodegradation.

UV sources can injure eyes and skin. Use shielding, interlocks and wavelength-appropriate protection. Photochemical reactions may generate reactive intermediates; control oxygen, solvent, temperature and vessel pressure, and record wavelength, irradiance and geometry for reproducibility.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Photochemistry: excited-state kinetics and energy transfer

1. Einstein relation and absorption

A photon carries E=hν=hc/λ. Absorption occurs when the photon energy matches an allowed energy difference and the transition dipole is non-zero. Beer–Lambert law A=εlc applies to a homogeneous, non-scattering sample within the instrument's linear range. Absorbed photon flux, not merely incident power, is needed for quantum-yield calculations.

2. Excited-state rate equation

For an excited state X* that decays by radiative and non-radiative first-order pathways, d[X*]/dt=−(kr+knr)[X*]. Integration gives [X*]t=[X*]0e^(−t/τ), where τ=1/(kr+knr). The fluorescence quantum yield is ΦF=kr/(kr+knr) for this simple model. Additional quenching or energy-transfer pathways add rate constants to the denominator.

3. Stern–Volmer quenching

For dynamic collisional quenching, F0/F=τ0/τ=1+KSV[Q], where KSV=kqτ0. A linear plot is consistent with a simple dynamic model. Static complex formation, heterogeneous environments, inner-filter effects and combined quenching can produce deviations; lifetime measurements help distinguish mechanisms.

4. Energy transfer and photoredox cycles

Triplet energy transfer is favourable when donor excited-state energy exceeds the acceptor triplet energy and orbital/spin constraints permit transfer. In photoredox catalysis, an excited photocatalyst can act as a stronger oxidant or reductant than its ground state. Construct a catalytic cycle by tracking oxidation state, electron balance and catalyst regeneration at every step.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Photon energy and molar energy

Problem: Calculate energy for 450 nm light in J photon⁻¹ and kJ mol⁻¹.

Solution: E=hc/λ=4.42×10⁻¹⁹ J photon⁻¹. Multiply by NA: 266 kJ mol⁻¹. This is blue visible light.

Worked numerical problem 2: Beer–Lambert concentration

Problem: A=0.750, ε=2.50×10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and l=1.00 cm. Find c.

Solution: c=A/(εl)=0.750/(2.50×10⁴)=3.00×10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹.

Worked numerical problem 3: Excited-state lifetime and yield

Problem: kr=4.0×10⁷ s⁻¹ and knr=6.0×10⁷ s⁻¹. Find τ and ΦF.

Solution: τ=1/(1.0×10⁸)=10 ns. ΦF=kr/(kr+knr)=0.40. Forty percent of excited molecules decay by fluorescence in this two-pathway model.

Worked numerical problem 4: Stern–Volmer quenching

Problem: KSV=12.0 L mol⁻¹ and quencher concentration 0.050 M. Find F0/F.

Solution: F0/F=1+KSV[Q]=1+12.0×0.050=1.60. Therefore F/F0=0.625, assuming simple dynamic quenching.

Worked numerical problem 5: Photochemical quantum yield

Problem: A sample absorbs 2.0×10¹⁸ photons and forms 3.0×10¹⁷ molecules of product. Find Φ.

Solution: Φ=3.0×10¹⁷/2.0×10¹⁸=0.150 molecule per absorbed photon. State whether chain amplification or multiple products per photon are possible.

Worked numerical problem 6: Energy-transfer feasibility

Problem: A donor triplet lies at 1.80 eV and an acceptor triplet at 1.45 eV. Is triplet energy transfer energetically plausible?

Solution: The donor has 0.35 eV more triplet energy, so transfer is energetically downhill by 0.35 eV. Spectral overlap, distance, coupling and spin constraints still determine the rate.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Distinguish fluorescence from phosphorescence.

Answer: Fluorescence commonly occurs from an excited singlet to a singlet ground state and is prompt. Phosphorescence commonly occurs from a triplet to singlet ground state and is slower because it is spin-forbidden.

Model answer 2

Question: What is the Stokes shift?

Answer: It is the separation between absorption and emission maxima, commonly caused by vibrational relaxation and solvent or structural reorganisation before emission.

Model answer 3

Question: Why must absorbed photon flux be used in quantum-yield calculations?

Answer: Photons that are not absorbed cannot initiate the measured photochemical event. Incident flux overestimates the number of photons available to the molecule.

Model answer 4

Question: How can dynamic and static quenching be distinguished?

Answer: Dynamic quenching reduces excited-state lifetime; pure static quenching reduces emitting population but does not necessarily change the lifetime of the remaining emissive molecules.

Model answer 5

Question: Explain why oxygen often quenches triplet states.

Answer: Molecular oxygen has a triplet ground state and can efficiently accept energy or participate in electron-transfer processes, shortening excited-state lifetimes and sometimes generating reactive oxygen species.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: Calculate the Stokes shift if absorption maximum is 360 nm and emission maximum is 420 nm.

Answer: In wavelength terms the separation is 60 nm. For a rigorous energy shift, convert each wavelength to wavenumber or energy because energy is proportional to 1/λ.

Model answer 7

Question: Why can a rigid molecular environment increase fluorescence?

Answer: Restricting molecular rotations and vibrations can suppress non-radiative relaxation pathways, increasing the fraction of excited states that decay radiatively.

Model answer 8

Question: What is intersystem crossing?

Answer: It is a radiationless transition between states of different spin multiplicity, commonly singlet to triplet, enabled by spin–orbit coupling.

Model answer 9

Question: Why are actinometers used?

Answer: They are calibrated photochemical systems with known quantum yield, used to determine photon flux reaching a reaction vessel under defined geometry and wavelength conditions.

Model answer 10

Question: How can photoredox catalyst regeneration be verified?

Answer: Track electron balance and oxidation state through the catalytic cycle, and use spectroscopic or electrochemical measurements to detect catalyst intermediates and confirm turnover rather than stoichiometric consumption.
