Foundations of human-computer interaction

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the interdisciplinary study and practice of designing, evaluating and understanding interactive computing systems for people. It draws on computer science, psychology, cognitive science, ergonomics, design, sociology and accessibility. HCI considers the complete relationship between a person, a task, a technology and the social and physical environment in which interaction takes place.

A successful interface is not simply attractive. It helps people achieve meaningful goals accurately, efficiently, safely and with an appropriate level of satisfaction. A technically correct system may still fail when terminology is confusing, feedback is missing, workflows conflict with real work or users cannot operate controls. HCI therefore treats usability and user experience as design requirements rather than cosmetic additions.

Human-centred design and ISO principles

Human-centred design places intended users, their tasks and their environments at the centre of development. It begins by understanding context, specifying user requirements, producing design solutions and evaluating those solutions with users. These activities are iterative: evaluation may reveal that the original problem statement or requirements need revision.

The process should involve users throughout the lifecycle, combine multidisciplinary expertise, consider the whole experience and use evaluation to improve designs. Participation should be meaningful and inclusive. A few senior stakeholders cannot reliably represent the needs of all users, especially people with disabilities, low digital confidence, limited connectivity or different language preferences.

Understanding users, stakeholders and personas

Users are people who directly operate a system, while stakeholders may also include administrators, customers, regulators, support staff, managers and people affected by its outputs. Stakeholder analysis identifies interests, influence, responsibilities, potential harms and information needs. A system designed only for its paying customer may overlook frontline staff or people whose data it processes.

Personas are evidence-informed representations of user groups. A useful persona describes goals, behaviours, context, capabilities, constraints and relevant technology access. Personas should be grounded in interviews, observation and research rather than stereotypes. They help teams discuss design decisions, but they are not substitutes for testing with actual users.

User research methods and field studies

Interviews explore experiences, motivations and explanations. Contextual inquiry combines observation with questions while people perform real tasks. Surveys can gather broader patterns but depend on clear wording and appropriate sampling. Diary studies capture behaviour over time, while focus groups reveal shared perceptions and disagreements.

Choose methods according to the question. To understand why staff bypass a workflow, observe the task and interview participants rather than relying only on a satisfaction score. Obtain informed consent, minimise collected personal information and explain how findings will be used. Record methods and limitations so that design decisions remain traceable to evidence.

Task analysis and workflow modelling

Task analysis decomposes a goal into activities, decisions, information needs and actions. Hierarchical task analysis represents goals, subtasks and plans; cognitive task analysis investigates knowledge and reasoning; and workflow models show the sequence and relationships between roles and systems.

Analyse frequency, duration, error consequences, interruptions, hand-offs and exceptional cases. A form may appear simple while requiring users to consult several records or seek approval elsewhere. Mapping the complete task can reveal opportunities to remove steps, clarify responsibility, prevent errors and reduce unnecessary cognitive effort.

Context of use and environmental constraints

Context of use includes physical conditions, social arrangements, organisational rules, devices, connectivity, lighting, noise, mobility and time pressure. The same interface may work well in a quiet office but fail on a small phone in bright sunlight or where network access is intermittent.

Designers should document representative contexts and test under realistic conditions. Consider shared devices, assistive technologies, low bandwidth, battery limits, interruptions and privacy in public spaces. Offline support, autosave, clear synchronisation status and resilient error recovery may be essential for users who cannot maintain continuous connectivity.

Cognitive psychology and mental models

Mental models are the internal explanations people use to predict how a system works. Users transfer expectations from familiar objects, services and previous applications. When an interface behaves consistently with a reasonable mental model, people can predict outcomes; when it violates expectations, they must guess or remember hidden rules.

Working memory is limited, so interfaces should avoid requiring users to remember information across distant screens. Recognition is generally easier than unaided recall. Group related information, use familiar language, show relevant choices and provide contextual help. Do not assume that an icon or metaphor is universally understood.

Attention, perception and information processing

Attention selects information for processing, while perception organises sensory input into meaningful patterns. Visual hierarchy, spacing, contrast, alignment and grouping influence what users notice. Important information can be missed when screens contain competing highlights, dense text, unexpected movement or ambiguous labels.

Design should support scanning and prioritisation. Place critical actions and status information where users can find them, use headings that communicate structure and distinguish interactive elements from static content. Avoid relying on colour alone, flashing effects or tiny targets. Test readability and comprehension across screen sizes and user conditions.

Memory, learning and cognitive load

Cognitive load describes the mental effort required to process information. Intrinsic load comes from task complexity, extraneous load from poor presentation and germane effort from building useful understanding. Interfaces cannot remove the inherent complexity of a task, but they can avoid adding unnecessary complexity through confusing navigation or inconsistent controls.

Support learning through progressive disclosure, examples, sensible defaults, clear instructions and immediate feedback. Keep terminology and control placement consistent. For infrequent or high-risk tasks, provide checklists and confirmation of important details. Avoid forcing users to memorise codes, rules or information that the system could safely display.

Usability: effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction

Usability is commonly evaluated through effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context. Effectiveness concerns whether users complete goals accurately; efficiency concerns the resources and time required; satisfaction concerns users' perceptions and comfort. Learnability, memorability, error prevention and accessibility are also important quality considerations.

A usability target must be measurable and tied to a task. For example, a defined proportion of representative users may be expected to complete a registration task without assistance, within a specified time and with no critical errors. Report sample characteristics, task conditions and confidence limits where appropriate rather than presenting a small test as universal proof.

User experience and emotional design

User Experience (UX) includes perceptions and responses before, during and after use. It may include usefulness, trust, credibility, enjoyment, aesthetics, accessibility, frustration and the wider service experience. UX is shaped not only by screens but also by policies, staff interactions, delivery reliability and expectations set by the organisation.

A visually polished interface cannot compensate for a service that loses information or makes unfair decisions. Emotional design should build confidence through clarity, respectful language, predictable behaviour and control. Avoid manipulative patterns that pressure users into purchases, consent or disclosure. Measure experience with qualitative feedback and appropriately interpreted quantitative indicators.

Interaction styles and interface paradigms

Common interaction styles include command languages, menus, forms, direct manipulation, natural-language interaction, touch, voice and conversational interfaces. Each style has strengths and limitations. Command interfaces can be powerful for expert users; menus support discovery; forms structure data entry; direct manipulation makes visible objects and actions easier to understand.

Choose interaction styles based on task frequency, complexity, error cost, user expertise, environment and available devices. A voice interface may be useful when hands are occupied but unsuitable in a noisy or privacy-sensitive setting. A graphical workflow may be easier to learn but slower for repeated expert operations. Hybrid designs can support different needs.

Navigation, information architecture and wayfinding

Information architecture organises content, functions and relationships so users can find what they need. Navigation systems include global navigation, local navigation, breadcrumbs, search, tabs and contextual links. Labels should reflect users' language and group related information according to their goals rather than internal organisational charts.

Provide clear location cues, consistent navigation and meaningful page titles. Avoid deep, unpredictable hierarchies and links with vague labels such as 'click here'. Search should tolerate reasonable variations and provide useful empty states. Users should be able to recover from wrong turns without losing completed work.

Visual interface design and layout

Visual design communicates hierarchy and supports comprehension through typography, colour, spacing, alignment, contrast, imagery and composition. A design system defines reusable components, tokens and rules so that screens remain consistent as a product grows. Visual consistency reduces the effort required to interpret each new screen.

Responsive layouts should adapt to available space without hiding essential functions. Use readable type sizes, sufficient contrast, clear focus indicators and appropriately sized controls. Avoid dense tables on narrow screens without a usable alternative. Test layouts with real content, including long names, translated text, validation messages and empty or error states.

Forms, input design and validation

Forms should request only information needed for a stated purpose. Group related fields, use labels that remain visible, indicate required fields and provide examples where formats may be unfamiliar. Choose input controls that match the data and allow users to correct mistakes without losing valid entries.

Validation should be timely, specific and actionable. Identify the field with an error, explain how to fix it and preserve the user's other entries. Distinguish format validation from business-rule validation and server-side verification. Avoid clearing an entire form after one error or relying only on colour to identify invalid values.

Feedback, system status and response time

Feedback tells users what happened after an action. Systems should acknowledge input, show progress for longer operations, confirm completion and explain failures. Without feedback, users may repeat actions, submit duplicate payments or assume that information has been saved when it has not.

Response-time design should reflect task expectations and the consequences of delay. Use progress indicators for operations that take noticeable time, preserve user work and provide a safe cancellation or retry route where possible. For asynchronous tasks, explain whether the user may leave the page and how they will know when processing is complete.

Error prevention, recovery and resilience

Errors may arise from slips, misunderstandings, invalid data, system failures or external conditions. Prevention includes sensible defaults, constraints, confirmation for consequential actions, clear affordances and checks at appropriate points. Confirmation dialogs should be reserved for actions with meaningful consequences rather than interrupting every routine operation.

Recovery includes undo, redo, version history, autosave, clear error messages and safe retry. Error messages should explain the problem in plain language, indicate what remains intact and offer a next step. Log technical details securely for support, but do not expose stack traces, credentials or sensitive records to ordinary users.

Accessibility and inclusive design

Accessibility aims to ensure that people with diverse abilities can perceive, understand, navigate and interact with digital services. Consider visual, auditory, motor, speech and cognitive access needs, including temporary and situational limitations. Accessibility is not a special feature for a small group; it improves resilience and usability across a broad population.

Use semantic headings, labels, keyboard operation, visible focus, meaningful alternative text, captions, transcripts, sufficient contrast and correctly announced status messages. Test with screen readers and keyboard-only navigation as well as automated tools. Follow recognised standards such as WCAG and assess conformance in the actual user journeys, not just isolated components.

Mobile, touch and responsive interaction

Mobile interaction is shaped by small screens, touch input, variable connectivity, orientation, battery use and frequent interruptions. Controls should have comfortable touch targets and sufficient spacing. Important tasks should not depend on hover, precision pointer movement or wide-screen layouts.

Responsive design reorganises content according to the viewport while preserving meaning and task completion. Prioritise essential content, avoid horizontal scrolling for ordinary reading and provide clear input types for mobile keyboards. Test on real devices and network conditions, including low-cost devices and older browsers used by the target population.

Prototyping: low, medium and high fidelity

A prototype is an artefact used to explore an idea, test assumptions or communicate behaviour. Low-fidelity prototypes may be sketches or paper screens; medium-fidelity prototypes show more detailed structure; high-fidelity prototypes simulate visual design and interactions. Fidelity should match the question being tested.

Early prototypes make it inexpensive to change structure and workflow before code is written. They should include realistic tasks and representative content. A polished prototype can create false confidence if users are only evaluating appearance rather than the underlying service. Clearly communicate what is functional, simulated or intentionally incomplete.

Usability testing and evaluation methods

Usability testing observes representative participants attempting realistic tasks while researchers collect evidence about completion, errors, time, navigation and experience. Moderated tests allow probing; unmoderated tests can reach more participants but provide less contextual insight. Heuristic evaluation and cognitive walkthroughs offer expert inspection methods.

Prepare neutral task scenarios, consent procedures, test environments and a plan for recording observations. Avoid leading participants toward the expected solution. Distinguish a usability problem from a participant's unfamiliarity with the domain, but do not dismiss repeated difficulties as user error. Prioritise findings by severity, frequency and impact.

Useful metrics include task completion rate, time on task, error frequency, abandonment, assistance required, satisfaction ratings and accessibility barriers. Metrics need operational definitions. For example, completion should specify whether partial success, assistance or critical mistakes count as failure.

A/B testing is appropriate when outcomes can be measured safely and users are not exposed to unacceptable risk. Heuristic evaluation and cognitive walkthroughs

Heuristic evaluation uses recognised usability principles to inspect an interface. Common principles include visibility of system status, match between system and real world, user control, consistency, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, flexibility, minimalist design and helpful recovery.

A cognitive walkthrough examines whether a new or infrequent user can identify the correct action, see that it is available, associate it with the goal and understand feedback. Evaluators should inspect complete task paths and document evidence, severity and recommendations. Expert review is efficient but does not replace user testing.

Ethics, privacy, trust and dark patterns

Ethical HCI considers autonomy, informed choice, dignity, fairness, transparency, privacy and the distribution of benefits and harms. Interfaces influence behaviour through defaults, framing, timing and the visibility of choices. Designers should recognise this power and avoid exploiting cognitive biases to obtain consent or purchases.

Dark patterns include obstructive cancellation, hidden costs, forced continuity, misleading urgency, confirm-shaming and confusing consent controls. Trustworthy design makes material terms clear, presents meaningful choices and allows users to change decisions. Collect only necessary data, explain its use and provide understandable privacy controls.

Applied case study: Redesigning a university student registration portal

A university registration portal is difficult to use on phones. Students report unclear programme requirements, repeated form errors and uncertainty about whether submissions have been saved. Administrative staff receive many duplicate enquiries and incomplete applications.

The project team maps the registration journey, interviews students and staff, observes task completion and reviews support logs. It creates personas based on evidence, defines the main tasks and tests a simplified information architecture. The redesign uses persistent labels, clear eligibility guidance, progressive steps, save-and-resume, accessible validation and explicit submission receipts.

The team pilots with students across different devices and access needs. It measures successful completion, time, error rates, support requests and accessibility barriers. Rollout proceeds only after critical defects are resolved and staff are trained to support exceptions.

Applied case study: Improving a mobile banking transfer journey

Customers abandon transfers because fees appear late, beneficiary details are difficult to verify and network interruptions create uncertainty. Some users repeat submissions because they cannot tell whether a transfer has completed.

Researchers observe the journey and identify points of uncertainty. The revised design displays fees and limits before confirmation, separates beneficiary review from final authorisation, provides clear progress and transaction references, and distinguishes pending, successful and failed states. Retry logic checks transaction status to reduce duplicate submissions.

The bank tests the flow with representative customers, including people using assistive technologies and low-bandwidth connections. It monitors completion, duplicate attempts, support contacts, error rates and complaints. Security and regulatory review are completed before release.

Applied case study: Accessible digital public-service application

Applied case study: AI-assisted customer support interface

A service organisation introduces an AI assistant to answer customer questions. Users cannot tell whether they are speaking to a person or automated system, and some generated answers are inaccurate or fail to account for account-specific restrictions.

The interface clearly identifies the assistant, states its capabilities and limits, grounds answers in approved support content and displays relevant source links. It offers a visible route to a human agent, asks for confirmation before consequential account actions and avoids requesting unnecessary sensitive information.

Evaluation measures resolution, escalation, factual accuracy, repeat contacts, user trust and error severity. High-risk topics are routed to trained staff. Conversation logs are protected, reviewed under governance rules and used to improve the service without treating generated responses as authoritative evidence.

University examination and revision questions

Answer with clear definitions, relevant examples and justification of design choices. The model answers provide concise guidance for core concepts.

Question 1. Define Human-Computer Interaction.

Model answer: The interdisciplinary study and practice of designing, evaluating and understanding interactive systems for people.

Question 2. What is human-centred design?

Model answer: An iterative approach that bases design on users, their tasks, requirements and context of use.

Question 3. Differentiate a user and a stakeholder.

Model answer: A user directly interacts with a system; a stakeholder may influence, support or be affected by it.

Question 4. What is a persona?

Model answer: An evidence-informed representation of a user group, including goals, behaviours and constraints.

Question 5. Name four user research methods.

Model answer: Interviews, contextual observation, surveys, diary studies, focus groups and usability tests.

Question 6. What is task analysis?

Model answer: The decomposition of a user goal into tasks, subtasks, decisions and information requirements.

Question 7. Define a mental model.

Model answer: A user's internal understanding of how a system works and what actions will produce.

Question 8. Explain cognitive load.

Model answer: The mental effort required to process information and complete a task.

Question 9. State the three common usability dimensions.

Model answer: Effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction.

Question 10. Differentiate usability and user experience.

Model answer: Usability concerns task effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction; UX includes broader perceptions and responses across the experience.

Question 11. What is information architecture?

Model answer: The organisation and labelling of content, functions and relationships to support finding and understanding.

Question 12. Why should forms use persistent labels?

Model answer: They remain available after users enter data and clarify the meaning of each field.

Question 13. What is progressive disclosure?

Model answer: Presenting essential information first and revealing additional detail when it is needed.

Question 14. State four error-prevention techniques.

Model answer: Constraints, sensible defaults, clear labels, inline validation and confirmation for consequential actions.

Question 15. What is a low-fidelity prototype?

Model answer: An inexpensive, simplified representation such as sketches or paper screens used to test early concepts.

Question 16. What is usability testing?

Model answer: Observing representative users attempting realistic tasks to identify interaction problems.

Question 17. Define heuristic evaluation.

Model answer: An expert inspection of an interface against established usability principles.

Question 18. What is a cognitive walkthrough?

Model answer: A task-focused inspection asking whether users can identify, select and understand actions and feedback.

Question 19. Name four usability metrics.

Model answer: Task completion, time on task, error rate, abandonment, assistance and satisfaction.

Question 20. Why is accessibility important?

Model answer: It enables people with diverse abilities and circumstances to use digital services and improves inclusion and resilience.

Question 21. Give four WCAG-related design practices.

Model answer: Semantic headings, keyboard access, visible focus, text alternatives, captions and sufficient contrast.

Question 22. What is responsive design?

Model answer: An approach that adapts layout and interaction to different screen sizes and device conditions.

Question 23. Define a dark pattern.

Model answer: An interface design that manipulates or obstructs users into choices they might not otherwise make.

Question 24. Why is feedback essential?

Model answer: It communicates system status and outcomes, reducing uncertainty and repeated or mistaken actions.

Question 25. Differentiate internationalisation and localisation.

Model answer: Internationalisation prepares a product for multiple locales; localisation adapts it to a particular locale.

Question 26. What is automation bias?

Model answer: The tendency to over-rely on automated recommendations or outputs, even when they may be wrong.

Question 27. A task takes 90 seconds before redesign and 60 seconds afterwards. Calculate the percentage reduction.

Model answer: (90 − 60) ÷ 90 × 100 = 33.3% reduction.

Question 28. In a test, 18 of 24 participants complete a task. Calculate the completion rate.

Model answer: 18 ÷ 24 × 100 = 75%.

Question 29. Why should usability studies recruit representative participants?

Model answer: The findings must reflect the range of users, tasks, abilities and contexts for which the system is intended.

Question 30. Give four measures for continuous UX improvement.

Model answer: Task success, accessibility defects, support contacts, satisfaction, error rates, retention and qualitative feedback.
