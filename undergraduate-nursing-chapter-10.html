Foundations of reproductive, maternal and newborn health

Maternal and newborn health spans reproductive wellbeing, preconception, pregnancy, childbirth, postnatal recovery and newborn development. Care should be respectful, person-centred, evidence-informed and free from discrimination, coercion and abuse. Communication must protect privacy and recognise the person's preferences, family context, language and decision-making rights.

Nursing and midwifery roles, scope and referral pathways differ by country. Students should use current national maternal and newborn guidelines, approved programme competencies and supervision. These notes are educational and do not authorise independent intrapartum procedures, prescribing or emergency management.

Reproductive anatomy, physiology and menstrual cycle

The female reproductive system includes ovaries, uterine tubes, uterus, cervix, vagina and external genital structures. The ovaries contain follicles and produce oocytes, oestrogens and progesterone. The uterus has endometrium, myometrium and perimetrium; the endometrium changes in response to ovarian hormones.

The hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis coordinates the menstrual cycle. Follicle-stimulating hormone supports follicular development; rising oestradiol contributes to endometrial proliferation and, when sustained, the luteinising hormone surge that triggers ovulation. The corpus luteum produces progesterone, supporting a secretory endometrium. Cycle length and symptoms vary.

Preconception care and reproductive planning

Preconception care identifies health conditions, medicines, nutritional needs, immunisation status, genetic or family history, mental health, substance exposure and social factors that may influence pregnancy. It supports informed reproductive choices and helps people access appropriate care before conception.

Counselling should be non-judgemental and tailored to the person's goals. Medication review must be performed by an authorised clinician; patients should not abruptly stop prescribed medicines. Folate, vaccination, chronic disease optimisation and screening recommendations vary by country and individual risk.

Normal pregnancy physiology and fetal development

Pregnancy produces adaptations in cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, gastrointestinal, endocrine and musculoskeletal systems. Plasma volume and cardiac output increase; systemic vascular resistance changes; renal filtration increases; and hormonal changes support uterine and breast development. Normal adaptations must be distinguished from warning symptoms and complications.

Embryonic and fetal development progresses through defined stages, with organ formation early in gestation and growth and maturation thereafter. The placenta supports gas, nutrient and waste exchange and produces hormones. Fetal growth and wellbeing are assessed using clinical history, examination and investigations appropriate to gestational age and local guidance.

Antenatal assessment and care

Antenatal care includes history, assessment of health and psychosocial needs, gestational dating, screening and prevention, health education and planning for birth and emergencies. The first contact establishes baseline health, previous pregnancy history, medicines, allergies, social support, safeguarding needs and risk factors.

Follow current local schedules for blood pressure, urine testing, blood tests, infection screening, immunisation, fetal growth assessment and ultrasound. Explain the purpose and limitations of tests and obtain informed consent. Encourage the pregnant person to report concerning symptoms promptly rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit.

Nutrition, wellbeing and health promotion in pregnancy

Nutrition education considers dietary diversity, food safety, hydration, culturally acceptable foods, nausea, anaemia risk and individual medical needs. Physical activity, sleep, mental wellbeing, oral health and avoidance of harmful substances are also relevant. Advice should be supportive rather than shaming and should reflect local food availability.

Supplement recommendations and food-safety advice vary by national guidance and individual circumstances. Refer concerns about severe vomiting, poor intake, eating disorders, substance dependence, intimate partner violence or mental distress through appropriate confidential pathways.

Labour physiology and stages of childbirth

Labour involves coordinated uterine contractions, cervical effacement and dilation, fetal descent and birth. The first stage extends from established labour to full cervical dilation; the second stage from full dilation to birth of the baby; and the third stage from birth to delivery of the placenta. Definitions and clinical management details follow local guidance.

Progress is influenced by uterine activity, fetal position and presentation, maternal anatomy, emotional state and clinical circumstances. Supportive care includes respectful communication, comfort measures, hydration and mobility where appropriate, observation and timely referral. Students must not independently conduct or manage birth outside authorised supervision.

Intrapartum assessment, fetal wellbeing and respectful care

Intrapartum assessment considers maternal observations, contractions, pain, labour progress, fetal presentation and wellbeing, membrane status, bleeding and the person's preferences. Monitoring method and frequency depend on risk, local guideline and clinical judgement. Explain procedures and findings in accessible language.

Respectful maternity care includes privacy, consent, companionship where permitted, culturally safe communication and freedom from mistreatment. Concerning maternal or fetal findings require prompt escalation to the qualified maternity team. A chart or partograph supports communication but does not replace assessment or clinical decisions.

Obstetric complications and emergency recognition

Important complications include hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, haemorrhage, sepsis, thromboembolism, obstructed labour, ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage and complications related to placental location or separation. Warning features can include severe headache, visual disturbance, severe abdominal pain, heavy bleeding, fever, collapse, seizures, breathlessness or reduced fetal movement.

Recognition and escalation are time-critical. Activate the local emergency pathway, call qualified help, communicate observations and follow the facility's approved emergency protocols. This chapter does not provide stand-alone treatment algorithms, drug doses or instructions for managing obstetric emergencies.

Postnatal assessment and maternal recovery

Postnatal care assesses physical recovery, bleeding, uterine involution, pain, perineal or surgical wounds, bladder and bowel function, mobility, breast health, sleep, mood and support needs. Recovery varies after vaginal and caesarean birth. Monitor for infection, haemorrhage, hypertensive complications, thromboembolism and mental health concerns.

Provide clear education on expected recovery and warning symptoms using current local discharge guidance. Ask about emotional wellbeing and safety in a private, supportive way. Escalate heavy bleeding, fever, severe headache, chest pain, breathlessness, unilateral leg swelling, confusion or thoughts of self-harm immediately.

Newborn transition, examination and essential care

At birth, the newborn transitions from placental gas exchange to breathing air and establishing independent circulation. Assessment includes breathing, heart rate, tone, colour and temperature, with structured scoring systems used according to local policy. A full newborn examination is performed by appropriately trained personnel.

Essential newborn care includes thermal protection, appropriate identification, infection prevention, early feeding support, assessment for congenital or acquired concerns and timely referral. Delayed transition, respiratory distress, poor feeding, abnormal temperature, lethargy, seizures or jaundice in the first day require prompt clinical assessment.

Breastfeeding, infant feeding and family planning

Breastfeeding support includes early skin-to-skin contact where safe, responsive feeding, positioning, attachment, milk transfer, feeding frequency and assessment of maternal comfort and infant output and weight trends. Provide practical, non-judgemental support and recognise that feeding decisions may be affected by health, medication, social and personal factors.

Where breastfeeding is not possible or chosen, families need safe, individualised information about appropriate alternatives and preparation. Postpartum contraception and birth spacing discussions should be voluntary, informed and culturally respectful. Method eligibility and timing depend on clinical factors and current national guidance.

Integrated clinical-learning case

Scenario: During a supervised placement, a student encounters a patient whose condition or care needs require closer assessment. The patient asks questions and expresses preferences that may affect the proposed plan. The student must listen, gather relevant information, check identity and consent, assess immediate safety, communicate findings and seek supervision.

Discussion prompts: Which information is subjective and which is objective? What additional assessment would be appropriate within the student's competence? Which features require urgent escalation? How can dignity, privacy and shared decision-making be protected? What information should be included in a structured handover?

Learning point: Safe nursing integrates knowledge, observation, communication, ethical conduct and teamwork. Clinical decisions and interventions must follow approved local protocols and qualified supervision.

Examination and revision questions

Answer in complete sentences. For extended responses, define terms, organise the explanation logically, use labelled diagrams where appropriate and connect the science to safe nursing care.

  1. List the main phases across the maternal-newborn continuum.

    Show answer guidance

    Preconception, antenatal care, labour and birth, postnatal recovery and newborn care.

  2. Which hormones are central to the menstrual cycle?

    Show answer guidance

    Follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinising hormone, oestrogen and progesterone.

  3. State two aims of preconception care.

    Show answer guidance

    Identify modifiable health risks and support informed reproductive planning before pregnancy.

  4. Name three physiological systems that adapt during pregnancy.

    Show answer guidance

    Cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, endocrine, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal systems adapt.

  5. What is the role of the placenta?

    Show answer guidance

    It supports exchange of gases, nutrients and waste between maternal and fetal circulations and produces hormones.

  6. What are the three stages of labour in the commonly taught framework?

    Show answer guidance

    First stage to full cervical dilation, second stage from full dilation to birth, and third stage from birth to delivery of the placenta.

  7. Give three warning symptoms in pregnancy that require prompt assessment.

    Show answer guidance

    Severe headache, visual disturbance, heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, fever, collapse, seizures, breathlessness or reduced fetal movement.

  8. What is respectful maternity care?

    Show answer guidance

    Care that protects dignity, privacy, informed choice, consent, cultural respect and freedom from mistreatment.

  9. What is the purpose of postnatal assessment?

    Show answer guidance

    To monitor maternal recovery, identify complications, support feeding and wellbeing, and plan safe follow-up.

  10. Describe one key feature of newborn transition at birth.

    Show answer guidance

    The newborn establishes breathing and transitions from placental gas exchange to pulmonary gas exchange and independent circulation.

  11. Name three essential newborn care priorities.

    Show answer guidance

    Thermal protection, feeding support, infection prevention, identification and assessment for concerns.

  12. What is skin-to-skin contact used for when safe and appropriate?

    Show answer guidance

    It supports warmth, bonding and early feeding and may help newborn adaptation.

  13. Why should postpartum family planning be voluntary?

    Show answer guidance

    Reproductive decisions require informed choice, autonomy and freedom from coercion.

  14. What should a student do if a maternal or newborn emergency sign appears?

    Show answer guidance

    Call qualified help immediately and follow the facility's approved emergency and escalation pathway.

  15. Why must national maternity guidelines be checked?

    Show answer guidance

    Schedules, screening, immunisation, referral criteria and clinical protocols vary and are updated over time.
