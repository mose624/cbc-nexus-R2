Unit guide and mathematical standard

These notes are intended for undergraduate study. Work through the derivations before memorising results. Unless stated otherwise, use SI units, state assumptions, retain units through calculations and round final values to a precision justified by the data. Check limiting cases and dimensions as part of every solution.

Method for numerical problems: identify known and unknown quantities; draw a labelled diagram where useful; select a physical model; state assumptions; derive or rearrange the governing relation; substitute values with units; calculate; then interpret and check the answer.

1. Periodic motion and simple harmonic motion

Simple harmonic motion (SHM) occurs when acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed towards equilibrium: a=−ω²x. The solution is x=A cos(ωt+φ), velocity v=−Aω sin(ωt+φ), and acceleration a=−Aω² cos(ωt+φ). The period T=2π/ω and frequency f=1/T.

2. Mass-spring oscillator

For a mass m attached to a spring of constant k, Hooke's law gives F=−kx. Newton's law yields m d²x/dt²=−kx, so d²x/dt²+(k/m)x=0 and ω=√(k/m). Total mechanical energy is E=½kA²=½mv_max².

3. Simple pendulum

For a point mass at distance L from a pivot, torque gives mL²θ¨=−mgL sinθ. For small angles sinθ≈θ, so θ¨+(g/L)θ=0 and T=2π√(L/g). The period is independent of bob mass and, within the small-angle approximation, amplitude.

4. Damped and driven oscillations

A damped oscillator satisfies m x¨+b x˙+kx=0. For underdamping, amplitude decays exponentially with envelope e^(−bt/2m). A driven oscillator adds F₀cosωt; steady-state amplitude depends on driving frequency and damping, with a resonance peak near the natural frequency for weak damping.

5. Travelling waves

A sinusoidal travelling wave may be written y(x,t)=A sin(kx−ωt+φ). Wavelength λ=2π/k, angular frequency ω=2πf and wave speed v=ω/k=fλ. The minus sign represents propagation in +x; a plus sign represents propagation in −x.

6. Superposition, interference and standing waves

When waves overlap, their displacements add. Constructive interference occurs for path difference mλ; destructive interference for (m+½)λ. Two equal waves travelling oppositely form a standing wave with nodes and antinodes. For a string fixed at both ends, λ_n=2L/n and f_n=nv/(2L).

7. Worked calculation: spring oscillation

A 0.40 kg mass is attached to a spring with k=100 N m⁻¹. The angular frequency is ω=√(k/m)=√250=15.81 rad s⁻¹. The period is T=2π/ω=0.397 s and frequency f=1/T=2.52 Hz. If amplitude A=0.050 m, maximum speed is v_max=ωA=0.791 m s⁻¹.

8. Worked calculation: pendulum

A pendulum has length 0.994 m and oscillates at small amplitude. T=2π√(L/g)=2π√(0.994/9.81)=2.00 s (approximately). Conversely, if measured T=2.00 s, g=4π²L/T²=4π²(0.994)/4=9.81 m s⁻².

9. Worked calculation: wave speed and harmonic

A string of length 0.80 m is fixed at both ends and has wave speed 120 m s⁻¹. Its fundamental frequency is f₁=v/(2L)=120/1.60=75 Hz. The third harmonic is f₃=3f₁=225 Hz, with three half-wavelength segments along the string.

10. Laboratory application: resonance

Drive a mass-spring system at varied frequencies and record steady-state amplitude. Plot amplitude against driving frequency. The peak identifies the resonance region; repeat for different damping. Record drive frequency, amplitude, phase where possible, and estimate uncertainty. Keep the oscillator within its linear elastic range.

Expanded concept-by-concept explanations and worked examples

Every concept below is followed immediately by a worked numerical example. Calculations use SI units and state the model assumptions.

1. Period, frequency and phase

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

40 cycles in 10 s gives f=4.0 Hz, T=0.25 s and ω=2πf=25.1 rad s⁻¹.

2. Deriving simple harmonic motion

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For m=0.50 kg and k=200 N m⁻¹, ω=√(k/m)=20 rad s⁻¹ and T=2π/ω=0.314 s. If A=0.040 m, vmax=ωA=0.80 m s⁻¹.

3. Energy in a spring oscillator

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For k=80 N m⁻¹ and A=0.10 m, E=½kA²=0.40 J. At x=0.060 m, U=0.144 J and K=E−U=0.256 J.

4. Simple pendulum derivation

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

For L=0.75 m, T=2π√(L/g)=1.74 s. This result assumes small angular displacement so sinθ≈θ.

5. Damping and quality factor

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

If amplitude halves in 5.0 s, envelope A/A₀=e^(−λt) gives λ=ln2/5=0.1386 s⁻¹. For m=0.20 kg, b=2mλ=0.0555 kg s⁻¹.

6. Forced oscillations and resonance

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A lightly damped oscillator with natural frequency 10 Hz has its strongest steady response near 10 Hz. At a 9.8 Hz drive it is near resonance; exact amplitude requires m, b, k and forcing amplitude.

7. Travelling waves and wave equation

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A wave with f=500 Hz and λ=0.68 m travels at v=fλ=340 m s⁻¹.

8. Superposition and interference

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

Two coherent waves each of amplitude 3 mm add to 6 mm when in phase; equal waves cancel when their phase difference is π.

9. Standing waves and harmonics

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

A 1.2 m string with wave speed 96 m s⁻¹ has f₁=v/(2L)=40 Hz; fourth harmonic f₄=160 Hz.

10. Beats and sound intensity

Worked example — step-by-step calculation

Frequencies 256 and 260 Hz produce beat frequency |260−256|=4 Hz, giving four loudness maxima each second.
