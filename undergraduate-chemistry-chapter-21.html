Detailed interpretation of every chapter diagram

Figure 21.1: Concentration-time kinetics curves

How to interpret it: Zero order gives linear [A] versus t; first order gives linear ln[A] versus t; second order in A gives linear 1/[A] versus t. The correct integrated plot identifies the order.

Laboratory connection: Measure concentration by UV-visible absorbance, titration, conductivity or chromatography at closely spaced times.

Figure 21.2: Integrated first-order rate plot

How to interpret it: ln[A]=ln[A]₀−kt. The slope is −k, intercept ln[A]₀ and t½=0.693/k, independent of initial concentration.

Laboratory connection: Follow dye bleaching or pseudo-first-order hydrolysis while one reactant remains in large excess.

Figure 21.3: Arrhenius plot

How to interpret it: ln k=ln A−Ea/RT. Slope of ln k against 1/T is −Ea/R; the intercept is ln A.

Laboratory connection: Determine k at multiple temperatures using a thermostatted bath and calibrated temperature probe.

Figure 21.4: Catalysed and uncatalysed energy profiles

How to interpret it: A catalyst provides an alternative lower-barrier pathway and accelerates forward and reverse reactions. It does not change equilibrium composition.

Laboratory connection: Compare initial rates at identical conditions, then verify that equilibrium composition is unchanged.

Figure 21.5: Consecutive reaction mechanism

How to interpret it: For A→B→C, intermediate B accumulates then declines. At its maximum, rate of formation equals rate of consumption.

Laboratory connection: Use rapid time-course HPLC or GC to quantify A, transient B and product C.

Additional university-level derivation

Additional derivation: For −d[A]/dt=k[A]ⁿ, separate variables: [A]⁻ⁿd[A]=−kdt. For n≠1, ([A]^(1−n)−[A]₀^(1−n))/(1−n)=−kt. For n=1, integration gives ln([A]/[A]₀)=−kt. The units of k therefore depend on reaction order.

Advanced numerical exercises with worked answers

Challenge 21.1

Question: A first-order reaction has k=3.20×10⁻³ s⁻¹. Find time for 87.5% conversion.

Worked answer: Remaining fraction=0.125. t=ln(1/0.125)/k=ln8/(3.20×10⁻³)=649 s.

Challenge 21.2

Question: Rate doubles from 298 K to 308 K. Estimate Ea.

Worked answer: Ea=R ln2/(1/298−1/308)=52.9 kJ mol⁻¹.

Challenge 21.3

Question: For rate=k[A]², k=0.250 L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ and [A]₀=0.800 M, find [A] after 10.0 s.

Worked answer: 1/[A]=1/[A]₀+kt=1.25+2.50=3.75 M⁻¹. [A]=0.267 M.

Calculation discipline: show the governing equation, substitute values with units, retain appropriate significant figures and state the chemical meaning of the result.

Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Define the reaction rate with stoichiometric normalisation. For aA+bB→cC+dD, rate=−(1/a)d[A]/dt=−(1/b)d[B]/dt=(1/c)d[C]/dt=(1/d)d[D]/dt. Average rate uses a finite time interval; instantaneous rate is the tangent slope to a concentration-time curve.

An empirical rate law is rate=k[A]^m[B]^n. The orders m and n come from experiment and need not match the balanced equation coefficients unless the reaction is elementary. The overall order is m+n; the units of k depend on that order and the chosen concentration/time units.

Initial-rate experiments vary one reactant concentration while holding others constant. Comparing two trials gives m=ln(rate2/rate1)/ln([A]2/[A]1). Pseudo-order conditions use a large excess of one reactant so its concentration is effectively constant during the measured interval.

For zero-order disappearance, −d[A]/dt=k. Integration gives [A]t=[A]0−kt and t1/2=[A]0/(2k). A plot of concentration against time is linear. Zero-order behaviour often appears when a catalyst surface or enzyme is saturated, or when a constant photon flux limits a photochemical process.

For first-order disappearance, −d[A]/dt=k[A]. Integration gives ln([A]t/[A]0)=−kt; the half-life is 0.693/k. A straight line in ln[A] versus time has slope −k. Radioactive decay and many unimolecular processes approximate first-order kinetics.

For second-order disappearance of A, −d[A]/dt=k[A]² and 1/[A]t=1/[A]0+kt. The half-life is 1/(k[A]0). The units of k are concentration⁻¹ time⁻¹. Do not use this integrated form for unequal initial concentrations of two different reactants.

Arrhenius behaviour is k=Ae^(−Ea/RT). A plot of ln k against 1/T has slope −Ea/R. A two-temperature calculation uses ln(k2/k1)=−Ea/R(1/T2−1/T1). The derived Ea is apparent if multiple steps contribute to the observed rate.

Collision theory links rate to collision frequency, orientation and the fraction of collisions with sufficient energy. Transition-state theory treats the activated complex as an equilibrium-like state across a free-energy barrier. Solvent reorganisation and entropy of activation can influence rates beyond simple collision energy.

An elementary step is a single molecular event. Its molecularity is unimolecular, bimolecular or (rarely) termolecular. A mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps whose sum gives the overall equation. Molecularity belongs to an elementary step, not to an overall multistep reaction.

A slow elementary step can control the rate, but rapid pre-equilibria and intermediate concentrations must be considered. Derive the predicted rate law from the proposed mechanism and test it against experimental orders. A mechanism is not established merely because its steps sum to the overall equation.

The steady-state approximation sets the rate of change of a reactive intermediate approximately to zero after an initial transient. Write its formation and consumption rates, solve for the intermediate concentration, then substitute into the product rate. State why the intermediate remains at low, nearly constant concentration.

The pre-equilibrium approximation assumes a fast reversible step establishes equilibrium before a slower subsequent step. Use the equilibrium expression to eliminate the intermediate. It is valid only when the first step relaxes much faster than the downstream consumption.

Consecutive reactions A→B→C can produce a transient maximum in B; parallel pathways A→B and A→C compete and product ratios depend on rate constants. Reversible reactions approach equilibrium when forward and reverse rates become equal. Time-course fitting helps distinguish these kinetic patterns.

Catalysts change the pathway and lower activation barriers without changing the overall ΔG or K. Homogeneous catalysts operate in the same phase as reactants; heterogeneous catalysts involve surface adsorption and desorption; enzymes use structured active sites and can display saturation or cooperativity.

For Michaelis–Menten kinetics, v=Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]). At [S]=Km, v=Vmax/2. Competitive inhibition increases apparent Km without changing Vmax in the ideal model; pure noncompetitive inhibition decreases Vmax. Real enzymes may show allostery, multiple substrates and product inhibition.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Advanced kinetics: derivations, mechanisms and interpretation

1. Integrated first-order law

For −d[A]/dt = k[A], separate variables: d[A]/[A] = −k dt. Integrating from [A]0 at t=0 to [A]t at time t gives ln([A]t/[A]0) = −kt. The half-life follows by setting [A]t = [A]0/2: t1/2 = ln2/k. A linear ln[A] versus t plot supports first-order behaviour, but does not by itself prove a molecular mechanism.

2. Integrated second-order law

For −d[A]/dt = k[A]², separation gives d[A]/[A]² = −kdt. Integration yields 1/[A]t − 1/[A]0 = kt. The half-life is 1/(k[A]0), so it depends on initial concentration. For A+B with unequal starting concentrations, integrate the two-reactant rate law using the concentration difference; do not apply the A² expression blindly.

3. Arrhenius equation

Taking logarithms of k = Aexp(−Ea/RT) gives ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT). The slope of ln k versus 1/T is −Ea/R. For two temperatures, ln(k2/k1) = −Ea/R(1/T2 − 1/T1). Temperatures must be kelvin. An apparent activation energy can reflect multiple elementary steps or changing mechanism.

4. Steady-state derivation for an intermediate

For A + B ⇌ I followed by I → P, write d[I]/dt = k1[A][B] − k−1[I] − k2[I]. Under steady state, set d[I]/dt ≈ 0, giving [I] = k1[A][B]/(k−1+k2). The product rate is d[P]/dt = k2[I] = k1k2[A][B]/(k−1+k2). The approximation is most useful after the initial transient and when the intermediate remains at low concentration.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Determine order from initial rates

Problem: Doubling [A] at fixed [B] makes rate four times larger; doubling [B] at fixed [A] doubles rate.

Solution: For rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, 2^m=4 so m=2; 2^n=2 so n=1. Overall order is 3. Rate law is k[A]²[B].

Worked numerical problem 2: First-order half-life and remaining fraction

Problem: A first-order process has k = 2.31×10⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate half-life and fraction after 600 s.

Solution: t1/2=0.693/k=300 s. Fraction remaining=e^(−kt)=e^(−1.386)=0.250. One quarter remains after two half-lives.

Worked numerical problem 3: Second-order concentration after time

Problem: For k=0.150 L mol⁻¹ min⁻¹, [A]0=0.200 mol L⁻¹ and t=20.0 min, find [A]t.

Solution: 1/[A]t=1/0.200+0.150×20=5.00+3.00=8.00 L mol⁻¹. [A]t=0.125 mol L⁻¹.

Worked numerical problem 4: Activation energy from two temperatures

Problem: k rises from 2.0×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at 290 K to 1.0×10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 310 K. Find Ea.

Solution: Ea=R ln(5)/(1/290−1/310)=6.10×10⁴ J mol⁻¹ =61.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The calculation assumes the same mechanism and Arrhenius regime at both temperatures.

Worked numerical problem 5: Pseudo-first-order hydrolysis

Problem: A reaction is first order in ester and first order in water. Water is present at effectively constant concentration 55.5 mol L⁻¹.

Solution: rate=k[ester][H2O]=kobs[ester], where kobs=k[H2O]. The observed kinetics are first order in ester, although the underlying rate law is second order overall.

Worked numerical problem 6: Catalyst and equilibrium

Problem: A catalyst increases forward and reverse rate constants by the same factor at equilibrium.

Solution: The catalyst lowers the kinetic barrier for both directions and accelerates approach to equilibrium. It does not change ΔG°, K or the equilibrium composition at fixed temperature.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Why must reaction order be measured experimentally?

Answer: For a complex reaction, the overall stoichiometric equation is the sum of elementary steps. The observed rate law depends on the mechanism and concentrations of intermediates, so exponents need not equal overall stoichiometric coefficients.

Model answer 2

Question: What does a linear ln[A] versus time plot indicate?

Answer: It is consistent with first-order disappearance under the measured conditions. The slope is −k. Other evidence is required to establish the reaction mechanism.

Model answer 3

Question: Explain the steady-state approximation.

Answer: It assumes an intermediate is produced and consumed at nearly equal rates after an initial transient, so its concentration changes slowly. It is not the same as assuming the intermediate is at equilibrium.

Model answer 4

Question: How does a catalyst affect activation energy and equilibrium?

Answer: It provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation barrier, increasing rates. It does not change the reaction free energy or equilibrium constant because it changes neither initial nor final thermodynamic states.

Model answer 5

Question: Distinguish molecularity from reaction order.

Answer: Molecularity is the number of reacting species in one elementary event and is an integer. Reaction order is determined from the experimental rate law and may be zero, fractional or integer.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: A first-order reaction is 75% complete after 20 minutes. Find k.

Answer: 25% remains, so ln(0.25)=−k(20). k=ln4/20=0.0693 min⁻¹. The half-life is 0.693/k=10.0 min.

Model answer 7

Question: How can a rate law distinguish proposed mechanisms?

Answer: A proposed mechanism predicts a rate expression after eliminating intermediates using justified approximations. Compare the predicted concentration dependencies and limiting behaviour with measured initial rates and time-course data.

Model answer 8

Question: Why may a reaction show non-Arrhenius behaviour?

Answer: The mechanism, rate-limiting step, diffusion regime, solvent structure or catalyst state may change with temperature. A single straight Arrhenius line is only expected over a range with comparable mechanism and conditions.

Model answer 9

Question: What is the difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis?

Answer: Homogeneous catalysts share the reactant phase and often provide molecularly defined cycles. Heterogeneous catalysts operate at interfaces and involve adsorption, surface reaction and desorption; transport and surface structure become important.

Model answer 10

Question: A reaction rate doubles when temperature rises from 298 to 308 K. Estimate Ea.

Answer: ln2=Ea/R(1/298−1/308). Ea=R ln2/(1/298−1/308)=52.9 kJ mol⁻¹. This is an apparent value assuming Arrhenius behaviour over the interval.
