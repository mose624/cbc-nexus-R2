Key equations and fully worked calculations

Rate law: rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. Determine m and n experimentally.

First order: ln([A]t/[A]0) = −kt; t½ = 0.693/k. Second order in A: 1/[A]t = 1/[A]0 + kt.

Arrhenius: ln(k₂/k₁) = −Ea/R(1/T₂ − 1/T₁).

Worked calculation A: first-order decay

A reactant has k = 0.0350 min⁻¹. Its half-life is t½ = 0.693/0.0350 = 19.8 min. After 40.0 min, [A]t/[A]0 = e⁻ᵏᵗ = e⁻¹·⁴⁰ = 0.247.

About 24.7% remains. This is consistent with slightly more than two half-lives (40.0/19.8 ≈ 2.02).

Worked calculation B: second-order integrated law

For −d[A]/dt = k[A]², let [A]0 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹, k = 0.200 L mol⁻¹ min⁻¹ and t = 10.0 min. Then 1/[A]t = 10.0 + 2.00 = 12.0 L mol⁻¹, so [A]t = 0.0833 mol L⁻¹.

Units of k are L mol⁻¹ min⁻¹ for this second-order rate law.

Worked calculation C: Arrhenius activation energy

A rate constant increases from 1.00 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at T₁ = 300 K to 4.00 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at T₂ = 320 K. Ea = R ln(4)/(1/300 − 1/320) = 5.54 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = 55.4 kJ mol⁻¹.

The estimate assumes a single Arrhenius regime and a temperature-independent pre-exponential factor over this interval.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Advanced kinetics: derivations, mechanisms and interpretation

1. Integrated first-order law

For −d[A]/dt = k[A], separate variables: d[A]/[A] = −k dt. Integrating from [A]0 at t=0 to [A]t at time t gives ln([A]t/[A]0) = −kt. The half-life follows by setting [A]t = [A]0/2: t1/2 = ln2/k. A linear ln[A] versus t plot supports first-order behaviour, but does not by itself prove a molecular mechanism.

2. Integrated second-order law

For −d[A]/dt = k[A]², separation gives d[A]/[A]² = −kdt. Integration yields 1/[A]t − 1/[A]0 = kt. The half-life is 1/(k[A]0), so it depends on initial concentration. For A+B with unequal starting concentrations, integrate the two-reactant rate law using the concentration difference; do not apply the A² expression blindly.

3. Arrhenius equation

Taking logarithms of k = Aexp(−Ea/RT) gives ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT). The slope of ln k versus 1/T is −Ea/R. For two temperatures, ln(k2/k1) = −Ea/R(1/T2 − 1/T1). Temperatures must be kelvin. An apparent activation energy can reflect multiple elementary steps or changing mechanism.

4. Steady-state derivation for an intermediate

For A + B ⇌ I followed by I → P, write d[I]/dt = k1[A][B] − k−1[I] − k2[I]. Under steady state, set d[I]/dt ≈ 0, giving [I] = k1[A][B]/(k−1+k2). The product rate is d[P]/dt = k2[I] = k1k2[A][B]/(k−1+k2). The approximation is most useful after the initial transient and when the intermediate remains at low concentration.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Determine order from initial rates

Problem: Doubling [A] at fixed [B] makes rate four times larger; doubling [B] at fixed [A] doubles rate.

Solution: For rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, 2^m=4 so m=2; 2^n=2 so n=1. Overall order is 3. Rate law is k[A]²[B].

Worked numerical problem 2: First-order half-life and remaining fraction

Problem: A first-order process has k = 2.31×10⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate half-life and fraction after 600 s.

Solution: t1/2=0.693/k=300 s. Fraction remaining=e^(−kt)=e^(−1.386)=0.250. One quarter remains after two half-lives.

Worked numerical problem 3: Second-order concentration after time

Problem: For k=0.150 L mol⁻¹ min⁻¹, [A]0=0.200 mol L⁻¹ and t=20.0 min, find [A]t.

Solution: 1/[A]t=1/0.200+0.150×20=5.00+3.00=8.00 L mol⁻¹. [A]t=0.125 mol L⁻¹.

Worked numerical problem 4: Activation energy from two temperatures

Problem: k rises from 2.0×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at 290 K to 1.0×10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 310 K. Find Ea.

Solution: Ea=R ln(5)/(1/290−1/310)=6.10×10⁴ J mol⁻¹ =61.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The calculation assumes the same mechanism and Arrhenius regime at both temperatures.

Worked numerical problem 5: Pseudo-first-order hydrolysis

Problem: A reaction is first order in ester and first order in water. Water is present at effectively constant concentration 55.5 mol L⁻¹.

Solution: rate=k[ester][H2O]=kobs[ester], where kobs=k[H2O]. The observed kinetics are first order in ester, although the underlying rate law is second order overall.

Worked numerical problem 6: Catalyst and equilibrium

Problem: A catalyst increases forward and reverse rate constants by the same factor at equilibrium.

Solution: The catalyst lowers the kinetic barrier for both directions and accelerates approach to equilibrium. It does not change ΔG°, K or the equilibrium composition at fixed temperature.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Why must reaction order be measured experimentally?

Answer: For a complex reaction, the overall stoichiometric equation is the sum of elementary steps. The observed rate law depends on the mechanism and concentrations of intermediates, so exponents need not equal overall stoichiometric coefficients.

Model answer 2

Question: What does a linear ln[A] versus time plot indicate?

Answer: It is consistent with first-order disappearance under the measured conditions. The slope is −k. Other evidence is required to establish the reaction mechanism.

Model answer 3

Question: Explain the steady-state approximation.

Answer: It assumes an intermediate is produced and consumed at nearly equal rates after an initial transient, so its concentration changes slowly. It is not the same as assuming the intermediate is at equilibrium.

Model answer 4

Question: How does a catalyst affect activation energy and equilibrium?

Answer: It provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation barrier, increasing rates. It does not change the reaction free energy or equilibrium constant because it changes neither initial nor final thermodynamic states.

Model answer 5

Question: Distinguish molecularity from reaction order.

Answer: Molecularity is the number of reacting species in one elementary event and is an integer. Reaction order is determined from the experimental rate law and may be zero, fractional or integer.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: A first-order reaction is 75% complete after 20 minutes. Find k.

Answer: 25% remains, so ln(0.25)=−k(20). k=ln4/20=0.0693 min⁻¹. The half-life is 0.693/k=10.0 min.

Model answer 7

Question: How can a rate law distinguish proposed mechanisms?

Answer: A proposed mechanism predicts a rate expression after eliminating intermediates using justified approximations. Compare the predicted concentration dependencies and limiting behaviour with measured initial rates and time-course data.

Model answer 8

Question: Why may a reaction show non-Arrhenius behaviour?

Answer: The mechanism, rate-limiting step, diffusion regime, solvent structure or catalyst state may change with temperature. A single straight Arrhenius line is only expected over a range with comparable mechanism and conditions.

Model answer 9

Question: What is the difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis?

Answer: Homogeneous catalysts share the reactant phase and often provide molecularly defined cycles. Heterogeneous catalysts operate at interfaces and involve adsorption, surface reaction and desorption; transport and surface structure become important.

Model answer 10

Question: A reaction rate doubles when temperature rises from 298 to 308 K. Estimate Ea.

Answer: ln2=Ea/R(1/298−1/308). Ea=R ln2/(1/298−1/308)=52.9 kJ mol⁻¹. This is an apparent value assuming Arrhenius behaviour over the interval.
