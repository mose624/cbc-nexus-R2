CBE NEXUS · UNDERGRADUATE PSYCHOLOGY · CHAPTER 14

Organisational and Occupational Psychology

Undergraduate textbook notes · People at work · Leadership · Wellbeing · Organisational systems

Learning outcomes: Explain major work-psychology concepts; analyse jobs and selection systems; compare motivation and leadership theories; evaluate teams and culture; identify psychosocial risks; and design ethical, evidence-informed organisational interventions.

Introduction

Organisational and occupational psychology applies scientific knowledge to the relationship between people and work. It examines how jobs, teams, leaders, technologies and organisational systems shape performance and wellbeing. The field is concerned not only with selecting capable employees but also with designing work that is safe, meaningful, fair and sustainable. This chapter introduces core theories and methods and shows how they can be used to diagnose organisational problems without blaming individuals for failures created by systems.

1. Definition, scope and evolution of organisational psychology

Organisational psychology studies how people think, feel and behave at work and how organisational systems influence individual and collective performance. It applies psychological theories and research methods to recruitment, learning, motivation, leadership, teamwork, wellbeing, job design, organisational culture, change and performance. Occupational psychology is closely related and often emphasises the relationship between people, work tasks and working conditions. In practice, the labels organisational psychology, occupational psychology and industrial-organisational psychology overlap, while professional titles and regulation vary between countries. The central concern is to improve the quality of working life and organisational effectiveness in ways that are scientifically defensible and ethically responsible.

The field developed from early studies of individual differences, vocational guidance, selection, fatigue, productivity and group behaviour. Its focus broadened beyond matching a worker to a task towards understanding motivation, social relationships, leadership, organisational structures and employee wellbeing. Contemporary practice recognises that productivity cannot be separated from safety, dignity, inclusion and sustainable work. An organisation may achieve short-term output through excessive pressure yet incur long-term costs through injury, turnover, burnout and loss of trust. Organisational psychologists therefore examine outcomes for employees, teams, customers and society rather than treating financial performance as the only criterion.

2. Levels of analysis and the work system

Work behaviour can be analysed at several connected levels. At the individual level, relevant factors include ability, personality, knowledge, values, motivation, health and experience. At the team level, attention shifts to communication, roles, norms, conflict, trust and coordination. At the organisational level, structure, culture, leadership systems, technology, policies and resource allocation shape behaviour. Wider influences include labour markets, law, economic conditions, culture, technology and environmental change. An explanation that focuses on only one level may misdiagnose a problem: low performance may reflect unclear goals, inadequate equipment or excessive workload rather than low employee motivation.

A systems perspective examines how components interact and how consequences feed back into the system. For example, understaffing may increase workload, which raises fatigue and errors; errors then create rework, increasing workload further. Interventions directed only at individual resilience will not solve a structural staffing problem. Organisational diagnosis should therefore map inputs, processes, outputs and feedback. It should identify where decision authority lies, whose work is affected and which unintended consequences may follow from a proposed change.

3. Job analysis and work design

Job analysis is the systematic collection of information about tasks, responsibilities, work context and the knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics needed for effective performance. Methods include interviews with job holders and supervisors, direct observation, work diaries, document analysis and structured questionnaires. A job description summarises duties and reporting relationships; a person specification describes relevant competencies and qualifications. Analysis should distinguish essential requirements from customary but unnecessary criteria. Poor job analysis can create unfair selection, unrealistic workloads and unclear accountability.

Work design concerns how tasks, autonomy, feedback, workload, relationships and physical conditions are organised. The job characteristics model highlights skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback as features that can influence experienced meaningfulness, responsibility and knowledge of results. These features are not universal prescriptions: some jobs require standardisation for safety, while others benefit from discretion. Good design considers cognitive demands, ergonomic conditions, emotional labour, shift patterns, accessibility and opportunities for learning. Employee participation can reveal practical constraints that management documents overlook.

4. Individual differences, personality and ability at work

Employees differ in cognitive abilities, personality traits, interests, values, experience, skills and preferred ways of working. These differences can influence learning, decision-making, collaboration and response to job demands, but they should not be used to stereotype people or make unsupported assumptions. Personality models such as the Big Five describe broad dimensions including openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and emotional stability. Their usefulness depends on the purpose, quality of measurement and context. A trait score is not a complete description of a person and should not be treated as destiny.

Cognitive ability can be relevant to some complex learning and problem-solving tasks, while specific skills and experience may be more predictive for others. Competency models translate job requirements into observable behaviours, but they can become vague or culturally biased if poorly designed. Fair assessment uses job-related criteria, reliable methods and reasonable adjustments. Employers should avoid using personality tests as entertainment or relying on unvalidated online quizzes for high-stakes decisions. Assessment information must be interpreted by competent practitioners and combined with other relevant evidence.

5. Recruitment, selection and fair assessment

Recruitment attracts a pool of applicants; selection evaluates evidence to identify candidates who meet essential job requirements. A defensible process begins with job analysis and clear criteria. Methods may include structured interviews, work samples, job knowledge tests, situational judgement exercises, assessment centres and validated ability measures. Structured methods use consistent questions and scoring anchors, improving comparability and reducing the influence of irrelevant impressions. A work sample can provide direct evidence of a task, although it must be designed so that applicants have a fair opportunity to demonstrate relevant ability.

Selection quality is assessed through reliability, validity, fairness, utility and candidate experience. Criterion-related validity asks whether assessment scores predict relevant work outcomes; content validity asks whether the assessment represents important job tasks; construct validity asks whether the measure assesses the intended psychological attribute. Adverse impact should be monitored across groups, while recognising that legal definitions and remedies differ by jurisdiction. Recruiters should provide accessible processes, protect applicant data, avoid discriminatory questions and communicate decisions respectfully. A statistically useful tool can still be ethically unacceptable if it is intrusive or unrelated to the job.

6. Training needs analysis and employee learning

Training and development build knowledge, skills, judgement and capability needed for current and future work. A training needs analysis operates at organisational, task and individual levels. Organisational analysis asks which strategic or operational outcomes require improvement. Task analysis identifies the competencies needed for particular duties. Individual analysis determines who needs support and what learning gap exists. Training should not be used as the default response to every performance problem: unclear procedures, defective equipment, poor staffing or conflicting incentives may require system changes instead.

Adult learning is strengthened when learners understand the purpose, connect new ideas to prior experience, practise relevant tasks and receive useful feedback. Effective programmes use demonstrations, guided practice, reflection, spaced retrieval and opportunities to apply learning at work. Transfer depends on supervisor support, time, tools and reinforcement. Evaluation should examine learning, behaviour change and organisational outcomes, not attendance alone. A course can be popular yet fail to improve performance if the workplace does not allow employees to use the new skills.

7. Motivation theories and employee engagement

Work motivation concerns the direction, intensity and persistence of effort. Expectancy theory proposes that motivation is influenced by beliefs that effort can produce performance, performance can lead to outcomes and those outcomes are valued. Equity and organisational justice perspectives consider how people compare contributions and rewards and whether procedures and treatment are fair. Goal-setting theory emphasises specific, challenging goals when people have commitment, ability, feedback and adequate resources. Self-determination theory highlights autonomy, competence and relatedness as important psychological needs. Each theory explains some patterns but should be applied with attention to job context and individual differences.

Employee engagement is often described through energy, dedication and absorption, although definitions and measures vary. Engagement is influenced by meaningful work, resources, leadership, role clarity, fairness, development opportunities and manageable demands. It should not be confused with compulsory enthusiasm or unpaid extra effort. A workplace can report high engagement scores while employees feel unable to raise concerns. Good measurement includes employee voice, workload, safety, inclusion and wellbeing, and leaders should act on findings rather than conduct surveys that produce no visible response.

8. Job satisfaction, commitment and psychological contracts

Job satisfaction refers to a person's evaluation of their job and its aspects, such as tasks, supervision, pay, colleagues, development and working conditions. It can be measured globally or by facets. Organisational commitment describes the psychological relationship between an employee and the organisation; models distinguish emotional attachment, perceived costs of leaving and felt obligation. These constructs are related but not identical. A person may enjoy the work but intend to leave because of poor career prospects, or remain because alternatives are limited despite low satisfaction.

The psychological contract concerns perceived mutual obligations that may not be written into a formal employment agreement. Employees may expect fair treatment, development or reasonable flexibility, while employers may expect reliability and contribution. Perceived breach can reduce trust and commitment. Managers should communicate realistic expectations and avoid promising opportunities that cannot be delivered. Surveys can identify patterns, but interpretation requires attention to job type, tenure, workload, labour-market conditions and whether employees feel safe to answer honestly.

9. Leadership theories and responsible leadership

Leadership is a process through which people influence direction, coordination and collective effort. Trait approaches examine characteristics associated with leadership, while behavioural approaches study task-focused and relationship-focused actions. Contingency theories propose that effectiveness depends on fit between leadership behaviour and situational conditions. Transformational leadership emphasises purpose, intellectual stimulation, individual consideration and motivating vision; transactional leadership clarifies expectations and uses contingent rewards or corrective action. No single style is best in every situation, and leadership labels should not substitute for evidence about actual behaviour and outcomes.

Responsible leadership considers ethical conduct, accountability, stakeholder interests and the long-term consequences of decisions. Leaders shape psychological safety by how they respond to questions, mistakes and dissent. A leader who demands openness but punishes criticism creates a contradictory climate. Effective leadership requires listening, clear decisions, fair processes and willingness to acknowledge uncertainty. Power differences can silence employees, so leaders should create channels for confidential feedback and protect people who raise legitimate concerns.

10. Teams, communication and collaboration

Teams are groups whose members coordinate interdependent tasks towards shared goals. Team effectiveness depends on clear purpose, appropriate membership, role clarity, resources, communication, trust and the ability to manage disagreement. Team development models describe possible patterns as groups form, negotiate roles, establish norms and perform, but real teams may move repeatedly between these states. A team should not be judged only by harmony: constructive disagreement can improve decisions when members can challenge assumptions respectfully.

Communication failures can arise from hierarchy, jargon, time pressure, cultural differences, remote work and unclear responsibility. Shared mental models help members understand tasks and anticipate one another's actions. Briefings, handovers, checklists and structured decision records can reduce coordination errors in high-risk work. Psychological safety supports speaking up, but it does not mean absence of standards or accountability. Managers should distinguish interpersonal conflict from disagreement about the task and address bullying or discrimination directly.

11. Conflict, negotiation and workplace relationships

Conflict may concern tasks, processes, resources, values or relationships. Some task disagreement can improve problem-solving, while persistent personal conflict damages trust and performance. Conflict analysis should identify the parties, interests, power differences, communication patterns, organisational rules and practical constraints. Negotiation seeks an agreement through discussion; principled approaches distinguish positions from underlying interests and explore options that may benefit more than one party. Not every dispute is suitable for informal negotiation, especially where there is harassment, violence or a serious power imbalance.

Mediation uses an impartial third party to support voluntary discussion and agreement, but it does not replace formal investigation where allegations require fact-finding. Managers should apply policies consistently, protect confidentiality as far as possible and prevent retaliation. Workplace relationships improve when expectations are clear, feedback is respectful and decisions are explained. Organisational psychologists may advise on conflict systems, but must remain alert to whose interests are represented and whether a process is genuinely fair.

12. Organisational culture, climate and values

Organisational culture refers to shared assumptions, values and patterns of meaning that influence how work is understood and conducted. It may be visible in stories, rituals, language, symbols and routines, but deeper assumptions can be difficult to observe. Organisational climate concerns employees' shared perceptions of policies, practices and behaviours that are rewarded or supported. Culture and climate are related but not identical: climate may change more quickly, while underlying assumptions can persist. Neither should be reduced to a slogan displayed on a wall.

Culture can support safety, learning and cooperation, or reinforce silence, exclusion and excessive competition. Subcultures may differ across professions, sites and hierarchical levels. Assessment should combine interviews, observation, document review and survey data, while protecting employees from retaliation. Leaders cannot transform culture simply by announcing new values; they must align incentives, resource decisions, promotion practices and everyday conduct with the desired standards. Culture change is more credible when employees can see consistent action.

13. Performance management and feedback

Performance management is an ongoing process for clarifying expectations, supporting capability, reviewing contribution and making decisions about development or accountability. It should connect individual responsibilities with team and organisational goals while recognising that outcomes are affected by resources, systems and external conditions. Effective objectives are clear and relevant, and employees should know how performance will be assessed. A single annual rating may miss changing priorities, informal contributions and barriers outside the employee's control.

Feedback is most useful when it is timely, specific, behaviour-focused and linked to improvement. It should describe observable evidence rather than attack personality. Performance discussions should allow employees to explain context, identify support and agree follow-up actions. Ratings can be distorted by recency, halo, similarity, leniency and central-tendency biases. Calibration and rater training may help, but organisations should also question whether the rating system itself creates unhealthy competition or discourages collaboration.

14. Occupational health, safety and psychosocial risk

Occupational health psychology examines how work affects health and how healthy work can be designed. Hazards include physical risks, chemical exposure, ergonomic strain, fatigue, violence, bullying, excessive demands, low control, role conflict and job insecurity. Psychosocial risk assessment identifies hazards in work organisation and relationships, not merely employees' ability to cope. Prevention should prioritise eliminating or reducing hazards at source, supported by appropriate training, reporting systems and access to occupational health services.

Stress is a response to demands perceived as exceeding available resources, but individual responses vary. Chronic exposure to high demands and inadequate control or support can contribute to exhaustion, sleep problems and reduced wellbeing. Burnout is commonly described through exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism and reduced professional efficacy, though measurement and classification vary. Organisations should not use resilience programmes to excuse unsafe workloads. Effective prevention examines staffing, scheduling, autonomy, fairness, harassment and recovery time.

15. Work-life balance, wellbeing and mental health at work

Work-life balance concerns the relationship between work demands and other valued roles and activities. Boundaries may be affected by shift patterns, remote work, caregiving, commuting, economic insecurity and digital availability. Flexible work can improve autonomy but may also extend working hours or isolate employees. Wellbeing includes psychological, social and physical dimensions and should not be reduced to a wellness app or occasional awareness event. Policies need to be assessed by how they operate in practice and whether employees can use them without penalty.

Workplace mental-health support may include prevention, manager training, confidential assistance, reasonable adjustments, return-to-work planning and referral pathways. Managers are not therapists and should not diagnose employees. They can listen, clarify work-related support and direct people to appropriate services. Confidentiality must be respected, and health information should be handled only by authorised personnel for legitimate purposes. A supportive workplace reduces stigma while maintaining clear performance expectations and safe systems.

16. Diversity, equity, inclusion and organisational justice

Workforces include people with different identities, experiences, languages, abilities, ages, family responsibilities and routes into employment. Diversity describes difference; equity concerns fair access and removal of unjust barriers; inclusion concerns meaningful participation and belonging. Equality of treatment is not always sufficient when people face different barriers. Reasonable adjustments, accessible recruitment, transparent promotion and inclusive work design can improve fairness. Policies should be evaluated through employee experience and outcomes, not only statements of intent.

Organisational justice includes distributive justice (fairness of outcomes), procedural justice (fairness of decision processes), interpersonal justice (respectful treatment) and informational justice (adequate explanations). Discrimination and harassment can damage wellbeing and performance while creating legal and ethical liability. Psychologists should examine selection rates, pay, progression, discipline and access to development across relevant groups, while protecting privacy and interpreting differences carefully. Inclusion requires accountability and action when harmful behaviour occurs.

17. Change management and organisational development

Organisational change may involve technology, restructuring, leadership, mergers, new procedures, culture or service redesign. People may experience uncertainty, loss of control, workload changes and concern about competence or job security. Change management includes diagnosis, stakeholder analysis, communication, participation, implementation support and evaluation. Resistance should not automatically be treated as irrational: employees may identify risks that planners have overlooked. Meaningful consultation can improve design and legitimacy, although leaders remain responsible for making and explaining decisions.

Organisational development uses planned, evidence-informed processes to improve organisational functioning and human experience. Interventions may target teams, leadership, communication, workflow, culture or systems. A diagnosis should precede intervention, and evaluation should assess both intended outcomes and unintended effects. Sustainable change requires resources, role clarity, learning and reinforcement. Change fatigue occurs when repeated initiatives arrive without sufficient capacity or visible benefit. Leaders should prioritise, sequence and stop activities that no longer serve a defensible purpose.

18. Decision-making, judgement and organisational behaviour

Workplace decisions range from routine choices to complex judgements under uncertainty. Bounded rationality recognises that people have limited time, information and cognitive capacity, so they often use simplified strategies. Heuristics can be efficient but may produce predictable biases, including anchoring, availability and confirmation bias. Group decisions can pool knowledge but may also create groupthink, conformity or diffusion of responsibility. Good decision processes make assumptions visible, invite dissent and clarify who owns the final decision.

Decision quality can be improved through structured criteria, pre-mortems, independent estimates, checklists and review of outcomes. Data should inform rather than replace judgement: measures may be incomplete, biased or poorly matched to the question. Ethical decisions consider affected stakeholders, rights, fairness, foreseeable harm and longer-term consequences. Organisational psychologists may facilitate decision processes and evaluate interventions, but should disclose limitations and avoid presenting uncertain predictions as facts.

19. Work stress, resilience and coping

Work stress can emerge when demands are high and resources, control or support are inadequate. The job demands-resources model proposes that demands can contribute to strain while resources support motivation and buffer some effects. Resources include autonomy, supportive supervision, feedback, tools, staffing and opportunities to develop. Individual coping strategies such as planning, social support and recovery routines may help, but they cannot compensate indefinitely for unsafe work design. Assessment should distinguish short-term pressure from chronic exposure and consider the person's wider circumstances.

Resilience refers to processes that support adaptation in the face of adversity, not an obligation to tolerate harmful conditions. Programmes that teach coping can be useful when paired with organisational prevention. Managers should monitor workload, role conflict, incivility and signs of fatigue. Employees need safe routes to report hazards and access support without stigma. Return-to-work planning after illness should be collaborative, realistic and reviewed, with reasonable adjustments where appropriate.

20. Human factors, ergonomics and safety behaviour

Human factors examines interactions among people, tasks, equipment, technology and environments. Ergonomics seeks to fit work systems to human capabilities and limitations rather than expecting people to adapt to poorly designed systems. Relevant factors include posture, repetition, display design, workload, fatigue, alarms, interruptions and environmental conditions. In complex systems, accidents often emerge from multiple interacting weaknesses rather than one careless individual. A systems approach investigates design, training, supervision, procedures and organisational pressures.

Safety culture is reflected in whether hazards are reported, investigated and addressed. Blame-focused responses can suppress information, while a learning approach examines causes and accountability proportionately. Checklists, standard operating procedures and training can reduce error, but they must be usable and supported by adequate resources. Safety-critical work requires attention to fatigue, shift scheduling, communication and handover. Human factors specialists test systems with users and observe real work rather than relying solely on idealised procedures.

21. Remote work, technology and the future of employment

Digital technology changes task content, supervision, communication, skill requirements and boundaries between work and home. Remote and hybrid work can improve flexibility and reduce commuting, but may also create isolation, unequal visibility, blurred working hours and barriers to informal learning. Technology can automate routine tasks while increasing cognitive demands and monitoring. Organisations should assess the impact on autonomy, privacy, workload, collaboration and access to development. A technology deployment is also a work-design intervention and should include employee participation.

Algorithmic management and automated selection raise questions about transparency, validity, bias, explainability and appeal. Employee monitoring should be proportionate, lawful and clearly communicated. Data collected for one purpose should not be repurposed without a legitimate basis. AI tools may assist administrative tasks but can reproduce historical inequalities or produce inaccurate assessments. Human oversight, audit trails and accessible routes to challenge decisions are important, particularly when automated systems affect hiring, pay, promotion or discipline.

22. Research methods and measurement in organisations

Organisational psychologists use surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, experiments, quasi-experiments, archival data and mixed methods. The method should match the question. Surveys can estimate perceptions across a workforce but may suffer from non-response, social desirability and common-method bias. Interviews provide depth but require careful sampling and analysis. Observation reveals work as performed but can change behaviour or raise privacy concerns. Administrative data can show patterns, yet may reflect inconsistent definitions or missing information.

Measurement quality requires clear constructs, reliable instruments and evidence of validity in the population and context where they are used. A scale developed in one language or sector may not function equivalently elsewhere. Cross-sectional associations do not establish causation. Before-and-after evaluation may be affected by other changes occurring at the same time. Strong evaluation uses comparison where feasible, transparent analysis and attention to unintended consequences. Researchers should explain limitations and avoid overstating what the data show.

23. Organisational interventions and evaluation

Interventions may target individuals, teams, managers or whole systems. Examples include selection redesign, supervisor development, workflow improvement, team facilitation, safety interventions, job crafting and wellbeing initiatives. A logic model links resources and activities to immediate outputs, intermediate outcomes and longer-term impact. Evaluation begins by specifying what success means, for whom and over what period. It should include employee experience and possible harms, not just productivity or cost savings.

Implementation quality matters: an effective programme may fail if managers do not support it, employees cannot attend or the intervention is poorly adapted. Process evaluation asks whether the programme reached its intended group and was delivered as planned. Outcome evaluation examines change, while economic evaluation considers costs and benefits. Findings should inform adaptation, continuation or discontinuation. Organisations should avoid collecting sensitive data without clear purpose, adequate protection and transparent governance.

24. Ethics, confidentiality and professional competence

Organisational psychologists may work for an employer while also assessing or advising about individual employees. This creates role conflicts that must be explained before information is collected. The practitioner clarifies who the client is, who will receive reports, what confidentiality applies and how findings may be used. In selection or fitness-for-work assessments, the employee should understand the purpose, limits and recipients of information. Only relevant information should be disclosed, and conclusions should be proportionate to the evidence.

Ethical practice includes competence, informed consent, privacy, fairness, integrity, avoidance of harm and accountability. Psychologists should use validated methods, maintain secure records, seek supervision and disclose conflicts of interest. They should not make unsupported diagnoses or share personal details with managers simply because the organisation pays for the service. Professional standards and legal duties vary by jurisdiction; practitioners must know the applicable rules and seek specialist advice when uncertain.

25. Applied case study: diagnosing a performance problem

Consider a service team whose error rate has increased while managers report that employees appear disengaged. A simplistic response might introduce motivation workshops or performance warnings. A systematic organisational assessment first examines the pattern of errors, workload, staffing, software changes, training, task complexity, handovers, role clarity and employee feedback. Interviews suggest that a new system has increased duplicate data entry and reduced time for checking. Staff also report that targets were not adjusted after vacancies arose. The initial hypothesis therefore concerns work design and resources, not a general lack of commitment.

A proportionate intervention may simplify the workflow, restore adequate checking time, clarify responsibilities, provide task-specific training and involve employees in testing changes. Baseline measures should include error rates, workload, rework, staff experience and service quality. Follow-up data should examine whether improvement is sustained and whether work has merely shifted to another team. The case illustrates why organisational diagnosis should precede intervention and why employee voice is a source of operational evidence.

26. Applied case study: wellbeing and organisational change

A hospital department introduces new digital scheduling software. Employees report uncertainty, longer shifts and concern that the system allocates work unfairly. A wellbeing survey shows rising fatigue, but the response rate is low because staff fear that managers can identify individual answers. A sound assessment examines system configuration, staffing, training, shift patterns, privacy, communication and the credibility of survey safeguards. It also asks whether different roles or groups experience the change differently.

An improvement plan could include transparent data governance, employee-led testing, training during paid time, review of scheduling rules, fatigue controls and a confidential feedback route. Evaluation should measure schedule predictability, overtime, errors, fatigue and trust, while monitoring whether the intervention creates new burdens. Leaders should report what changed in response to feedback. This example demonstrates that wellbeing, technology, fairness and operational effectiveness are connected rather than separate organisational projects.

27. Professional roles, consultation and career pathways

Organisational psychologists may work as internal specialists, external consultants, researchers, human-resource advisers, assessment professionals, leadership developers or occupational-health collaborators. Their responsibilities may include job analysis, assessment design, organisational diagnosis, evaluation, coaching within competence, facilitation and policy advice. Some tasks require registration or protected titles in particular jurisdictions. Practitioners should distinguish psychological expertise from general management advice and should not claim competence in clinical treatment, legal interpretation or medical assessment without appropriate qualifications.

Consultancy requires contracting, stakeholder analysis, confidentiality agreements, clear deliverables and management of conflicts of interest. The psychologist should be willing to challenge a client's request when it is discriminatory, unsafe or unsupported by evidence. Career development involves supervised experience, continuing education, research literacy, professional networks and reflective practice. The ability to communicate findings to both technical and non-technical audiences is essential, as is the courage to explain uncertainty and limits.

28. Integrating organisational effectiveness with decent work

A mature organisational psychology approach evaluates performance and human consequences together. High output achieved through unsafe conditions, exclusion or chronic overwork is not sustainable effectiveness. Decent work includes fair opportunity, safe conditions, reasonable treatment, meaningful participation and respect for rights. Organisational psychologists can contribute by designing valid assessment, improving job quality, strengthening leadership, evaluating change and ensuring employee voice informs decisions. They must also recognise the limits of their influence when organisational incentives conflict with employee welfare.

The discipline is most valuable when it combines rigorous evidence with practical humility. It asks whether the problem has been correctly defined, whether the data represent the people affected, whether the intervention addresses causes and whether outcomes are monitored transparently. Ethical practice requires attention to power, privacy, fairness and unintended effects. The goal is not merely to make people fit organisations, but to help create work systems in which people and organisations can function effectively and responsibly.

Structured university examination questions and model answers

Strong examination responses define concepts accurately, compare theories, apply evidence to a workplace example and evaluate ethical or methodological limitations.

1. Define organisational psychology.

Model answer: The scientific study and application of psychological principles to people, teams and systems at work, including performance, wellbeing, leadership, selection and organisational change.

2. Distinguish individual, team and organisational levels of analysis.

Model answer: Individual analysis examines person-level factors; team analysis examines interaction and coordination; organisational analysis examines structure, culture, policies and systems. They influence one another.

3. What is job analysis?

Model answer: A systematic process for identifying job tasks, responsibilities, context and the competencies required for effective performance.

4. Explain the five job characteristics in the job characteristics model.

Model answer: Skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback are job features associated with meaningfulness, responsibility and knowledge of results.

5. Why should personality tests not be used alone in selection?

Model answer: A score is an incomplete and context-dependent measure. Selection should use job-related, validated and fair methods alongside other evidence.

6. Differentiate recruitment and selection.

Model answer: Recruitment attracts applicants; selection evaluates evidence against job-related criteria to make an appointment decision.

7. Explain criterion-related validity.

Model answer: It concerns the extent to which assessment scores are associated with or predict relevant work criteria, such as later job performance.

8. Describe the three levels of training needs analysis.

Model answer: Organisational analysis identifies strategic needs; task analysis identifies job competencies; individual analysis identifies who needs which learning.

9. Summarise expectancy theory of motivation.

Model answer: Motivation depends on beliefs that effort can produce performance, performance can lead to outcomes and those outcomes are valued.

10. Distinguish job satisfaction from organisational commitment.

Model answer: Satisfaction evaluates aspects of the job; commitment describes the employee's psychological relationship with the organisation.

11. Compare transformational and transactional leadership.

Model answer: Transformational leadership develops shared purpose and motivation; transactional leadership clarifies expectations and links performance to rewards or corrective action.

12. What is psychological safety in a team?

Model answer: A shared belief that members can ask questions, raise concerns and admit mistakes without interpersonal punishment, while standards and accountability remain.

13. Differentiate task conflict and relationship conflict.

Model answer: Task conflict concerns ideas or work content and can sometimes improve decisions; relationship conflict is personal and commonly undermines trust.

14. Distinguish organisational culture from climate.

Model answer: Culture concerns deeper shared assumptions and values; climate concerns shared perceptions of current policies, practices and behaviours.

15. Identify three common performance-rating biases.

Model answer: Halo effect, recency bias, similarity bias, leniency and central-tendency bias are examples.

16. What is a psychosocial hazard?

Model answer: A feature of work organisation or relationships, such as excessive demands, low control, bullying or role conflict, that can adversely affect health.

17. Why is resilience training insufficient as the sole response to burnout risk?

Model answer: It does not remove structural hazards such as understaffing, unsafe workloads or harassment; prevention must address work design and conditions.

18. Explain organisational justice.

Model answer: Perceived fairness of outcomes, decision procedures, interpersonal treatment and explanations or information.

19. What is organisational development?

Model answer: A planned, evidence-informed process for improving organisational functioning through diagnosis, participation, intervention and evaluation.

20. Give two ways to reduce group decision bias.

Model answer: Invite dissent, obtain independent estimates, use structured criteria, conduct a pre-mortem or assign a critical reviewer.

21. Explain the job demands-resources model.

Model answer: Job demands can contribute to strain, while resources such as autonomy, support and feedback support motivation and may buffer demands.

22. What is human factors engineering?

Model answer: The study and design of interactions among people, tasks, tools, technology and environments to improve usability, safety and performance.

23. Identify two ethical issues in algorithmic recruitment.

Model answer: Potential bias and lack of transparency or appeal; also privacy, validity and inappropriate reuse of applicant data.

24. Why does a before-and-after evaluation not always establish causation?

Model answer: Other events or changes may explain the observed difference, and there may be no suitable comparison group.

25. What should be clarified before an organisational assessment of an employee?

Model answer: Who the client is, the purpose, consent, confidentiality limits, report recipients and how information may be used.

26. How should a performance problem be diagnosed before training is prescribed?

Model answer: Examine tasks, systems, workload, resources, role clarity, incentives and employee capability to determine whether a genuine learning gap exists.

27. Give two indicators for evaluating a wellbeing intervention.

Model answer: Examples include fatigue, absence, turnover, workload, psychological safety, access to support and employee-reported wellbeing.

28. Explain why employee participation matters in organisational change.

Model answer: Employees understand real work and can identify risks, improve design, strengthen legitimacy and support implementation.

29. What is the central ethical aim of organisational psychology?

Model answer: To use psychological knowledge competently and fairly while protecting dignity, privacy, safety and wellbeing and supporting responsible organisational effectiveness.

Chapter synthesis and revision framework

Organisational psychology links individual behaviour to teams, structures and wider environments. Job analysis informs work design and fair selection; training should respond to diagnosed capability gaps; motivation and leadership operate within systems of resources and justice; and wellbeing depends on both individual support and prevention of workplace hazards. Research methods help evaluate these processes, but measurement must be valid, transparent and appropriate to the population.

When asked to solve an organisational problem, define the outcome, gather evidence from multiple levels, involve affected employees, identify root causes, select a proportionate intervention and monitor intended and unintended effects. Ethical practice requires competence, confidentiality, fairness, privacy and attention to power. Sustainable effectiveness depends on decent work and organisational accountability, not productivity at any cost.

Revision sequence: scope and systems → job analysis and work design → individual differences and selection → learning and motivation → leadership and teams → culture and performance → safety and wellbeing → diversity and change → research and evaluation → ethics and professional practice.
