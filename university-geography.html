Diploma Geography: foundations and applied skills

Diploma study establishes the language of geography and the practical ability to observe, map and explain places and environments.

Module 1 — Earth systems, location and map skills

Earth is an oblate spheroid with a layered interior: crust, mantle and core. Latitude measures angular distance north or south of the Equator; longitude measures east or west of the prime meridian. Scale relates map distance to ground distance. A representative fraction of 1:50,000 means one unit on the map represents 50,000 of the same units on the ground.

Worked scale example: A road measures 6 cm on a 1:50,000 map. Ground distance = 6 × 50,000 cm = 300,000 cm = 3,000 m = 3 km. Always convert units after applying the scale.

Module 2 — Weather and climate

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions; climate describes statistical patterns, usually assessed over a standard period such as 30 years. Elements include temperature, rainfall, humidity, pressure, wind and cloud cover. Controls include latitude, altitude, continentality, ocean currents, relief and prevailing winds. Orographic rainfall occurs when moist air rises over relief, cools and condenses; a rain shadow may form on the leeward side.

Moist air approaches mountain → rises and expands → cools to dew point → condensation and windward rain → descending leeward air warms and dries

Module 3 — Geomorphology and hydrology

Weathering breaks down rock in place (physical, chemical and biological); erosion removes and transports material. Rivers erode vertically and laterally, transport sediment and deposit it when energy falls. Drainage-basin processes include interception, infiltration, surface runoff, throughflow, groundwater flow and channel discharge. Discharge is Q = A × V, where A is cross-sectional area in m² and V is mean velocity in m/s, giving m³/s.

Worked discharge: If a channel cross-section is 12 m² and mean velocity is 1.5 m/s, Q = 12 × 1.5 = 18 m³/s.

Module 4 — Population and settlement

Population geography examines distribution, density, fertility, mortality, migration and demographic change. Crude birth rate = (live births ÷ mid-year population) × 1,000. Net migration is immigration minus emigration. Settlement patterns are shaped by water, relief, transport, employment, security, policy and historical land relations.

Module 5 — Resources, hazards and sustainability

Distinguish renewable flows from non-renewable stocks, while recognising that renewable resources can be depleted if extraction exceeds regeneration. Hazard becomes disaster when an extreme event interacts with exposure, vulnerability and limited capacity. Risk reduction includes land-use planning, early warning, resilient infrastructure, ecosystem protection and inclusive preparedness.

Undergraduate Geography: theory and analytical frameworks

Physical geography

Systems theory: landscapes and environments can be analysed as systems with inputs, stores, transfers and outputs. Feedback may be positive (amplifying change) or negative (dampening change). Plate tectonics: lithospheric plates move over the asthenosphere; divergent boundaries create crust, convergent boundaries involve subduction or collision, and transform boundaries accommodate lateral motion. Climate system: energy balance, atmospheric circulation, ocean-atmosphere coupling and land-surface feedback shape climate patterns.

Human geography

Central Place Theory (Christaller): explains idealised settlement hierarchies and service areas under assumptions about a uniform plain, evenly distributed population and rational consumers. Real-world transport, terrain, history and inequality complicate the hexagonal model. Von Thünen's land-use model: predicts concentric agricultural land-use zones around a market under simplified assumptions about transport costs, land rent and uniform conditions. Bid-rent theory: explains how competing land users' willingness to pay varies with accessibility and distance from a centre.

CBD / central market → high-accessibility uses and high bid rents → progressively lower bid rents with distance (idealised; actual cities are polycentric)

Population transition and migration

The demographic transition model describes a historical pattern in which mortality generally declines before fertility, producing phases of rapid growth and later stabilisation. It is a model, not a universal law: timing and pathways differ with public health, policy, conflict, gender relations, education and economic change. Push-pull frameworks organise migration factors but should not obscure networks, legal constraints, household strategies and forced displacement.

Development and political ecology

Modernisation approaches emphasise structural transformation, productivity and institutional change; dependency and world-systems approaches stress unequal incorporation into global economic structures. Sustainable development links social wellbeing, economic activity and ecological integrity. Political ecology asks how power, access, knowledge and distribution shape environmental change and resource conflict.

GIS and remote sensing

GIS captures, stores, analyses and visualises geographically referenced data. Raster data represent continuous surfaces or gridded observations; vector data represent points, lines and polygons. Remote sensing derives information from reflected or emitted energy measured by sensors. Spatial analysis includes buffering, overlay, network analysis, interpolation and suitability modelling. Coordinate reference systems and map projections must be selected carefully because every projection distorts some properties.

Postgraduate Geography: advanced research and critical evaluation

Spatial statistics and spatial dependence

Spatial observations are often not independent. Tobler's First Law states that, all else equal, nearer things tend to be more related than distant things. Global Moran's I tests spatial autocorrelation; local indicators identify clusters and spatial outliers. Results depend on the spatial weights matrix, scale, zoning and data quality. The modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) means patterns can change when boundaries or aggregation levels change.

Climate risk and adaptation

Risk frameworks commonly connect hazard, exposure and vulnerability, with capacity shaping consequences. Climate attribution distinguishes long-term forced trends from natural variability. Adaptation evaluation should examine effectiveness, distributional justice, maladaptation, uncertainty, time horizons and local knowledge—not only aggregate economic benefit.

Political ecology, resilience and environmental governance

Resilience concerns the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance, reorganise and retain essential functions; it is not automatically socially desirable if an inequitable system is being preserved. Common-pool resource analysis, including Ostrom's work on collective action, examines rules, monitoring, sanctions, trust and institutional fit. Apply theories contextually rather than assuming a single governance model works everywhere.

Advanced research design

Formulate a spatially explicit research question, justify scale and case selection, define units of analysis, assess data provenance, select field/GIS/statistical methods and pre-specify limitations. Mixed-method research may combine satellite imagery, administrative datasets, interviews, participatory mapping and field observations. Address consent, community data sovereignty, sensitive coordinates and risks of exposing vulnerable groups.

Critical methods questions

  • Does the chosen spatial scale match the process being studied?
  • Could spatial autocorrelation invalidate conventional regression standard errors?
  • How do classification choices, missing data and map design affect interpretation?
  • Who benefits from a proposed environmental intervention, and who bears its costs?

Methods, diagrams and worked applications

Map and spatial-data workflow

Research question → choose scale and coordinate reference system → acquire and document data → clean and validate → analyse → map with legend, scale and source → discuss uncertainty

For a choropleth map, use rates or standardised values rather than raw counts when comparing areas with different populations. Select a classification method deliberately (equal interval, quantile or natural breaks) and state it. Avoid implying precision unsupported by the data.

Fieldwork sampling

Systematic sampling uses regular intervals; stratified sampling divides a population into meaningful groups before sampling; random sampling gives units a known selection probability; purposive sampling selects information-rich cases for qualitative research. Explain the sampling frame, non-response and possible bias.

Worked application: population density

A district has 480,000 residents and an area of 1,200 km². Density = 480,000 ÷ 1,200 = 400 persons per km². This average conceals internal variation, so map sub-district patterns before drawing conclusions.

Indicative marking guidance

Q2: 8 cm × 25,000 = 200,000 cm = 2 km. For essays, award marks for accurate theory, appropriate evidence, analytical comparison, critical evaluation, labelled diagrams and a justified conclusion. Research-design answers must align question, scale, data and method and identify limitations.

Self-assessment: A strong answer defines key concepts, applies appropriate theory, uses relevant examples or evidence, explains limitations and reaches a reasoned conclusion. Allocate marks to knowledge, application, analysis and communication rather than memorised phrases alone.

Further learning and integrity

Use your institution's library catalogue, peer-reviewed journals, official statistical agencies, national mapping or meteorological services, and reputable international datasets. Verify publication dates, definitions, geographical coverage and methods before using data. Never invent references, statistics, field observations or examination provenance.

Chapter 1 — Geography as an integrative discipline

1.1 Meaning, scope and intellectual traditions

Geography investigates the location, distribution, interaction and changing character of physical and human phenomena across Earth's surface. It asks not only where something occurs, but why it occurs there, how it is connected to other places, who benefits from the arrangement, and how the pattern changes through time. Its integrative character arises because social and environmental processes are inseparable in many real situations. Urban flooding, for example, involves rainfall intensity and catchment hydrology, but also drainage infrastructure, land markets, planning decisions, household income, public health and political accountability.

Physical geography studies natural systems and processes, including climate, landforms, soils, water, ecosystems and biogeography. Human geography examines population, culture, economy, politics, urbanisation, mobility and social difference. Environmental geography connects these fields by examining coupled human–environment systems. Geographical thought also includes regional geography, which synthesises the distinctive characteristics and connections of places, and systematic geography, which studies particular themes across multiple locations.

Geographical questions operate at multiple scales. A household may experience water insecurity at the local scale; a river basin links upstream and downstream communities; national policy determines infrastructure investment; and global climate processes influence rainfall probabilities. Scale is therefore analytical, not merely cartographic. A conclusion reached at one scale may not hold at another. Researchers should state the scale of observation and explain why it is suitable for the question.

1.2 Space, place, location and region

Location identifies where something is, using absolute coordinates or relative descriptions. Space refers to the arrangements, distances, networks and relationships through which phenomena are organised. Place adds meaning, identity, memory and lived experience to a location. Region is an area grouped by selected characteristics: a formal region may share a measurable attribute, a functional region may be organised around a node and its flows, while a perceptual region reflects how people understand an area. Regions are analytical constructions; their boundaries depend on the purpose and criteria used.

Distance can be measured as straight-line distance, network distance, travel time, cost or social separation. A school may be only two kilometres from a settlement in Euclidean terms yet difficult to reach because a river, steep slope or unsafe road interrupts access. Accessibility analysis should therefore use a measure that reflects the actual process being studied. Transport planners may model travel time; public-health researchers may consider walking routes, affordability and service quality.

1.3 Approaches to geographical explanation

Descriptive geography identifies and characterises patterns. Explanatory geography develops accounts of the processes that produce them. Interpretive approaches examine meanings, identities and lived experiences. Critical approaches ask how power, inequality and institutions shape spatial outcomes. Quantitative methods identify measurable relationships, while qualitative methods investigate experience, practice and meaning. These approaches are not mutually exclusive. A strong research design selects methods because they answer the research question, not because one method is fashionable.

Environmental determinism historically claimed that physical environments directly determine human societies. It is now widely criticised for oversimplification, colonial assumptions and failure to account for institutions, technology, history and human agency. Possibilist and political-ecological approaches instead examine how environments create opportunities and constraints that people negotiate under unequal conditions. Avoid statements that attribute social outcomes to climate or terrain alone.

Chapter 2 — Earth's structure, tectonics and landform development

2.1 Internal structure and heat

Earth's interior is commonly described in terms of the crust, mantle and core. The crust is the thin outer rocky layer; continental crust is generally thicker and less dense than oceanic crust. The mantle is predominantly solid rock that can deform and flow over geological timescales. The core is mainly metallic, with a liquid outer core and solid inner core. Internal heat derives from primordial formation and continuing radioactive decay. Heat transfer and mantle dynamics contribute to the movement of lithospheric plates.

The lithosphere comprises the crust and rigid uppermost mantle. It is divided into plates that move relative to one another above the weaker, ductile asthenosphere. Plate motion is associated with slab pull, ridge push and mantle processes; no single simple force explains every boundary equally. Plate tectonics provides a unifying framework for explaining the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, ocean basins, mountain belts and many large-scale landforms.

2.2 Plate boundaries and their products

At divergent boundaries, plates move apart. Upwelling mantle material partially melts as pressure falls, producing new oceanic crust at mid-ocean ridges. Continental rifting may create fault-bounded valleys and, if extension continues, a new ocean basin. At convergent boundaries, plates move together. Dense oceanic lithosphere may descend beneath another plate, forming a subduction zone, trench, earthquakes and volcanic arc. Where buoyant continental crust collides with continental crust, compression, folding, thrust faulting and crustal thickening can create major mountain belts. At transform boundaries, plates slide laterally past one another; frictional locking and sudden rupture generate earthquakes.

Conceptual plate-boundary comparison
Divergent: plate ← | rising mantle | → plate — new crust
Convergent: oceanic plate → beneath overriding plate — trench, earthquakes, possible volcanic arc
Transform: plate ↑ alongside plate ↓ — strike-slip fault and shallow earthquakes

Earthquakes occur when accumulated stress is released along faults. The focus or hypocentre is the point where rupture begins; the epicentre is the surface location directly above it. Seismic waves include body waves that travel through Earth's interior and surface waves that travel along the surface. Magnitude measures earthquake size using instrumental estimates, whereas intensity describes observed effects at a location. These terms should not be confused in hazard reports.

2.3 Weathering, mass movement and slope evolution

Weathering is the in-place breakdown or alteration of rock. Mechanical weathering includes freeze–thaw action, thermal expansion, salt crystallisation and pressure release. Chemical weathering includes hydrolysis, oxidation, carbonation and solution. Biological weathering occurs through roots, burrowing organisms and organic acids. Rates depend on mineral composition, climate, relief, vegetation, drainage and exposure time. Weathering supplies material for soils and sediment transport.

Mass movement is the downslope movement of rock, soil or debris under gravity. It includes falls, slides, flows and slow creep. A slope may fail when driving stresses exceed resisting forces. Water can increase mass by adding weight, raise pore-water pressure and reduce effective stress; vegetation roots may provide reinforcement, while removal of vegetation or undercutting can increase instability. Risk management should combine hazard mapping, drainage control, land-use planning, engineering where justified, monitoring and community preparedness. Avoid assuming that every steep slope is equally unstable.

2.4 Fluvial landforms

Rivers shape landscapes through erosion, transport and deposition. Hydraulic action dislodges material through the force of moving water; abrasion occurs when transported particles wear the bed and banks; attrition makes particles collide and become smaller and rounder; solution transports dissolved minerals. Transport occurs as dissolved load, suspended load, saltation and traction. Deposition is more likely when velocity and competence decline, although channel geometry, sediment supply and turbulence also matter.

In upper courses, steep gradients and confined valleys often favour vertical incision, rapids and waterfalls. Downstream, lateral erosion and deposition may produce meanders, floodplains, levees and oxbow lakes. These are tendencies rather than rigid rules: geology, discharge variability, sediment supply, vegetation and human engineering alter river form. Floodplains are productive but expose people and infrastructure to flood risk. Flood management should consider the entire catchment rather than treating a single channel reach in isolation.

Chapter 3 — Atmosphere, climate processes and climate change

3.1 Atmospheric structure and energy balance

The atmosphere is a mixture of gases, dominated by nitrogen and oxygen, with variable water vapour and smaller concentrations of other gases and aerosols. The troposphere contains most weather phenomena and generally cools with height, although inversions can occur. Above it, the stratosphere contains the ozone layer and has a temperature structure influenced by ultraviolet absorption. Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude because less air lies above a given point.

Earth's climate is shaped by incoming shortwave solar radiation and outgoing longwave terrestrial radiation. Some incoming energy is reflected by clouds, aerosols, ice and bright surfaces; the remainder is absorbed by the atmosphere and surface. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit portions of outgoing infrared radiation, warming the lower atmosphere and surface relative to a planet without this natural effect. Human emissions increase concentrations of greenhouse gases and strengthen the greenhouse effect, creating an energy imbalance and long-term warming.

3.2 Global circulation and rainfall

Unequal solar heating between equator and poles, Earth's rotation and the distribution of land and ocean help organise atmospheric circulation. The Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells are idealised circulation zones. Rising air near the equator is associated with low pressure and frequent convection; descending air in subtropical belts contributes to many dry regions. Mid-latitude weather is influenced by westerlies, fronts and travelling depressions. Seasonal shifts in pressure belts and monsoon circulations are important in many tropical and subtropical regions.

Rainfall forms when air is cooled to saturation and water vapour condenses on cloud condensation nuclei. Convectional rainfall occurs when surface heating causes moist air to rise. Orographic rainfall occurs as air is forced over relief. Frontal rainfall occurs where air masses meet and warmer air is lifted over colder, denser air. Rainfall amount, intensity, duration and spatial distribution are distinct variables; each has implications for agriculture, water supply and flood risk.

3.3 Climate classification and its limitations

Climate classification systems group locations according to selected temperature and precipitation characteristics. Köppen–Geiger classification is widely used because it relates climatic patterns to vegetation zones, but it simplifies continuous variation and can be sensitive to thresholds and the reference period. A classification label should not replace analysis of seasonal rainfall, extremes, microclimates, elevation or local livelihoods. Climate normals describe long-term averages, while variability and extremes describe departures and distributions that averages can hide.

3.4 Climate change, impacts and adaptation

Climate change refers to persistent changes in the mean state or variability of climate, often assessed over decades or longer. Evidence includes instrumental temperature records, ocean heat content, glacier and ice-sheet mass loss, sea-level rise, changes in phenology and multiple independent proxy records. Attribution assesses the contribution of different drivers, including greenhouse gases, aerosols, land-use change and natural variability. A sound explanation distinguishes observed trends, projected scenarios and uncertainty ranges.

Impacts are mediated by exposure and vulnerability. The same rainfall event can have very different consequences depending on drainage, housing quality, warning systems, health, income and institutional capacity. Adaptation options include climate-informed infrastructure, water conservation, ecosystem restoration, heat-health planning, crop diversification and early warning. Mitigation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions or increases removals. Some responses can create maladaptation if they transfer risk, deepen inequality or lock societies into vulnerable development pathways.

Chapter 4 — Hydrological systems, soils and ecosystems

4.1 The drainage basin as an open system

A drainage basin is an area from which water drains to a common outlet. Its boundary is the watershed or drainage divide. Precipitation is an input; interception, soil moisture, groundwater and channel storage are stores; infiltration, percolation, throughflow, groundwater flow and channel flow are transfers; evapotranspiration and river discharge are outputs. A simplified water balance is P = Q + ET + ΔS, where P is precipitation, Q is runoff or discharge expressed as an equivalent depth over the basin, ET is evapotranspiration and ΔS is change in storage over the chosen period. Units and time periods must be consistent.

Infiltration capacity depends on soil texture, structure, antecedent moisture, vegetation, compaction and rainfall intensity. If rainfall intensity exceeds infiltration capacity, infiltration-excess overland flow may occur. Saturation-excess runoff occurs when soil becomes saturated and additional water flows over the surface. Urbanisation often increases impermeable cover and can accelerate runoff, but the effect varies with drainage design, soil conditions and catchment form.

4.2 Soils and soil conservation

Soil is a dynamic body of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and organisms. Soil formation is influenced by parent material, climate, organisms, relief, time and human activity. A soil profile may contain organic and mineral horizons that differ in colour, structure, texture and chemical properties. Texture refers to the proportions of sand, silt and clay; structure refers to how particles aggregate. Soil pH influences nutrient availability and biological activity. Soil degradation includes erosion, salinisation, compaction, contamination and loss of organic matter.

Conservation approaches include contour cultivation, terracing, cover crops, mulching, agroforestry, controlled grazing, riparian buffers and maintenance of soil organic matter. Measures should fit local slope, rainfall, farming systems, tenure and labour availability. A technically sound intervention may fail if land users cannot afford it, lack secure rights or were excluded from planning.

4.3 Ecosystems, biomes and biodiversity

An ecosystem includes organisms and their physical environment, linked by energy flows and nutrient cycling. Producers convert energy, consumers obtain energy by feeding, and decomposers recycle nutrients. Food chains simplify feeding relationships; food webs show their complexity. Primary productivity describes the rate at which producers store energy as biomass. Biomes are broad ecological regions shaped by climate, soils, disturbance and evolutionary history. Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity and contributes to ecosystem functions and human wellbeing.

Conservation strategies include protected areas, ecological corridors, community conservation, restoration, sustainable harvesting and control of invasive species. Conservation outcomes depend on governance, land rights, enforcement, financing and distribution of benefits. Evaluate whether interventions respect local communities and whether monitoring measures ecological as well as social outcomes.

Chapter 5 — Population, migration, urbanisation and regional development

5.1 Population measures and demographic change

Population geography analyses population size, structure, distribution and change. Population density is a ratio of population to area, but arithmetic density may hide concentrations and sparsely inhabited zones. Physiological density compares population with cultivable land; agricultural density compares farmers with agricultural land. Age-sex pyramids show population structure and can indicate past fertility, mortality, migration and conflict patterns. Demographic indicators require clear denominators and reference periods.

Fertility is influenced by education, income, child survival, access to reproductive health services, gender relations, urbanisation, cultural norms and policy. Mortality reflects disease environments, nutrition, sanitation, healthcare, conflict and living conditions. Migration may be internal or international, voluntary or forced, temporary or permanent. A push-pull framework can organise reasons for movement, but migration decisions are also shaped by social networks, resources, legal categories, family strategies and unequal mobility rights.

5.2 Demographic transition: application and critique

The demographic transition model describes a broad sequence in which death rates often decline before birth rates, producing rapid population growth, followed in many settings by fertility decline and slower growth. The model is useful as a comparative heuristic, but it should not be treated as a universal prediction. Countries may experience reversals due to conflict, epidemics, policy shifts or economic shocks. Some populations have experienced low fertility, ageing and migration-driven change; others continue to experience youthful age structures and rapid growth. Explain the historical and institutional context rather than assigning a stage mechanically.

5.3 Urbanisation and urban systems

Urbanisation is an increase in the proportion of a population living in urban areas; urban growth is an increase in the number of urban residents. They are related but not identical. Urban growth can result from natural increase, rural–urban migration, international migration, reclassification and boundary change. Urban systems include settlements linked by flows of people, goods, capital, information and services. Primate-city patterns, rank-size distributions and polycentric regions describe different settlement hierarchies.

Urban land use reflects accessibility, land values, planning, infrastructure, history and social power. Central Place Theory models the spatial distribution of service centres under restrictive assumptions. Bid-rent models explain how competing users value accessibility differently. Contemporary cities often have multiple centres, informal economies, fragmented governance and unequal access to services, so classical models are best used as starting points for critical comparison rather than literal maps.

5.4 Regional inequality and development

Regional development is uneven because investment, infrastructure, skills, institutions, resource endowments and political influence are unevenly distributed. Cumulative causation suggests that early advantages can attract further investment and skilled labour, reinforcing spatial concentration. Growth-pole approaches seek to stimulate wider development through investment in selected centres, but spillovers are not automatic. Core–periphery analysis examines how economic and political relations can concentrate decision-making and value in core areas while peripheral regions supply labour or resources.

Development should be assessed using multiple dimensions. Income measures are useful but do not fully capture health, education, security, political participation, environmental quality or distribution. Human development approaches broaden the focus to people's capabilities and freedoms. Sustainable development asks whether present wellbeing can be improved without undermining ecological systems and the opportunities of future generations. Disaggregate indicators by gender, age, disability, location and income where data permit.

Chapter 6 — GIS, cartography, remote sensing and spatial analysis

6.1 Geographic information and data quality

Geographic information combines a location reference with attributes describing the feature or observation. Data may be primary, collected for the research question, or secondary, collected previously for another purpose. Metadata should document source, date, coordinate reference system, resolution, scale, classification, processing history, licensing and known limitations. Positional accuracy, attribute accuracy, completeness, consistency and temporal currency are distinct dimensions of data quality.

Vector models represent discrete objects as points, lines and polygons. Raster models represent space as a grid of cells and are suitable for continuous surfaces or imagery. Resolution is the ground area represented by a cell; finer resolution does not automatically mean more accurate information. Mixed-resolution layers can create false precision. Topology describes spatial relationships such as connectivity, adjacency and containment and is important in network and boundary analysis.

6.2 Coordinate systems and map projections

Geographic coordinates use latitude and longitude on a reference ellipsoid. Projected coordinate systems transform the curved Earth onto a flat plane. Every map projection distorts some combination of area, shape, distance or direction. Equal-area projections preserve area; conformal projections preserve local angles; equidistant projections preserve selected distances; azimuthal projections preserve directions from a selected centre. Select a projection appropriate to the geographical extent and analytical task. Combining layers in incompatible coordinate systems without proper transformation can produce spatial misalignment.

6.3 Spatial analysis operations

Buffering creates zones at a specified distance from features. Overlay combines layers to identify intersections or relationships. Network analysis models movement along connected routes and can calculate shortest paths, service areas or travel times. Interpolation estimates values at unsampled locations using assumptions about spatial continuity. Suitability analysis combines criteria such as slope, land cover, distance to roads and hazard exposure. Weighting criteria is a normative decision and should be justified, tested and reported transparently.

6.4 Remote sensing and image interpretation

Passive sensors measure naturally reflected or emitted energy; active sensors transmit energy and measure its return, as in radar. Spectral bands capture different wavelength regions. Vegetation indices such as NDVI use red and near-infrared reflectance to indicate vegetation greenness, but interpretation is affected by soil background, atmospheric conditions, canopy structure and season. Image classification may be supervised or unsupervised. Accuracy assessment requires reference data and a confusion matrix; overall accuracy alone can conceal poor performance for minority classes. Report class-specific producer's and user's accuracy where possible.

6.5 Cartographic communication

A map is an argument as well as a display. A good map has a clear purpose, appropriate scale, legible symbols, meaningful legend, north or orientation information where useful, scale bar, source and date. Choropleth maps should normally display standardised rates rather than absolute counts. Sequential colour schemes suit ordered quantities; diverging schemes suit values around a meaningful midpoint; qualitative schemes distinguish categories. Avoid truncated or misleading legends and unnecessary three-dimensional effects.

Chapter 7 — Field methods, research design and academic writing

7.1 From topic to researchable question

A research topic is broad; a research question specifies the relationship, process, population, place and time to be investigated. A good question is answerable with available evidence, conceptually clear, ethically acceptable and appropriately bounded. Objectives should use observable verbs such as measure, compare, map, explain, evaluate or interpret. A conceptual framework identifies the main concepts and proposed relationships. Hypotheses are appropriate where a testable expectation can be stated, but not every qualitative study requires a hypothesis.

7.2 Sampling and measurement

Probability sampling includes simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling. It supports statistical inference when the sampling frame and selection process are sound. Non-probability sampling includes purposive, convenience, quota and snowball approaches. It can be appropriate for exploratory or hard-to-reach populations but requires careful discussion of selection bias. Measurement should be valid for the concept, reliable across repeated observations and sensitive to context. Pilot instruments before full deployment.

7.3 Interviews, observation and participatory mapping

Semi-structured interviews use a topic guide while allowing participants to explain experiences in their own terms. Focus groups can reveal shared norms and disagreements, but power relations may silence some participants. Observation records practices and spatial behaviour; researchers should distinguish what was directly observed from their interpretation. Participatory mapping can document local knowledge and contested boundaries, but maps may expose sensitive information or imply that one group's account is universally accepted. Obtain informed consent and agree how outputs will be stored, shared and anonymised.

7.4 Quantitative analysis and interpretation

Descriptive statistics summarise distributions using measures such as mean, median, mode, range and standard deviation. Correlation measures association, not causation. Regression estimates relationships conditional on model specification and assumptions. Omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error, selection and spatial dependence can bias interpretation. Report sample size, variable definitions, uncertainty and diagnostics. A statistically significant coefficient may be substantively small; effect size and context matter.

7.5 Writing and referencing

A geographical report commonly includes an introduction, literature review, conceptual framework, study area, methods, results, discussion, conclusion, references and appendices. Separate findings from interpretation where appropriate. Maps and tables should have numbered captions, units, sources and explanatory text. Paraphrase accurately and cite the original source; direct quotations should be limited and clearly marked. Never fabricate a source, dataset, interview or field observation. Explain limitations honestly and state how they affect conclusions.

Extended applied case study — Urban flood risk as a coupled system

Consider a rapidly growing city where intense rainfall causes repeated flooding in low-lying neighbourhoods. A physical explanation may identify short-duration rainfall, saturated soils, limited channel capacity and high runoff. A land-use analysis may show that wetlands and floodplains have been converted to housing or roads. A political-economic explanation may examine why lower-income households occupy hazardous land, how land tenure and housing markets operate, and whether drainage investment is distributed equitably. A governance analysis may examine coordination between municipal authorities, water agencies, emergency services and residents.

A robust study would first define the hazard and outcome: for example, flood depth, duration, frequency, property damage or disruption to access. It would compile rainfall records, elevation data, drainage networks, land-cover imagery, historical flood reports and household survey data. GIS could delineate catchments, map impervious surfaces, estimate flow paths and identify exposed assets. Interviews could investigate warning access, evacuation constraints, informal drainage maintenance and local knowledge. Statistical models could examine associations between exposure and reported impacts, while acknowledging measurement error and spatial dependence.

Results should not equate mapped exposure with individual vulnerability. Two households at similar flood depth may experience different losses because of building quality, savings, insurance, health, household composition, social networks and access to transport. The final recommendations should distinguish structural measures, nature-based measures, planning controls, warning systems, social protection and relocation where unavoidable. Relocation requires due process, compensation, livelihood protection and participation; otherwise a risk-reduction project may transfer harm to already disadvantaged residents.

Case study tasks: (1) Draw a systems diagram showing rainfall, land cover, runoff, drainage, exposure and vulnerability. (2) Propose three spatial datasets and explain their limitations. (3) Design a sampling strategy for household interviews. (4) Recommend two interventions and identify possible unintended consequences. (5) Explain how you would evaluate whether flood risk has genuinely declined.
