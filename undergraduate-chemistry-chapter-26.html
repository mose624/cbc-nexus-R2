Detailed interpretation of every chapter diagram

Figure 26.1: Carbon cycle and greenhouse effect

How to interpret it: Carbon exchanges among atmosphere, ocean, organisms, soil and geological reservoirs. Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation at characteristic frequencies.

Laboratory connection: Measure pH, alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon to evaluate carbonate chemistry in aquatic samples.

Figure 26.2: Ozone formation and depletion

How to interpret it: O₂ photolysis generates O atoms that combine with O₂ to form O₃. Catalytic radical cycles convert ozone back to oxygen without consuming the catalyst overall.

Laboratory connection: Use validated UV absorption methods and interpret ozone measurements with altitude and meteorological conditions.

Figure 26.3: Water treatment process train

How to interpret it: Coagulation destabilises colloids; flocculation forms aggregates; sedimentation removes settleable solids; filtration removes particles; disinfection inactivates pathogens.

Laboratory connection: Measure turbidity, pH, residual disinfectant and microbial indicators before and after treatment stages.

Figure 26.4: Life-cycle assessment boundary

How to interpret it: LCA defines a functional unit and system boundary, then inventories material, energy and emissions from extraction to end-of-life. Conclusions depend on boundary and allocation.

Laboratory connection: Prepare a mass-energy inventory per defined functional unit and report excluded processes and uncertainty.

Figure 26.5: Green chemistry metrics

How to interpret it: Atom economy measures theoretical reactant atoms incorporated into desired product; E-factor is waste/product mass; PMI is total input/product mass.

Laboratory connection: Calculate metrics from actual batch records, including solvents and work-up materials under the declared accounting boundary.

Additional university-level derivation

Additional derivation: For linear equilibrium sorption, Cs=KdCw. Total contaminant per bulk volume is θCw+ρbCs=Cw(θ+ρbKd). Dividing by dissolved mass θCw gives retardation factor R=1+ρbKd/θ. This assumes local equilibrium and linear sorption.

Advanced numerical exercises with worked answers

Challenge 26.1

Question: Concentration falls from 80.0 to 10.0 mg L⁻¹ in 6.00 days by first-order decay. Find k and half-life.

Worked answer: k=ln(80/10)/6=0.3466 d⁻¹. t½=0.693/k=2.00 days.

Challenge 26.2

Question: Kd=2.0 L kg⁻¹, bulk density=1.5 kg L⁻¹ and water content=0.30 L L⁻¹. Find retardation factor.

Worked answer: R=1+ρbKd/θ=1+(1.5×2.0)/0.30=11.0.

Challenge 26.3

Question: A process uses 120 kg material to produce 30 kg product; waste is 90 kg. Find PMI and E-factor.

Worked answer: PMI=120/30=4.00. E-factor=90/30=3.00 kg waste per kg product.

Calculation discipline: show the governing equation, substitute values with units, retain appropriate significant figures and state the chemical meaning of the result.

Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Environmental chemistry follows chemical species through air, water, soil, sediment and organisms. Sources release chemicals; transport moves them; transformation changes their form; exposure creates contact with receptors. Risk assessment must consider concentration, chemical form, persistence, bioavailability and duration.

Speciation describes the forms of an element or molecule in a system. pH, redox potential, ligand concentration and organic matter determine whether a metal is free, complexed, precipitated or sorbed. Bioavailability is often more closely related to accessible species than to total concentration.

The atmosphere is chemically active. Photolysis produces radicals; radical chain reactions transform volatile organic compounds and nitrogen oxides. Primary pollutants are emitted directly, while secondary pollutants such as ozone and secondary aerosols form after emission.

Tropospheric ozone forms through sunlight-driven NOx–VOC chemistry. The ozone production regime depends on the relative abundance of nitrogen oxides and reactive organic compounds, so reducing one precursor can have nonlinear effects. Stratospheric ozone absorbs harmful UV radiation and is chemically distinct in its environmental role.

SO2 and NOx can be oxidised to sulfuric and nitric acids and deposited by precipitation or directly from gases and particles. Acid deposition changes lake and soil acidity, mobilises some metals and damages vegetation and infrastructure. Buffering capacity determines ecosystem sensitivity.

CO2, CH4, N2O and halogenated gases absorb infrared radiation and influence radiative forcing. Compare gases using emission mass, atmospheric lifetime and global warming potential over a stated time horizon. Carbon cycles among atmosphere, ocean, biosphere and geological reservoirs.

The carbonate system contains dissolved CO2*, bicarbonate and carbonate. Their relative proportions depend on pH and equilibrium constants. Alkalinity is acid-neutralising capacity, not simply pH. CO2 uptake can lower pH and reduce carbonate ion availability, affecting calcifying organisms.

Excess nutrients can cause eutrophication. Algal biomass grows, then microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen. Biochemical oxygen demand measures oxygen consumed in a defined incubation test; chemical oxygen demand measures chemically oxidisable material. Neither alone describes all ecological effects.

Drinking-water treatment may include coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration and disinfection. Coagulants destabilise colloids; filtration removes suspended particles; disinfectants inactivate pathogens. Treatment must also control disinfection by-products and maintain distribution-system quality.

Soil contaminant fate depends on sorption to mineral and organic surfaces, ion exchange, precipitation, complexation and redox reactions. pH and organic carbon often strongly influence mobility. Leaching can transport contaminants to groundwater; site heterogeneity complicates sampling and remediation.

Persistent organic pollutants resist degradation and may bioaccumulate. Emerging contaminants include pharmaceuticals, PFAS and microplastics. Detection alone does not establish risk: exposure, dose, persistence, toxicity, transformation products and uncertainty must be evaluated.

Toxicology examines adverse effects as functions of dose, route, duration and susceptibility. Hazard is the inherent capacity to cause harm; risk depends on exposure. Dose-response may be nonlinear, and acute effects differ from chronic outcomes. Use validated endpoints and avoid extrapolating beyond evidence.

Green chemistry designs products and processes to reduce hazardous substances and waste at source. Principles include prevention, atom economy, safer solvents, energy efficiency, renewable feedstocks, catalysis, design for degradation and real-time monitoring. Prevention is generally preferable to end-of-pipe treatment.

Atom economy uses stoichiometric mass of desired product relative to reactant mass. E-factor compares waste with product; PMI compares total material input with product. Life-cycle assessment uses functional units and system boundaries to evaluate impacts from raw material extraction to end-of-life.

Waste hierarchy prioritises prevention, reuse, recycling, recovery and safe disposal. Remediation options include excavation, containment, adsorption, oxidation/reduction, bioremediation and phytoremediation. Select methods based on contaminant chemistry, site geology, secondary waste and long-term monitoring.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Environmental chemistry: equilibria, fate and sustainability metrics

1. Partitioning and distribution

A contaminant partitions among air, water, soil organic matter and biota according to chemical potential and phase properties. Henry's law can be written p=Hcc for a dilute solute under a specified convention; note that alternative Henry constants use reciprocal definitions. The octanol–water partition coefficient Kow is a screening indicator for hydrophobicity, but ionisation, metabolism and binding to natural organic matter can change environmental behaviour.

2. Carbonate equilibria and alkalinity

Dissolved CO2 participates in equilibria CO2* + H2O ⇌ H+ + HCO3− and HCO3− ⇌ H+ + CO32−. The Henderson–Hasselbalch relation pH=pKa+log([base]/[acid]) applies to a conjugate pair under appropriate dilute-solution assumptions. Alkalinity measures acid-neutralising capacity and is not identical to pH. Addition of CO2 shifts speciation toward bicarbonate and carbonate depletion at a given total inorganic carbon.

3. First-order environmental degradation

For a simple first-order loss process, dC/dt=−kC, so C=C0e^(−kt) and t1/2=ln2/k. The apparent rate constant may combine photolysis, biodegradation, hydrolysis and other pathways. Field conditions vary with sunlight, temperature, oxygen, microbial communities and sorption, so laboratory half-lives should not be transferred uncritically to the environment.

4. Green chemistry metrics

Atom economy is based on stoichiometric molecular masses and does not count solvents or work-up waste. E-factor includes waste according to a stated accounting boundary. Process mass intensity (PMI) is total mass input divided by mass of product. Life-cycle assessment uses a functional unit and system boundary to compare impacts from raw material extraction through use and end-of-life.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: First-order contaminant decay

Problem: A pollutant degrades with k=0.080 day⁻¹. Find its half-life and fraction after 10 days.

Solution: t1/2=0.693/0.080=8.66 days. C/C0=e^(−0.8)=0.449. About 44.9% remains after ten days under constant first-order conditions.

Worked numerical problem 2: Henderson–Hasselbalch buffer

Problem: A water sample has [HCO3−]/[CO2*]=10 and pKa1=6.35. Estimate pH.

Solution: pH=6.35+log10(10)=7.35. This is an idealised carbonate-pair estimate; activities, alkalinity and other acid–base systems may matter.

Worked numerical problem 3: Partitioning ratio

Problem: At equilibrium, a contaminant has Csoil organic phase/Cwater=25 L kg⁻¹. If water concentration is 0.020 mg L⁻¹, estimate sorbed concentration per kg organic phase.

Solution: Cs=25×0.020=0.50 mg kg⁻¹ organic phase, using the stated distribution coefficient and equilibrium assumption.

Worked numerical problem 4: Atom economy

Problem: A synthesis uses 100 g mol⁻¹ reactant A and 50 g mol⁻¹ B to make 120 g mol⁻¹ desired product plus by-products.

Solution: Atom economy=120/(100+50)×100=80.0%. This metric follows the balanced stoichiometry and excludes solvent, yield and purification waste.

Worked numerical problem 5: E-factor and PMI

Problem: A process uses 12 kg input to make 3 kg product and generates 9 kg waste. Calculate E-factor and PMI.

Solution: E-factor=9/3=3.0 kg waste per kg product. PMI=12/3=4.0 kg input per kg product. Check whether water and recycled streams are included in the boundary.

Worked numerical problem 6: Pollutant removal and residual level

Problem: Treatment reduces 8.0 mg L⁻¹ to 0.40 mg L⁻¹.

Solution: Removal=(8.0−0.40)/8.0×100=95.0%. The residual concentration is 0.40 mg L⁻¹; assess this against the relevant quality standard.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Distinguish hazard from risk.

Answer: Hazard is the intrinsic capacity of a substance or process to cause harm. Risk considers the likelihood and severity of harm under a defined exposure scenario.

Model answer 2

Question: Why is total metal concentration insufficient for toxicity assessment?

Answer: Oxidation state, complexation, pH, redox conditions and binding to particles affect free-ion activity, mobility and bioavailability.

Model answer 3

Question: Explain eutrophication.

Answer: Excess nitrogen and phosphorus stimulate algal and plant growth. Biomass decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, potentially causing hypoxia, fish kills and ecosystem change.

Model answer 4

Question: Why does atom economy not measure the whole environmental performance of a process?

Answer: It ignores yield, solvent use, energy, purification, toxicity and waste treatment. Complement it with E-factor, PMI and life-cycle assessment.

Model answer 5

Question: What is a functional unit in life-cycle assessment?

Answer: It is the quantified service or product function used as the basis for comparison, such as one kilogram of material meeting a defined specification or one unit of service delivered.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: Why is ozone beneficial in the stratosphere but harmful near the ground?

Answer: Stratospheric ozone absorbs biologically damaging ultraviolet radiation. Tropospheric ozone is a reactive oxidant that damages lungs, vegetation and materials and contributes to photochemical smog.

Model answer 7

Question: Explain why pH affects metal mobility in soil.

Answer: pH changes proton competition at mineral surfaces, metal hydrolysis, complexation and precipitation. Acidic conditions often increase mobility of many cationic metals, though behaviour is element-specific.

Model answer 8

Question: What is biomagnification?

Answer: It is an increase in contaminant concentration at higher trophic levels, often for persistent substances that accumulate in organisms faster than they are eliminated.

Model answer 9

Question: Why should life-cycle assessment use a functional unit?

Answer: A functional unit defines the service being compared so alternatives are evaluated on equivalent performance rather than arbitrary product mass or packaging.

Model answer 10

Question: Compare bioremediation and phytoremediation.

Answer: Bioremediation uses microorganisms to transform or immobilise contaminants. Phytoremediation uses plants and associated root-zone processes for uptake, stabilisation or transformation; both depend on contaminant bioavailability and site conditions.
