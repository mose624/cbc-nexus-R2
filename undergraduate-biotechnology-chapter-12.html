Conceptual workflow

1. Principles of immune recognition

The immune system distinguishes molecular patterns through innate and adaptive mechanisms. Innate immunity uses germline-encoded receptors and rapid responses; adaptive immunity involves antigen-specific B and T lymphocytes, clonal expansion and immunological memory. Antibodies bind epitopes through variable regions, while constant regions mediate interactions with immune effector systems. Antigen recognition is shaped by affinity, avidity, valency and molecular presentation.

Monoclonal antibodies provide defined recognition reagents, but specificity is not absolute in every assay. Cross-reactivity, matrix interference, heterophilic antibodies and changes in antigen structure can affect results. Antibody validation should use appropriate positive, negative and orthogonal controls.

2. Immunoassay formats and signal generation

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) convert antigen–antibody binding into a measurable signal. Direct, indirect, sandwich and competitive formats differ in reagent arrangement and signal interpretation. Sandwich assays commonly use capture and detection antibodies recognising distinct epitopes; competitive assays may show an inverse relation between analyte concentration and signal.

Calibration curves relate signal to known standards over a validated range. Samples outside that range should not be extrapolated casually. Dilution linearity, recovery, precision, specificity, detection limit and quantification limit are important validation characteristics.

3. Molecular diagnostics

Molecular diagnostics detect nucleic-acid sequences or molecular signatures associated with organisms, inherited variants or disease processes. PCR amplifies a selected DNA target; reverse-transcription PCR begins with RNA converted to complementary DNA. Real-time PCR monitors signal during amplification, while digital PCR partitions a sample to support absolute quantification under defined assumptions. Isothermal methods operate without repeated thermal cycling.

Analytical detection is not identical to clinical diagnosis. Interpretation depends on specimen quality, timing, prevalence, symptoms, validated cut-offs and the clinical question. A detected sequence may not establish organism viability, active disease or causation.

4. Biosensors and point-of-care systems

A biosensor combines a biological recognition element with a transducer and signal-processing system. Recognition elements may include enzymes, antibodies, nucleic acids, receptors or cells. Transducers convert binding or reaction events into electrical, optical, thermal or mass-related signals. Sensor performance is evaluated through sensitivity, selectivity, response time, dynamic range, stability, reproducibility and resistance to matrix interference.

Point-of-care systems aim to deliver results near the patient or user. Their value depends on usability, quality control, maintenance, supply chains, training, connectivity, affordability and the ability to act on results. A technically sensitive sensor may still be unsuitable if it produces frequent false alarms or requires impractical sample handling.

5. Validation and interpretation of diagnostic tests

Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the target condition who test positive; specificity is the proportion without the condition who test negative. Positive and negative predictive values depend on prevalence and the tested population. Likelihood ratios can help relate test results to pre-test odds. These metrics must be reported with confidence intervals and a clearly defined reference standard.

Analytical validation addresses whether an assay measures the target reliably. Clinical validation addresses how well results relate to the intended clinical condition or outcome. Clinical utility asks whether using the test improves decisions or outcomes. These are distinct questions.

6. Quality systems and responsible implementation

Diagnostic quality systems include documented procedures, staff competence, equipment maintenance, reagent lot control, internal quality control, external quality assessment, traceability and corrective action. Pre-analytical errors—wrong patient, poor collection, transport delay or unsuitable storage—can invalidate an otherwise well-performing assay.

Human samples and health data require informed consent or another lawful basis, privacy safeguards and controlled access. Results must be communicated within professional scope, with confirmatory testing and referral pathways where required. Local regulatory approval and laboratory accreditation requirements vary by jurisdiction.

Worked example: predictive values and prevalence

Assume a test has 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity in a population where prevalence is 10%. Among 1,000 people, approximately 100 have the condition: 90 test positive and 10 negative. Of 900 without the condition, 855 test negative and 45 positive. Positive predictive value = 90/(90+45) = 66.7%; negative predictive value = 855/(855+10) = 98.8%.

The same test can have a different predictive value in a population with a different prevalence. These calculations assume the stated test performance applies to the population and that the reference classification is valid. Do not interpret a positive result as certainty.

Case study: designing a rapid infection test

A public-health laboratory evaluates a rapid antigen test for use in community clinics. The intended use is defined before performance is assessed: target population, symptoms or screening status, specimen type, timing and user setting. The study compares the rapid test with an appropriate reference method and records invalid results, operator effects and specimen quality.

Performance is reported separately for relevant subgroups and across disease stages where sample size permits. The team considers false-negative consequences, confirmatory testing, training, storage conditions, waste disposal, affordability and supply continuity. A test that works in an expert laboratory may require additional evaluation before deployment by minimally trained users.

Key technical glossary

Use these definitions as a starting point; some terms have narrower meanings in particular subdisciplines.

Antigen
Molecule or molecular structure recognised by an immune receptor or antibody.
Epitope
Specific region of an antigen recognised by an antibody or immune receptor.
Affinity
Strength of interaction between one antibody binding site and one epitope.
Avidity
Overall functional strength of multiple simultaneous binding interactions.
Monoclonal antibody
Antibody reagent with a defined sequence or clonal origin and target specificity.
ELISA
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay that uses enzyme-generated signal to detect or quantify an analyte.
Sandwich assay
Assay format in which analyte is captured and detected by two recognition reagents.
Competitive assay
Assay format in which analyte competes with a labelled or immobilised counterpart, often producing inverse signal.
PCR
Polymerase chain reaction for amplification of selected DNA sequences.
Reverse transcription
Enzymatic conversion of RNA into complementary DNA.
Analytical sensitivity
Ability of an assay to detect low concentrations or small quantities of target under defined conditions.
Clinical sensitivity
Proportion of people with the target condition who test positive.
Specificity
Proportion of people without the target condition who test negative.
Predictive value
Probability that a test result correctly reflects condition status in the tested population.
Biosensor
Device combining a biological recognition element with a transducer and signal-processing system.
