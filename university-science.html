Scientific reasoning

A scientific investigation begins with a question and a testable hypothesis. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables. Use a suitable comparison or control, collect reliable measurements, analyse patterns and state whether the evidence supports the hypothesis. A hypothesis is not proven simply because one experiment agrees with it.

Question → hypothesis → design and controls → measurements → analysis → interpretation → replication and communication

Undergraduate science: core disciplinary knowledge

Biological sciences

Progress from molecular and cellular organisation to organisms, populations and ecosystems. Key themes include gene expression, inheritance, evolution by natural selection, homeostasis, organismal physiology, ecology, biodiversity, microbiology and biotechnology. Interpret biological processes across levels rather than treating a single mechanism as a complete explanation.

Chemical sciences

Connect quantum models of atomic structure to periodic trends, bonding and molecular geometry. Thermodynamics describes energetic favourability; kinetics describes reaction rates; equilibrium describes the balance of forward and reverse processes. Organic chemistry studies structure, mechanisms and stereochemistry; analytical chemistry identifies and quantifies substances; inorganic and materials chemistry examine metals, coordination compounds and functional solids.

Physical sciences

Mechanics models motion and forces; electromagnetism describes electric and magnetic fields; thermodynamics and statistical mechanics connect macroscopic properties to microscopic states; quantum mechanics describes matter and radiation at small scales. Mathematical models must be interpreted with their assumptions, boundary conditions and domain of validity.

Interdisciplinary science

Biochemistry connects molecular structure and biological function; biophysics applies physical models to living systems; geochemistry studies chemical processes in Earth systems; environmental science integrates ecological, chemical, physical and social evidence; materials science links composition, processing, structure and performance.

Postgraduate and research-level science

Advanced study may include genomics, systems biology, immunology, molecular diagnostics, synthetic chemistry, catalysis, spectroscopy, nanomaterials, condensed-matter physics, computational modelling, climate science and advanced environmental analysis. A research proposal should define a gap, formulate a tractable question, justify methods, identify data and equipment needs, assess safety and ethics, and specify how results will be validated.

Reproducibility and research integrity

Keep complete laboratory records, preserve raw data, document exclusions and transformations, distinguish exploratory from confirmatory analyses, report null or conflicting findings, and disclose relevant conflicts. Reproducibility depends on transparent methods, suitable controls, calibrated instruments, traceable materials and appropriate statistical analysis.

Laboratory principles and worked data examples

Measurement and uncertainty

A measurement is an estimate, not an exact value. Record instrument resolution and calibration information. For independent uncertainties in a sum or difference, absolute uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature; for products or quotients, relative uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature, subject to the assumptions and measurement model.

Worked example: A sample has measured mass 25.0 g and volume 10.0 cm³. Density ρ=m/V=2.50 g cm⁻³. If mass and volume are each reported to three significant figures, report density consistently and state uncertainty if available.

Graph interpretation

Plot the dependent variable against the independent variable, label axes with units, show the data and an appropriate fitted model, and inspect residuals. A straight-line fit is not justified merely because it is convenient; consider the mechanism and uncertainty.

Laboratory safety and quality

Use approved procedures, training, personal protective equipment and risk assessments. Label samples, segregate waste, maintain equipment and record incidents. Never improvise hazardous procedures from a general online note.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Distinguish a hypothesis from a scientific theory.
A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or prediction. A scientific theory is a broad, evidence-supported explanatory framework that integrates many observations and tests.
2. Explain why a control group may be needed.
A suitable control provides a comparison that helps determine whether observed differences are associated with the treatment or variable being investigated rather than background conditions.
3. Calculate density for a 60 g object with volume 20 cm³.
ρ=60/20=3 g cm⁻³. Include measurement precision and uncertainty when supplied.
4. Compare thermodynamics and kinetics.
Thermodynamics addresses energetic favourability and equilibrium; kinetics addresses reaction rates and mechanisms. A thermodynamically favourable reaction may still be slow.
5. Identify four elements of a reproducible experiment.
Examples include a precise protocol, calibrated instruments, appropriate controls, documented materials and conditions, raw-data preservation, stated analysis methods and transparent reporting of exclusions.

Chapter 1: The Nature of Science, Evidence and Measurement

Chapter outcomes. By the end of this chapter, learners should be able to distinguish observation from inference, formulate testable questions, identify variables, explain measurement quality, interpret uncertainty and communicate a defensible scientific conclusion.

1.1 Science as a way of knowing

Science is a systematic process for developing and testing explanations about the natural world. It combines observation, logical reasoning, measurement, modelling, experimentation, criticism and communication. Scientific knowledge is provisional in the precise sense that explanations remain open to revision when stronger evidence or more powerful models become available. Provisional does not mean arbitrary: a mature scientific explanation is constrained by reproducible observations, consistency with established evidence and successful predictions.

Observation records what is detected using the senses or instruments; inference interprets what those observations may mean. For example, a rise in the temperature of a reaction vessel is an observation. The claim that the reaction released heat is an inference that should be tested against the apparatus, surroundings and measurement method. Separating observation from interpretation reduces confirmation bias and makes reports easier to audit.

1.2 Questions, hypotheses and models

A useful research question is specific, answerable and connected to an observable phenomenon. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction that can be tested. It should identify the conditions under which evidence would count against it. A model is a simplified representation—conceptual, mathematical, physical or computational—used to explain patterns or predict outcomes. Every model has assumptions and a domain of validity. A simple ideal-gas model is useful under specified conditions, but real gases depart from ideal behaviour when intermolecular forces and molecular volume become important.

Scientific theories are broad explanatory frameworks supported by multiple independent lines of evidence. In everyday speech, theory may mean a guess; in science, a theory such as evolution or germ theory integrates observations, generates testable predictions and explains a wide range of phenomena. A law describes a regular relationship under stated conditions; a theory explains mechanisms and relationships. Neither is simply a rung that automatically turns into the other.

1.3 Variables and experimental design

The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed or selected for comparison. The dependent variable is the measured outcome. Controlled variables are conditions kept as similar as practicable so that the comparison is interpretable. A control condition provides a reference for estimating what would happen without the treatment or under a baseline condition. Randomisation can reduce systematic allocation bias; replication helps estimate variability and determine whether an effect is repeatable.

Suppose a student investigates how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis in an aquatic plant. Light intensity is the independent variable; oxygen production per unit time is a possible dependent measure. Temperature, carbon dioxide availability, plant size, acclimation time and measurement duration should be controlled or recorded. A series of light levels, repeated trials and a clear method for measuring oxygen make the investigation stronger. The student should also recognise that oxygen bubbles may not provide a perfectly accurate measure of oxygen production if bubble size varies.

1.4 Measurement, accuracy and precision

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted reference value; precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate if an instrument is miscalibrated. Resolution is the smallest increment an instrument can display or reliably distinguish. Calibration compares an instrument with a traceable reference and may reveal systematic error. A complete measurement record includes the value, unit, instrument or method, relevant conditions and uncertainty where known.

Random errors cause measurements to vary unpredictably and can often be reduced by repeated measurement and suitable statistical summaries. Systematic errors shift results in a consistent direction and are not removed simply by taking more readings. Examples include a zero error, a biased sampling method or a temperature probe that has not reached thermal equilibrium. Good experimental design seeks to identify both types rather than treating every discrepancy as random noise.

1.5 Significant figures and dimensional reasoning

Units are part of a physical quantity, not decoration. Dimensional analysis checks whether an equation is structurally plausible. If distance is measured in metres and time in seconds, speed has dimensions of metres per second. An expression that adds a length to a time is dimensionally invalid. Significant figures communicate the precision implied by measurements; calculations should not imply more precision than the input data support. Exact counted quantities and defined conversion factors are treated differently from measured quantities.

Worked example: A cart travels 12.0 m in 4.0 s. Its average speed is v = distance/time = 12.0/4.0 = 3.0 m s⁻¹. The reported precision should reflect the least precise measured input. This is an average over the interval; it does not establish that the cart moved at constant speed.

1.6 Uncertainty and reporting

Uncertainty expresses the range of values reasonably associated with a measurement under a stated method. It is not the same as a mistake. For independent uncertainties, propagation rules depend on the mathematical operation and the model. For a sum or difference, absolute uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature; for a product or quotient, relative uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature. These rules rely on assumptions about independence and distributions, so a report should state how uncertainty was estimated.

1.7 Data presentation and interpretation

Tables should have descriptive headings and units. Graph axes must identify variables and units; scales should show the data without distorting the pattern. A fitted line or curve should be justified by the scientific model and the data, not selected only because it looks attractive. Residual plots can reveal curvature, changing variance or influential observations. Correlation indicates association, not necessarily causation: a third variable, reverse causation or selection effects may explain an observed relationship.

1.8 Ethics, integrity and reproducibility

Research integrity requires honest recording, retention of raw data, transparent methods, accurate attribution and disclosure of relevant conflicts. Do not invent, selectively suppress or alter results to fit an expected outcome. Predefine exclusion rules where possible and explain deviations. Human and animal research requires appropriate ethical review, consent or other lawful authority, welfare safeguards and institutional oversight. Environmental sampling should minimise harm and follow permits. Reproducibility improves when protocols, code, materials, instrument settings and analysis decisions are documented sufficiently for another competent researcher to understand the work.

Chapter summary

  • Scientific explanations are tested against evidence and remain open to revision.
  • Questions, hypotheses and models must be precise and explicit about assumptions.
  • Sound experiments identify variables, controls, replication and possible sources of bias.
  • Accuracy, precision, resolution and uncertainty describe different aspects of measurement quality.
  • Units, graphs, transparent methods and research integrity make findings interpretable and reproducible.

Review and application questions

  1. Distinguish observation, inference, hypothesis, model and theory using one biological example.
  2. Design an investigation of the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. Identify variables, controls, replication and at least three limitations.
  3. A balance reads 0.20 g when empty. Explain the likely error and how you would check and correct it.
  4. Why does repeating a measurement reduce random uncertainty but not necessarily systematic error?
  5. Explain why a strong correlation between two variables does not, by itself, establish causation.
Indicative marking guidance: Award credit for precise definitions, a logically controlled design, recognition of calibration and measurement limitations, and a clear distinction between association and causal evidence. Full answers should justify choices rather than merely list terminology.

Chapter 2: Cell Biology, Biomolecules and Membrane Transport

Cells are the basic structural and functional units of living organisms. Cell theory states that organisms are composed of cells, the cell is a fundamental unit of life, and new cells arise from pre-existing cells. Modern cell biology examines how molecular components organise into compartments, exchange matter and energy, communicate and reproduce.

2.1 Prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisation

Prokaryotic cells, including bacteria and archaea, lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Their DNA occupies a nucleoid region, and many contain plasmids. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. The nucleus houses most genomic DNA; mitochondria participate in aerobic energy conversion; chloroplasts in plants and algae capture light energy; the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus contribute to synthesis, modification and trafficking of proteins and lipids. Ribosomes translate messenger RNA into polypeptides in both cell types, although their sizes and molecular compositions differ.

Cell structure reflects function. A neuron has an extended shape suited to signalling over distance. Intestinal epithelial cells may have microvilli that increase absorptive surface area. Root hair cells increase contact with soil water and dissolved minerals. Structure alone does not prove function, but it provides hypotheses that can be tested through microscopy, biochemical assays and physiological experiments.

2.2 Biological molecules

Water is a polar molecule and an effective solvent for many ions and polar compounds. Hydrogen bonding contributes to its relatively high specific heat capacity and cohesion. Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides. Glucose is a major metabolic substrate; glycogen and starch store glucose in animals and plants respectively; cellulose contributes to plant cell walls. Lipids include triglycerides, phospholipids and steroids. Phospholipids form bilayers because their hydrophilic heads interact with water while hydrophobic tails avoid it.

Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Their function depends on sequence and three-dimensional structure. Enzymes lower activation energy by stabilising transition states; they do not change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products. Temperature, pH, substrate concentration and inhibitors can affect enzyme activity. Nucleic acids store, transmit and help express genetic information. DNA usually forms a double helix with complementary base pairing; RNA molecules perform roles in gene expression, regulation and catalysis.

2.3 Membrane structure and transport

The fluid mosaic model describes membranes as dynamic lipid bilayers containing proteins, cholesterol and associated carbohydrates. Selective permeability means that some substances cross more readily than others. Small non-polar molecules may diffuse through the lipid bilayer; ions and many polar molecules require channels or carriers. Simple diffusion moves substances down a concentration or electrochemical gradient without direct metabolic energy input. Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins but remains passive. Active transport moves substances against a gradient and requires energy directly or indirectly.

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane in response to differences in water potential. In a hypotonic environment, a cell may gain water; in a hypertonic environment it may lose water. Plant cell walls resist excessive expansion and contribute to turgor. Animal cells lack a rigid wall and may swell or shrink under different osmotic conditions. The direction and magnitude of water movement depend on solute concentration, pressure and membrane properties.

2.4 ATP and cellular energy

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a transferable energy currency. ATP hydrolysis is coupled to energetically unfavourable processes such as biosynthesis, active transport and movement. Cells regenerate ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation, oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation in photosynthetic organisms. The phrase “energy stored in a bond” is an oversimplification: the net energetics of hydrolysis depend on the relative stability and concentrations of reactants and products.

2.5 Microscopy and experimental evidence

Light microscopy can reveal cells, tissues and some organelles; resolution is limited by wavelength and numerical aperture. Electron microscopy provides higher resolution but requires specialised preparation and does not normally show living cells in their natural state. Stains, fluorescent labels and molecular probes can improve contrast or identify specific components. Microscopy images should include scale bars, magnification information and preparation methods where relevant.

  3. Distinguish simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis and active transport.
  4. Explain why enzyme activity may decline at very high temperature.
  5. Describe two limitations of microscopy and how they affect interpretation.
