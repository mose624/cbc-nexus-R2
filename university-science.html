Scientific reasoning

A scientific investigation begins with a question and a testable hypothesis. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables. Use a suitable comparison or control, collect reliable measurements, analyse patterns and state whether the evidence supports the hypothesis. A hypothesis is not proven simply because one experiment agrees with it.

Question → hypothesis → design and controls → measurements → analysis → interpretation → replication and communication

Undergraduate science: core disciplinary knowledge

Biological sciences

Progress from molecular and cellular organisation to organisms, populations and ecosystems. Key themes include gene expression, inheritance, evolution by natural selection, homeostasis, organismal physiology, ecology, biodiversity, microbiology and biotechnology. Interpret biological processes across levels rather than treating a single mechanism as a complete explanation.

Chemical sciences

Connect quantum models of atomic structure to periodic trends, bonding and molecular geometry. Thermodynamics describes energetic favourability; kinetics describes reaction rates; equilibrium describes the balance of forward and reverse processes. Organic chemistry studies structure, mechanisms and stereochemistry; analytical chemistry identifies and quantifies substances; inorganic and materials chemistry examine metals, coordination compounds and functional solids.

Physical sciences

Mechanics models motion and forces; electromagnetism describes electric and magnetic fields; thermodynamics and statistical mechanics connect macroscopic properties to microscopic states; quantum mechanics describes matter and radiation at small scales. Mathematical models must be interpreted with their assumptions, boundary conditions and domain of validity.

Interdisciplinary science

Biochemistry connects molecular structure and biological function; biophysics applies physical models to living systems; geochemistry studies chemical processes in Earth systems; environmental science integrates ecological, chemical, physical and social evidence; materials science links composition, processing, structure and performance.

Postgraduate and research-level science

Advanced study may include genomics, systems biology, immunology, molecular diagnostics, synthetic chemistry, catalysis, spectroscopy, nanomaterials, condensed-matter physics, computational modelling, climate science and advanced environmental analysis. A research proposal should define a gap, formulate a tractable question, justify methods, identify data and equipment needs, assess safety and ethics, and specify how results will be validated.

Reproducibility and research integrity

Keep complete laboratory records, preserve raw data, document exclusions and transformations, distinguish exploratory from confirmatory analyses, report null or conflicting findings, and disclose relevant conflicts. Reproducibility depends on transparent methods, suitable controls, calibrated instruments, traceable materials and appropriate statistical analysis.

Laboratory principles and worked data examples

Measurement and uncertainty

A measurement is an estimate, not an exact value. Record instrument resolution and calibration information. For independent uncertainties in a sum or difference, absolute uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature; for products or quotients, relative uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature, subject to the assumptions and measurement model.

Worked example: A sample has measured mass 25.0 g and volume 10.0 cm³. Density ρ=m/V=2.50 g cm⁻³. If mass and volume are each reported to three significant figures, report density consistently and state uncertainty if available.

Graph interpretation

Plot the dependent variable against the independent variable, label axes with units, show the data and an appropriate fitted model, and inspect residuals. A straight-line fit is not justified merely because it is convenient; consider the mechanism and uncertainty.

Laboratory safety and quality

Use approved procedures, training, personal protective equipment and risk assessments. Label samples, segregate waste, maintain equipment and record incidents. Never improvise hazardous procedures from a general online note.

Practice questions with guidance

1. Distinguish a hypothesis from a scientific theory.
A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or prediction. A scientific theory is a broad, evidence-supported explanatory framework that integrates many observations and tests.
2. Explain why a control group may be needed.
A suitable control provides a comparison that helps determine whether observed differences are associated with the treatment or variable being investigated rather than background conditions.
3. Calculate density for a 60 g object with volume 20 cm³.
ρ=60/20=3 g cm⁻³. Include measurement precision and uncertainty when supplied.
4. Compare thermodynamics and kinetics.
Thermodynamics addresses energetic favourability and equilibrium; kinetics addresses reaction rates and mechanisms. A thermodynamically favourable reaction may still be slow.
5. Identify four elements of a reproducible experiment.
Examples include a precise protocol, calibrated instruments, appropriate controls, documented materials and conditions, raw-data preservation, stated analysis methods and transparent reporting of exclusions.

Chapter 1: The Nature of Science, Evidence and Measurement

Chapter outcomes. By the end of this chapter, learners should be able to distinguish observation from inference, formulate testable questions, identify variables, explain measurement quality, interpret uncertainty and communicate a defensible scientific conclusion.

1.1 Science as a way of knowing

Science is a systematic process for developing and testing explanations about the natural world. It combines observation, logical reasoning, measurement, modelling, experimentation, criticism and communication. Scientific knowledge is provisional in the precise sense that explanations remain open to revision when stronger evidence or more powerful models become available. Provisional does not mean arbitrary: a mature scientific explanation is constrained by reproducible observations, consistency with established evidence and successful predictions.

Observation records what is detected using the senses or instruments; inference interprets what those observations may mean. For example, a rise in the temperature of a reaction vessel is an observation. The claim that the reaction released heat is an inference that should be tested against the apparatus, surroundings and measurement method. Separating observation from interpretation reduces confirmation bias and makes reports easier to audit.

1.2 Questions, hypotheses and models

A useful research question is specific, answerable and connected to an observable phenomenon. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction that can be tested. It should identify the conditions under which evidence would count against it. A model is a simplified representation—conceptual, mathematical, physical or computational—used to explain patterns or predict outcomes. Every model has assumptions and a domain of validity. A simple ideal-gas model is useful under specified conditions, but real gases depart from ideal behaviour when intermolecular forces and molecular volume become important.

Scientific theories are broad explanatory frameworks supported by multiple independent lines of evidence. In everyday speech, theory may mean a guess; in science, a theory such as evolution or germ theory integrates observations, generates testable predictions and explains a wide range of phenomena. A law describes a regular relationship under stated conditions; a theory explains mechanisms and relationships. Neither is simply a rung that automatically turns into the other.

1.3 Variables and experimental design

The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed or selected for comparison. The dependent variable is the measured outcome. Controlled variables are conditions kept as similar as practicable so that the comparison is interpretable. A control condition provides a reference for estimating what would happen without the treatment or under a baseline condition. Randomisation can reduce systematic allocation bias; replication helps estimate variability and determine whether an effect is repeatable.

Suppose a student investigates how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis in an aquatic plant. Light intensity is the independent variable; oxygen production per unit time is a possible dependent measure. Temperature, carbon dioxide availability, plant size, acclimation time and measurement duration should be controlled or recorded. A series of light levels, repeated trials and a clear method for measuring oxygen make the investigation stronger. The student should also recognise that oxygen bubbles may not provide a perfectly accurate measure of oxygen production if bubble size varies.

1.4 Measurement, accuracy and precision

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted reference value; precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate if an instrument is miscalibrated. Resolution is the smallest increment an instrument can display or reliably distinguish. Calibration compares an instrument with a traceable reference and may reveal systematic error. A complete measurement record includes the value, unit, instrument or method, relevant conditions and uncertainty where known.

Random errors cause measurements to vary unpredictably and can often be reduced by repeated measurement and suitable statistical summaries. Systematic errors shift results in a consistent direction and are not removed simply by taking more readings. Examples include a zero error, a biased sampling method or a temperature probe that has not reached thermal equilibrium. Good experimental design seeks to identify both types rather than treating every discrepancy as random noise.

1.5 Significant figures and dimensional reasoning

Units are part of a physical quantity, not decoration. Dimensional analysis checks whether an equation is structurally plausible. If distance is measured in metres and time in seconds, speed has dimensions of metres per second. An expression that adds a length to a time is dimensionally invalid. Significant figures communicate the precision implied by measurements; calculations should not imply more precision than the input data support. Exact counted quantities and defined conversion factors are treated differently from measured quantities.

Worked example: A cart travels 12.0 m in 4.0 s. Its average speed is v = distance/time = 12.0/4.0 = 3.0 m s⁻¹. The reported precision should reflect the least precise measured input. This is an average over the interval; it does not establish that the cart moved at constant speed.

1.6 Uncertainty and reporting

Uncertainty expresses the range of values reasonably associated with a measurement under a stated method. It is not the same as a mistake. For independent uncertainties, propagation rules depend on the mathematical operation and the model. For a sum or difference, absolute uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature; for a product or quotient, relative uncertainties are commonly combined in quadrature. These rules rely on assumptions about independence and distributions, so a report should state how uncertainty was estimated.

1.7 Data presentation and interpretation

Tables should have descriptive headings and units. Graph axes must identify variables and units; scales should show the data without distorting the pattern. A fitted line or curve should be justified by the scientific model and the data, not selected only because it looks attractive. Residual plots can reveal curvature, changing variance or influential observations. Correlation indicates association, not necessarily causation: a third variable, reverse causation or selection effects may explain an observed relationship.

1.8 Ethics, integrity and reproducibility

Research integrity requires honest recording, retention of raw data, transparent methods, accurate attribution and disclosure of relevant conflicts. Do not invent, selectively suppress or alter results to fit an expected outcome. Predefine exclusion rules where possible and explain deviations. Human and animal research requires appropriate ethical review, consent or other lawful authority, welfare safeguards and institutional oversight. Environmental sampling should minimise harm and follow permits. Reproducibility improves when protocols, code, materials, instrument settings and analysis decisions are documented sufficiently for another competent researcher to understand the work.

Chapter summary

  • Scientific explanations are tested against evidence and remain open to revision.
  • Questions, hypotheses and models must be precise and explicit about assumptions.
  • Sound experiments identify variables, controls, replication and possible sources of bias.
  • Accuracy, precision, resolution and uncertainty describe different aspects of measurement quality.
  • Units, graphs, transparent methods and research integrity make findings interpretable and reproducible.

Review and application questions

  1. Distinguish observation, inference, hypothesis, model and theory using one biological example.
  2. Design an investigation of the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. Identify variables, controls, replication and at least three limitations.
  3. A balance reads 0.20 g when empty. Explain the likely error and how you would check and correct it.
  4. Why does repeating a measurement reduce random uncertainty but not necessarily systematic error?
  5. Explain why a strong correlation between two variables does not, by itself, establish causation.

Chapter 2: Cell Biology, Biomolecules and Membrane Transport

Cells are the basic structural and functional units of living organisms. Cell theory states that organisms are composed of cells, the cell is a fundamental unit of life, and new cells arise from pre-existing cells. Modern cell biology examines how molecular components organise into compartments, exchange matter and energy, communicate and reproduce.

2.1 Prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisation

Prokaryotic cells, including bacteria and archaea, lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Their DNA occupies a nucleoid region, and many contain plasmids. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. The nucleus houses most genomic DNA; mitochondria participate in aerobic energy conversion; chloroplasts in plants and algae capture light energy; the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus contribute to synthesis, modification and trafficking of proteins and lipids. Ribosomes translate messenger RNA into polypeptides in both cell types, although their sizes and molecular compositions differ.

Cell structure reflects function. A neuron has an extended shape suited to signalling over distance. Intestinal epithelial cells may have microvilli that increase absorptive surface area. Root hair cells increase contact with soil water and dissolved minerals. Structure alone does not prove function, but it provides hypotheses that can be tested through microscopy, biochemical assays and physiological experiments.

2.2 Biological molecules

Water is a polar molecule and an effective solvent for many ions and polar compounds. Hydrogen bonding contributes to its relatively high specific heat capacity and cohesion. Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides. Glucose is a major metabolic substrate; glycogen and starch store glucose in animals and plants respectively; cellulose contributes to plant cell walls. Lipids include triglycerides, phospholipids and steroids. Phospholipids form bilayers because their hydrophilic heads interact with water while hydrophobic tails avoid it.

Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Their function depends on sequence and three-dimensional structure. Enzymes lower activation energy by stabilising transition states; they do not change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products. Temperature, pH, substrate concentration and inhibitors can affect enzyme activity. Nucleic acids store, transmit and help express genetic information. DNA usually forms a double helix with complementary base pairing; RNA molecules perform roles in gene expression, regulation and catalysis.

2.3 Membrane structure and transport

The fluid mosaic model describes membranes as dynamic lipid bilayers containing proteins, cholesterol and associated carbohydrates. Selective permeability means that some substances cross more readily than others. Small non-polar molecules may diffuse through the lipid bilayer; ions and many polar molecules require channels or carriers. Simple diffusion moves substances down a concentration or electrochemical gradient without direct metabolic energy input. Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins but remains passive. Active transport moves substances against a gradient and requires energy directly or indirectly.

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane in response to differences in water potential. In a hypotonic environment, a cell may gain water; in a hypertonic environment it may lose water. Plant cell walls resist excessive expansion and contribute to turgor. Animal cells lack a rigid wall and may swell or shrink under different osmotic conditions. The direction and magnitude of water movement depend on solute concentration, pressure and membrane properties.

Extracellular solution ⇄ phospholipid bilayer with channels and carriers ⇄ cytoplasm
Passive transport: down electrochemical gradient
Active transport: energy-coupled movement against gradient

2.4 ATP and cellular energy

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a transferable energy currency. ATP hydrolysis is coupled to energetically unfavourable processes such as biosynthesis, active transport and movement. Cells regenerate ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation, oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation in photosynthetic organisms. The phrase “energy stored in a bond” is an oversimplification: the net energetics of hydrolysis depend on the relative stability and concentrations of reactants and products.

2.5 Microscopy and experimental evidence

Light microscopy can reveal cells, tissues and some organelles; resolution is limited by wavelength and numerical aperture. Electron microscopy provides higher resolution but requires specialised preparation and does not normally show living cells in their natural state. Stains, fluorescent labels and molecular probes can improve contrast or identify specific components. Microscopy images should include scale bars, magnification information and preparation methods where relevant.

Review questions

  1. Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell organisation.
  2. Explain how phospholipid structure produces a selectively permeable membrane.
  3. Distinguish simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis and active transport.
  4. Explain why enzyme activity may decline at very high temperature.
  5. Describe two limitations of microscopy and how they affect interpretation.

Chapter 3: Genetics, Gene Expression and Cellular Metabolism

3.1 DNA, genes and chromosomes

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) stores hereditary information in a sequence of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, phosphate group and nitrogenous base. In the common double-stranded form, adenine pairs with thymine and guanine pairs with cytosine. Complementary base pairing supports accurate replication and provides a template for transcription. A gene is a DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product, usually an RNA or protein, together with regulatory sequences that influence when and where it is expressed. A chromosome is an organised DNA-protein structure; chromosome number and organisation vary among species.

3.2 DNA replication and inheritance

DNA replication is described as semi-conservative because each daughter DNA molecule contains one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. Helicase separates strands, primase lays RNA primers, DNA polymerases extend new strands in a defined direction, and other enzymes process and join fragments. Proofreading and repair pathways reduce errors, although mutations can arise. Mutations may be neutral, harmful or beneficial depending on context; their effects can range from no detectable change to altered protein function or gene regulation.

During meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and recombine, and chromosome segregation produces haploid gametes. Independent assortment and crossing over contribute to genetic variation. Mendelian inheritance describes patterns for some traits, but many human and organismal traits are polygenic, influenced by environment, or shaped by complex gene interactions. A dominant allele is not necessarily common, advantageous or more biologically powerful; dominance describes the phenotype of a heterozygote under a defined genetic model.

3.3 From DNA to functional product

Transcription uses a DNA template to produce RNA. In eukaryotes, primary messenger RNA is processed through capping, splicing and polyadenylation before export from the nucleus. Translation occurs at ribosomes, where transfer RNAs match codons in messenger RNA with amino acids. The resulting polypeptide folds and may undergo chemical modification, assembly or transport. Gene expression is regulated at chromatin, transcriptional, RNA-processing, translational and post-translational levels. Regulation enables cells with largely the same genome to develop different specialised functions.

DNA --transcription--> RNA --translation at ribosome--> polypeptide --folding/modification--> functional protein

3.4 Cellular respiration

Cellular respiration transfers energy from nutrients into ATP. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and converts glucose into pyruvate, producing a small net yield of ATP and reduced electron carriers. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is processed to acetyl-CoA, which enters the citric acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix. The cycle releases carbon dioxide and generates reduced electron carriers. Electrons pass through the inner mitochondrial membrane electron transport chain; the resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthase. Oxygen acts as the terminal electron acceptor in aerobic respiration.

Fermentation allows continued glycolysis when oxidative phosphorylation is unavailable or insufficient by regenerating NAD⁺. It yields much less ATP per glucose than complete aerobic oxidation. The exact energy yield depends on cell type, shuttle systems, coupling efficiency and conditions; avoid treating one textbook number as universal. Metabolism is regulated by substrate availability, enzyme activity, energy status, hormones and cellular signalling.

3.5 Photosynthesis and carbon fixation

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy. In oxygenic photosynthesis, light-dependent reactions in thylakoid membranes generate ATP and NADPH and release oxygen through water oxidation. The Calvin-Benson cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into carbohydrate precursors. The cycle does not directly produce glucose as a single-step product; carbon intermediates are used in broader biosynthetic pathways. C3, C4 and CAM plants differ in carbon-concentrating strategies that influence water-use efficiency and performance under different environmental conditions.

3.6 Enzyme regulation and metabolic integration

Enzymes are regulated through substrate availability, allosteric effectors, covalent modification, compartmentalisation and changes in gene expression. Feedback inhibition occurs when a pathway product inhibits an earlier enzyme, helping prevent wasteful overproduction. Metabolic pathways are integrated: carbohydrate, lipid and amino-acid metabolism share intermediates and respond to energy demand. A pathway diagram should show direction, key control points and the cellular compartment, rather than implying that every reaction occurs at the same location.

3.7 Applying molecular knowledge

PCR amplifies selected DNA regions through repeated cycles of denaturation, primer annealing and extension. Gel electrophoresis separates nucleic acid fragments by size under specified conditions. Sequencing determines nucleotide order, while gene-expression methods estimate RNA abundance. Each method has limitations: contamination, primer specificity, sampling bias, sequencing errors and differences in data processing can affect results. Molecular evidence should be interpreted alongside controls and biological context.

Review questions

  1. Explain why DNA replication is called semi-conservative.
  2. Describe transcription and translation, identifying the main molecular participants.
  3. Compare aerobic respiration and fermentation in terms of electron acceptors and ATP production.
  4. Explain how light-dependent reactions support carbon fixation.
  5. Why can a DNA sequence variant have different effects in different genetic and environmental contexts?

Chapter 4: Evolution, Biodiversity and Ecology

4.1 Evolution by natural selection

Evolution is change in heritable characteristics of populations across generations. Natural selection occurs when individuals vary in traits, some of that variation is heritable, and individuals with particular variants leave different numbers of surviving offspring in a given environment. Selection acts on phenotypes, while population-level genetic change is observed across generations. Fitness is relative reproductive contribution in a particular environment, not strength, health or moral worth. A trait that is advantageous under one set of conditions may be neutral or disadvantageous under another.

Mutation introduces new genetic variants; recombination reshuffles existing variants; gene flow moves alleles between populations; genetic drift changes allele frequencies through chance, especially in small populations. Natural selection, mutation, migration and drift interact. The founder effect and population bottlenecks can reduce genetic diversity through sampling effects. Evolution does not have a predetermined direction or goal, and organisms do not evolve traits because they consciously need them.

4.2 Evidence for evolution

Evidence includes patterns in the fossil record, comparative anatomy, embryology, biogeography, molecular homology and directly observed evolutionary change. Fossils provide a time-ordered record that is incomplete because preservation is uneven. Homologous structures share ancestry even when they perform different functions; analogous structures may have similar functions but evolved independently. Molecular comparisons can estimate relatedness when appropriate models and assumptions are used. Multiple independent evidence streams strengthen evolutionary explanations.

4.3 Speciation and biodiversity

Speciation occurs when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge sufficiently to form distinct evolutionary lineages. Geographic isolation can reduce gene flow, but speciation can also occur without physical separation. Reproductive barriers may act before fertilisation, such as differences in behaviour or breeding time, or after fertilisation through reduced hybrid viability or fertility. Species concepts differ and may be difficult to apply to fossils, asexual organisms and populations with ongoing gene flow.

4.4 Ecosystem structure and energy flow

An ecosystem includes organisms and the physical environment with which they interact. Producers capture energy, consumers obtain energy by feeding, and decomposers recycle nutrients. Energy flows through trophic levels and is dissipated as heat; matter cycles through biogeochemical pathways. Food webs represent interconnected feeding relationships more accurately than a single food chain. Ecological pyramids may represent numbers, biomass or energy, and these are not interchangeable.

Sunlight → producers → primary consumers → secondary consumers → higher consumers
Dead organic matter and waste → decomposers → inorganic nutrients → producers
Energy flows and dissipates; nutrients are recycled.

4.5 Population ecology

Population size changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Exponential growth is a simplified model for populations with abundant resources; logistic growth introduces a carrying-capacity parameter. Carrying capacity is not necessarily fixed: it can change with climate, resource availability, disease, habitat and human activity. Density-dependent factors often become stronger as population density rises, while density-independent events can affect populations regardless of density. Real populations fluctuate and may not follow a smooth curve.

4.6 Biogeochemical cycles

The carbon cycle includes photosynthetic uptake, respiration, decomposition, ocean-atmosphere exchange, sedimentation and combustion. The nitrogen cycle includes fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification, carried out by organisms and abiotic processes. Phosphorus cycles through rocks, soil, water and organisms and has no major gaseous phase under ordinary ecosystem conditions. Human activities can alter nutrient cycles through fossil-fuel combustion, land-use change, fertiliser use and wastewater discharge.

4.7 Conservation and human impacts

Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. Threats include habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution and climate change. Conservation approaches include protected areas, habitat restoration, sustainable harvesting, ecological corridors, captive breeding where appropriate, and community-led stewardship. Conservation decisions involve ecological evidence, livelihoods, land rights, governance and intergenerational equity. Metrics such as species richness, abundance and ecosystem function describe different dimensions and should be selected to match the question.

Chapter 5: Chemical Structure, Bonding and Reaction Energetics

5.1 Atomic structure and periodicity

An atom contains a positively charged nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons occupying quantised energy states. Atomic number identifies the element; isotopes differ in neutron number. Electron configurations help explain periodic trends and chemical behaviour. Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally increases, influencing atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity. Down a group, additional occupied shells and shielding influence these properties. Trends are useful patterns, not substitutes for explaining the electronic structure of a particular element.

5.2 Chemical bonding

Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, often formed through electron transfer. Covalent bonding involves shared electron pairs. Metallic bonding is described through positive ion cores and delocalised electrons. Real bonding can have mixed character. Molecular shape can be predicted approximately using electron-pair repulsion, while molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and geometry. Intermolecular forces—including dispersion forces, dipole-dipole interactions and hydrogen bonding—help explain boiling points, solubility and material properties.

Ionic: cation (+) ⇄ electrostatic attraction ⇄ anion (−)
Covalent: atom — shared electron pair — atom
Metallic: positive ion cores within a mobile electron sea

5.3 Amount of substance and stoichiometry

The mole links particle number to measurable mass. Molar mass converts mass to amount of substance. A balanced chemical equation expresses conservation of atoms and provides mole ratios for quantitative calculations. Limiting reagents determine the maximum amount of product; excess reagents remain after the limiting reagent is consumed. Percentage yield compares actual yield with theoretical yield, while atom economy considers the proportion of reactant atoms incorporated into the desired product.

Worked example: For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to produce two moles of water. If 3.0 mol H₂ reacts with 2.0 mol O₂, hydrogen is limiting because 3.0 mol H₂ requires 1.5 mol O₂. The theoretical water yield is 3.0 mol.

5.4 Enthalpy and reaction profiles

Enthalpy change describes heat transfer at constant pressure for a defined process. Exothermic reactions release heat to surroundings and have negative enthalpy change under the usual sign convention; endothermic reactions absorb heat and have positive enthalpy change. Activation energy is the energy barrier to reaction. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated overall; it does not change the reaction enthalpy or equilibrium constant.

5.5 Equilibrium and reaction rates

At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, so macroscopic concentrations remain constant under fixed conditions. Equilibrium does not mean equal concentrations. The equilibrium constant expresses the ratio of product and reactant activities or concentrations under a specified convention and temperature. Le Chatelier's principle predicts the direction of response to a disturbance, but quantitative equilibrium calculations are needed to determine the final composition. Reaction rate depends on concentration, temperature, surface area, catalysts and reaction mechanism.

5.6 Acids, bases and buffers

Arrhenius, Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis theories describe acids and bases from different perspectives. pH is related to hydrogen-ion activity and is commonly approximated using concentration in dilute solutions. Strong acids ionise extensively in water; weak acids establish equilibria. Buffer solutions resist pH changes through a weak acid and its conjugate base, within a useful capacity range. Buffer performance depends on component concentrations and the ratio of conjugate pair forms.

Chapter 6: Physics — Mechanics, Waves and Thermodynamics

6.1 Motion and reference frames

Mechanics describes motion and the interactions that change it. Displacement is a vector change in position; distance is the total path length. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, while speed is the magnitude of velocity. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. A description of motion depends on a reference frame, and two observers moving relative to one another may assign different coordinates and velocities to the same event.

6.2 Newtonian dynamics

Newton's laws relate forces to motion. In an inertial frame, an object maintains constant velocity when the resultant external force is zero. The net force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass this becomes F = ma. Forces arise from interactions and act on different bodies in equal and opposite pairs. Common forces include weight, normal contact force, friction, tension, drag and spring force. A free-body diagram identifies the forces acting on one chosen body.

Worked example: A 4 kg trolley experiences a net horizontal force of 12 N on a low-friction track. Its acceleration is a = F/m = 12/4 = 3 m s⁻². If friction is not negligible, the applied force must first be combined with friction to find the net force.

6.3 Work, energy and momentum

Work is energy transferred by a force acting through displacement. For a constant force at angle θ to displacement, W = Fs cos θ. Kinetic energy is one-half mv²; gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface is approximately mgh for a suitably small height range. Conservation of energy states that total energy is conserved, although energy may transfer between system and surroundings. Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity; impulse equals change in momentum. Momentum conservation applies to an appropriately isolated system.

6.4 Circular motion and gravitation

Uniform circular motion involves acceleration toward the centre even when speed is constant, because velocity direction changes. The required centripetal acceleration is v²/r. Gravitation is an attractive interaction between masses; Newton's law of universal gravitation gives a force proportional to the product of masses and inversely proportional to separation squared. Orbital motion can be understood through gravitational attraction and tangential velocity. Models should distinguish circular approximations from elliptical or perturbed orbits.

6.5 Oscillations and waves

Simple harmonic motion occurs when restoring acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium. Period, frequency, amplitude and phase describe oscillatory motion. Waves transfer energy and information without requiring net transport of the medium over a complete cycle. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to propagation; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to propagation. Wave speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength. Reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference describe important wave behaviours.

Transverse wave: crest — equilibrium — trough; amplitude is maximum displacement; wavelength is distance between successive in-phase points; wave speed v = fλ.

6.6 Thermodynamics

Temperature relates to thermal state and, in kinetic models, to average microscopic energy. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. The first law of thermodynamics expresses energy conservation for a thermodynamic system: change in internal energy equals heat added to the system minus work done by the system under one common sign convention. The second law introduces entropy and constrains spontaneous processes; heat does not spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work.

6.7 Heat transfer and thermal properties

Heat transfer occurs through conduction, convection and radiation. Thermal conductivity characterises conductive heat transfer in a material; specific heat capacity describes energy required to raise unit mass by one degree; latent heat describes energy transfer during a phase change without temperature change for a pure substance at fixed pressure. Engineering and environmental applications require boundary conditions, geometry and material data.

Chapter 7: Scientific Data, Statistics and Research Communication

7.1 Populations, samples and sampling

A population is the full set of units about which a study seeks to make a statement; a sample is the subset actually observed. Sampling frames may omit relevant units, creating coverage error. Random sampling gives known selection probabilities under a defined design, while convenience sampling may be useful for exploratory work but limits generalisation. Stratified sampling ensures representation of important subgroups; cluster sampling selects groups and can reduce field costs but may increase sampling error. Sample size should be justified by the research question, expected variability, effect size, design and ethical considerations.

7.2 Descriptive statistics

The mean is sensitive to extreme values; the median is more robust for skewed distributions. The mode identifies the most frequent value or category. Range, interquartile range, variance and standard deviation describe dispersion in different ways. A distribution should be examined visually before choosing a summary. Histograms show distribution shape; box plots show medians, quartiles and potential outliers; scatter plots show relationships between two quantitative variables. Outliers should be investigated, not automatically deleted.

7.3 Probability and distributions

Probability quantifies uncertainty under a defined model. Conditional probability describes the probability of an event given another event. Independence means that knowing one event occurred does not change the probability of the other under the model. The binomial distribution models a fixed number of independent trials with two outcomes and constant success probability; the normal distribution is a continuous symmetric model used in many settings, but real data should not be assumed normal without checking. Sampling distributions underpin statistical inference.

7.4 Estimation and hypothesis testing

An estimator is a rule for estimating a population parameter from sample data. Bias, variance, consistency and efficiency describe estimator properties. A confidence interval combines an estimate with uncertainty under specified assumptions. Hypothesis testing evaluates data against a null model; significance thresholds are conventions, not measures of scientific importance. Multiple testing increases false-positive risk unless addressed through design or analysis. Report effect estimates and uncertainty, not only p-values.

7.5 Correlation and regression

Correlation describes the strength and direction of a linear association for a specified measure. Regression models an outcome in relation to predictors and can support prediction or adjusted association analysis. Model assumptions include functional form, error structure and independence or other dependence conditions appropriate to the design. Extrapolation beyond observed data is risky. A regression coefficient is not automatically causal; causal interpretation requires design and assumptions that justify it.

7.6 Scientific figures and reproducible reports

A figure should answer a question. Use labels, units, legends and scale bars where relevant. Avoid three-dimensional chart effects, truncated axes that exaggerate differences and colour choices that exclude readers. Captions should explain sample size, experimental conditions, statistical summaries and abbreviations. Methods should be detailed enough to understand and, where possible, reproduce the work. Data dictionaries define variables; version control records analysis changes; code should be documented and checked.

7.7 Research proposal structure

A proposal typically includes a concise title, background, problem statement, rationale, objectives, research questions or hypotheses, literature synthesis, methods, sampling, data analysis, ethical considerations, timeline, budget and references. Objectives should be achievable and aligned with methods. A literature review should synthesise evidence and identify a genuine knowledge gap rather than list summaries. Feasibility includes access to equipment, expertise, data and permissions.

7.8 Sample examination paper

Section A – short answer. (1) Define a population and a sample. (2) State two differences between mean and median. (3) Explain why correlation does not prove causation. (4) Identify two features of a reproducible method section. (5) Define a confidence interval in context.

Section B – data interpretation. A class compares seed germination under three salinity levels using four replicate dishes per condition. Describe an appropriate table and graph; identify the experimental unit; state two controlled variables; explain why four replicates may provide limited precision; and propose an appropriate analysis after inspecting the distribution and design.

Section C – extended response. Design a study of how land-use change affects local insect diversity. Include a testable question, sampling strategy, controls or comparison sites, identification protocol, ethical and environmental safeguards, statistical plan, likely limitations and a justified conclusion format.
