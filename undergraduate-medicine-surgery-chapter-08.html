Core framework and conceptual foundations

Pharmacology studies how medicines interact with biological systems and how the body handles medicines. A safe therapeutic decision requires a defined indication, expected benefit, relevant contraindications, patient-specific factors, monitoring plan and review point. Drug choice cannot be separated from diagnosis, pregnancy status, age, renal and hepatic function, allergies, concurrent medicines, patient preferences, affordability and local guidance. Educational examples in this chapter do not constitute individual prescribing instructions.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

Pharmacokinetics is commonly organised as absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME). Bioavailability describes the fraction of an administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged; first-pass metabolism can reduce oral bioavailability. Volume of distribution relates the amount of drug in the body to plasma concentration. Clearance is the volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time; half-life depends on clearance and apparent volume of distribution under first-order conditions. Pharmacodynamics examines concentration-effect relationships, receptor affinity, efficacy, potency, agonism, antagonism and therapeutic index. Competitive antagonists may shift agonist dose-response curves, while non-competitive mechanisms can reduce maximal response. Adverse drug reactions may be dose-related, idiosyncratic, allergic or related to long-term exposure. Medication reconciliation identifies discrepancies at transitions of care. High-risk medicines require safeguards, clear documentation and monitoring. Antimicrobial selection must incorporate likely pathogen, infection site, resistance patterns, patient factors and stewardship principles; de-escalate when microbiological and clinical information permits.

Integrated clinical case

An older adult with chronic kidney disease is admitted with a new infection and has a long medication list. Describe a structured medication review: confirm medicines and allergies, identify renal clearance concerns, assess interactions and duplications, evaluate indication and duration, plan monitoring, and communicate changes at discharge. Do not select a specific regimen without clinical data and local guidance.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.
