Core framework and conceptual foundations

Physiology explains how integrated systems maintain internal conditions within ranges compatible with cellular function. Homeostasis is dynamic regulation, not a fixed state. A regulated variable is sensed by receptors, compared with a control target by an integrating system, and modified by effectors through negative feedback. Positive feedback is less common and amplifies a process until a defined endpoint, as in the propagation of an action potential or uterine contractions during labour. Distinguish the controlled variable from the mechanism that controls it, and distinguish a compensatory response from correction of the primary cause.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

Membrane potentials arise from ion gradients, selective permeability and electrogenic transport. The Na+/K+-ATPase maintains sodium and potassium gradients; ion channels determine rapid changes in membrane conductance. In excitable cells, depolarisation commonly involves voltage-gated sodium or calcium channels, while repolarisation involves potassium conductance. Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume; stroke volume is influenced by preload, afterload and contractility. Arterial pressure is related to cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, while local autoregulation adjusts tissue perfusion. Pulmonary gas exchange depends on ventilation-perfusion matching, diffusion capacity, alveolar ventilation and haemoglobin concentration. Renal filtration depends on glomerular hydrostatic and oncotic pressures and the filtration coefficient; tubular transport then modifies the filtrate. Acid-base interpretation requires pH, PaCO2 and bicarbonate considered together, followed by assessment of compensation and possible mixed disorders. Endocrine axes commonly use hypothalamic and pituitary signals with target-gland negative feedback.

Integrated clinical case

A person develops profuse diarrhoea and becomes dizzy on standing. Explain the physiological sequence linking extracellular fluid loss to reduced venous return, stroke volume, cardiac output, baroreceptor responses and renal sodium-water conservation. Separate rapid neural compensation from slower hormonal responses.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.

Focused physiology masterclass: mechanisms, equations and integrated homeostasis

How to study physiology. Physiology explains how living systems work. For each process, identify the regulated variable, sensor, integrating centre, effector, response and feedback signal. Distinguish negative feedback, which opposes a deviation, from positive feedback, which amplifies a process until a defined endpoint. A physiological value is not interpreted in isolation: age, posture, activity, hydration, medication, disease and measurement method can all affect it. Numerical values in teaching examples are illustrative; use the reference intervals and protocols adopted by the relevant laboratory or institution.

1. Homeostasis, control systems and steady state

Homeostasis is the maintenance of the internal environment within ranges compatible with cell function. It does not mean that every variable is fixed. Body temperature, plasma glucose, arterial pressure, plasma osmolality and ion concentrations fluctuate around regulated levels. A control system contains a sensor, an afferent pathway, a controller or integrating centre, an efferent pathway and an effector. For example, thermoreceptors provide information to hypothalamic networks; autonomic, behavioural and endocrine responses then alter heat production and heat loss.

Negative feedback is common in endocrine and cardiovascular regulation. When plasma glucose rises, pancreatic beta cells increase insulin secretion; insulin promotes glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues and storage pathways, helping reduce circulating glucose. As glucose falls, the stimulus for insulin secretion diminishes. Positive feedback is less common and usually operates over a limited interval: cervical stretch during labour can increase oxytocin release and uterine contractions, which increase stretch until delivery interrupts the loop. Feed-forward control anticipates a disturbance, as when autonomic activity changes around the onset of exercise.

Steady state and equilibrium are not synonymous. A steady state can be maintained while substances continuously enter and leave a compartment, provided rates are balanced. The body is an open system that consumes energy to maintain gradients and organised function. When ATP production fails, membrane gradients dissipate and cellular function deteriorates.

2. Membrane transport, electrochemical gradients and resting potential

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer containing channels, carriers, pumps and receptors. Simple diffusion moves a substance down its concentration gradient without a carrier-mediated energy requirement. Facilitated diffusion uses a membrane protein but still proceeds down an electrochemical gradient. Primary active transport directly couples transport to energy, commonly ATP hydrolysis; the sodium-potassium ATPase exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions per ATP molecule. Secondary active transport uses energy stored in an ion gradient established by primary transport, as in sodium-glucose cotransport.

Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane in response to an effective osmotic gradient. Tonicity describes the effect of a solution on cell volume and depends mainly on non-penetrating solutes. A hypertonic extracellular solution tends to draw water out of cells; a hypotonic solution tends to cause cellular swelling. Osmolarity counts the concentration of osmotically active particles, whereas effective tonicity depends on whether solutes cross the membrane over the relevant time period.

The resting membrane potential arises from unequal ion distributions and selective membrane permeability. In many excitable cells, resting permeability to potassium is relatively high, so potassium movement strongly influences the resting potential. The sodium-potassium ATPase maintains the gradients over time and is electrogenic, but it is not the sole immediate generator of the resting voltage. The Nernst equation estimates the equilibrium potential for one ion; the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz relationship considers multiple permeant ions and their relative permeabilities.

Worked concept: if extracellular potassium rises, the potassium equilibrium potential becomes less negative. The resting membrane potential may therefore become less negative (depolarise). The effect on excitability is not simply “more potassium means more firing”: sustained or severe depolarisation can inactivate voltage-gated sodium channels and impair action-potential generation. The outcome depends on magnitude, duration, cell type and other ionic conditions.

3. Action potentials, synapses and neuromuscular transmission

An action potential is a rapid, regenerative change in membrane voltage. In a typical neuronal axon, depolarisation to threshold opens voltage-gated sodium channels, producing a rapid inward sodium current. Sodium-channel inactivation and opening of voltage-gated potassium channels contribute to repolarisation. After-hyperpolarisation may follow because potassium conductance remains elevated briefly. The refractory period limits immediate re-excitation and helps ensure forward propagation. Myelination increases conduction velocity by enabling saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.

At a chemical synapse, an arriving action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium entry promotes vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. Neurotransmitter binds postsynaptic receptors and produces an excitatory or inhibitory response depending on receptor type and ionic conductance. Signal termination may involve enzymatic breakdown, reuptake or diffusion. At the skeletal neuromuscular junction, acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate, generating an end-plate potential that normally triggers a muscle action potential. Acetylcholinesterase rapidly hydrolyses acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft.

Clinical reasoning requires distinguishing a defect in nerve conduction, neurotransmitter release, postsynaptic receptor function and muscle membrane excitability. Weakness alone does not identify which component is affected. Laboratory tests and clinical examination must be interpreted in context by qualified professionals.

4. Skeletal muscle excitation-contraction coupling

A motor neuron action potential triggers acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction. The muscle action potential spreads over the sarcolemma and down transverse tubules. Voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptors in the T-tubule membrane communicate with ryanodine receptors in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, causing calcium release into the cytosol. Calcium binds troponin C, shifting the troponin-tropomyosin complex and exposing myosin-binding sites on actin. Myosin heads cycle through attachment, power stroke, detachment and re-cocking; ATP binding permits detachment, and ATP hydrolysis energises the myosin head for another cycle.

Relaxation requires calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA pumps and restoration of the regulatory state of the thin filament. ATP is therefore needed for cross-bridge cycling and calcium handling. Muscle force depends on motor-unit recruitment, firing frequency, muscle length, contraction velocity, fibre properties and fatigue. The length-tension relationship reflects overlap between actin and myosin filaments; force is reduced when sarcomeres are excessively shortened or stretched.

5. Cardiac electrophysiology and the cardiac cycle

Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node exhibit spontaneous diastolic depolarisation. Their action-potential upstroke depends primarily on calcium currents rather than the fast sodium current characteristic of many working atrial and ventricular myocytes. In working ventricular muscle, rapid depolarisation is followed by a plateau phase in which inward calcium current is balanced by outward potassium currents. This prolonged action potential and refractory period prevent sustained tetanic contraction and allow the heart to alternate contraction and filling.

The cardiac cycle links electrical events to pressure and volume changes. During ventricular filling, atrioventricular valves are open and semilunar valves closed. Atrial systole contributes to late ventricular filling. During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure rises after atrioventricular valve closure while all valves are closed. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic or pulmonary arterial pressure, semilunar valves open and ejection begins. During isovolumetric relaxation, semilunar valves close and ventricular pressure falls before atrioventricular valves open again. Heart sounds are associated principally with valve closure and the vibrations generated in the cardiovascular system.

Stroke volume is end-diastolic volume minus end-systolic volume. Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Ejection fraction is stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume and is one measure of systolic performance, but it does not capture every aspect of cardiac function. Preload relates to myocardial fibre stretch before contraction; afterload is the load against which the ventricle ejects; contractility describes intrinsic force generation at a given loading condition. These concepts interact rather than acting as independent switches.

Worked calculation: for a hypothetical heart rate of 70 beats/min and stroke volume of 70 mL/beat, cardiac output = 70 × 70 = 4,900 mL/min, or 4.9 L/min. This arithmetic illustrates the relationship; it is not a target value or a clinical assessment on its own.

6. Vascular physiology, pressure, flow and microcirculation

Blood flow through a vessel is influenced by the pressure difference across it and its resistance. In a simplified rigid cylindrical tube with laminar flow, Poiseuille's relationship predicts that flow is proportional to the fourth power of radius and inversely proportional to viscosity and vessel length. Real blood vessels are elastic, branching and actively regulated, and blood is not a perfectly Newtonian fluid under all conditions; the equation is a conceptual model rather than a complete description of circulation.

Mean arterial pressure is commonly approximated as cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance, with central venous pressure often small enough to be neglected in basic calculations. Arterioles are major resistance vessels and alter tissue perfusion through smooth-muscle tone. Capillary exchange is influenced by hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, permeability, surface area and lymphatic drainage. The traditional Starling model describes filtration and reabsorption forces; the endothelial glycocalyx and revised Starling principle emphasise that sustained reabsorption at the venous end is more limited than the classical simplified diagram suggests, with lymphatics returning filtered fluid to the circulation.

Local metabolic factors, endothelial mediators, myogenic responses and autonomic influences regulate vascular tone. Nitric oxide promotes vascular smooth-muscle relaxation through cyclic GMP signalling. Sympathetic alpha-adrenergic stimulation commonly causes vasoconstriction in many vascular beds, while beta-2 effects can contribute to vasodilation in selected tissues. The net response depends on receptor distribution, circulating mediators and local metabolic demand.

7. Respiratory mechanics, ventilation and gas exchange

Ventilation moves air between atmosphere and alveoli. During quiet inspiration, contraction of the diaphragm increases thoracic volume and lowers intrapleural pressure, allowing alveolar pressure to fall slightly below atmospheric pressure so air enters. Quiet expiration is largely passive as inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil reduces thoracic volume. Lung compliance describes the change in lung volume per unit change in transpulmonary pressure. Compliance is reduced when lungs are stiff and increased when elastic recoil is lost; compliance alone does not describe airway resistance or gas exchange.

Airway resistance is strongly influenced by airway radius, smooth-muscle tone, mucus, airway geometry and lung volume. In obstructive disease, expiratory flow limitation and air trapping may occur. In restrictive patterns, total lung capacity is reduced, although the underlying causes may be pulmonary, chest-wall or neuromuscular. Spirometry measures airflow and volume relationships; interpretation requires quality-controlled manoeuvres and appropriate reference equations.

Alveolar ventilation is the volume of fresh air reaching gas-exchanging regions per minute. It can be estimated as respiratory frequency multiplied by (tidal volume minus dead-space volume). Increasing respiratory rate does not necessarily improve alveolar ventilation if tidal volume becomes very small. Diffusion across the alveolar-capillary membrane depends on surface area, thickness, gas solubility and partial-pressure gradient. Ventilation-perfusion matching is essential: alveoli that are ventilated but poorly perfused and regions that are perfused but poorly ventilated both reduce gas-exchange efficiency.

8. Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport

Most oxygen is transported bound reversibly to haemoglobin; only a small fraction is dissolved in plasma. The oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve is sigmoidal because haemoglobin exhibits cooperative oxygen binding. Increased carbon dioxide, increased hydrogen ion concentration, increased temperature and increased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shift the curve to the right, facilitating oxygen unloading in metabolically active tissues. A left shift indicates increased affinity and may reduce tissue unloading at a given partial pressure. Oxygen content depends strongly on haemoglobin concentration and saturation, not just arterial oxygen partial pressure.

Carbon dioxide is transported dissolved, bound to proteins including haemoglobin, and predominantly as bicarbonate. In red blood cells, carbonic anhydrase catalyses the reversible conversion of carbon dioxide and water to carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate. The chloride shift supports bicarbonate movement between red cells and plasma. In the lungs, the reactions proceed in the direction that regenerates carbon dioxide for exhalation. The Haldane effect describes how oxygenation of haemoglobin in the lungs reduces its capacity to carry carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions, promoting carbon dioxide release.

9. Renal physiology, filtration and tubular handling

The nephron consists of a renal corpuscle and tubule. Glomerular filtration depends on the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the filtration barrier and the filtration coefficient. The filtration barrier includes fenestrated glomerular endothelium, glomerular basement membrane and podocyte slit diaphragms. The filtrate is modified along the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule and collecting duct through selective reabsorption and secretion.

The proximal tubule reabsorbs the largest fraction of filtered sodium and water, along with glucose, amino acids and bicarbonate under normal conditions. The loop of Henle establishes a medullary osmotic gradient through countercurrent multiplication: the thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs sodium, potassium and chloride but is relatively impermeable to water, while the descending limb is more water permeable. The vasa recta help preserve the medullary gradient through countercurrent exchange. Antidiuretic hormone increases collecting-duct water permeability through insertion of aquaporin-2 channels; aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in appropriate distal nephron cells.

Renin release from juxtaglomerular cells is influenced by renal perfusion pressure, macula densa sodium chloride delivery and sympathetic beta-1 stimulation. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system supports extracellular fluid volume and arterial pressure through effects on vascular tone, sodium handling and hormonal signalling. Atrial natriuretic peptide promotes natriuresis in response to atrial stretch. These systems interact with thirst, vasopressin and renal autoregulation.

10. Acid-base physiology and compensation

Arterial pH reflects the relationship between bicarbonate and dissolved carbon dioxide. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is commonly written as pH = 6.1 + log10([HCO3−] / (0.03 × PaCO2)), with units and constants appropriate to the conventional clinical formulation. Bicarbonate is regulated mainly by the kidneys, while carbon dioxide is regulated through alveolar ventilation. Chemical buffers, especially bicarbonate, proteins and phosphate, act rapidly; respiratory compensation occurs over minutes to hours; renal adaptation takes longer.

Primary acid-base disorders are metabolic acidosis, metabolic alkalosis, respiratory acidosis and respiratory alkalosis. Compensation moves pH towards normal but does not necessarily restore it completely. A measured pattern should be assessed for the primary process, expected compensation and possible mixed disorder. In metabolic acidosis, the anion gap can help classify causes, but interpretation depends on albumin, laboratory method and clinical context. Do not diagnose a disorder from one number without checking sample quality, oxygenation, electrolytes and the patient's condition.

Worked interpretation framework: first establish whether pH indicates acidemia or alkalemia; next inspect PaCO2 and bicarbonate to identify which variable moves in the direction that explains the pH; then assess whether compensation is appropriate using a recognised formula; finally consider an additional disorder and the clinical context. This is an educational sequence, not a substitute for supervised clinical interpretation.

11. Gastrointestinal, endocrine and metabolic integration

Gastrointestinal motility depends on smooth muscle, enteric neural circuits, interstitial cells of Cajal, autonomic inputs and hormones. Peristalsis coordinates contraction behind and relaxation ahead of a bolus; segmentation mixes intestinal contents and supports absorption. Gastric acid secretion by parietal cells is stimulated through histamine, gastrin and acetylcholine pathways and inhibited by somatostatin and other signals. Pancreatic bicarbonate neutralises gastric acid in the duodenum, while pancreatic enzymes and bile salts support digestion and absorption.

Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to glucose and other nutrient signals. It promotes glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue through GLUT4 translocation, supports glycogen and lipid synthesis and suppresses hepatic glucose output. Glucagon from alpha cells promotes hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. In fasting, counter-regulatory hormones including glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol and growth hormone help maintain fuel availability. The balance changes with feeding, fasting, exercise, illness and stress.

Endocrine signalling uses peptide hormones, steroid hormones, amino-acid derivatives and other mediators. Peptide hormones commonly bind cell-surface receptors and activate second-messenger pathways; steroid hormones often bind intracellular receptors that regulate gene transcription. Hormone concentration reflects synthesis, secretion, transport, metabolism and clearance. Feedback loops may involve hypothalamus, pituitary and target gland, as in the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axes. Negative feedback at multiple levels helps stabilise output.

12. Temperature, exercise and integrated stress responses

During exercise, skeletal muscle ATP demand rises. Cardiac output increases through changes in heart rate and stroke volume, while blood flow is redistributed according to tissue demand and thermoregulatory needs. Ventilation rises to support gas exchange. At moderate workloads, oxygen delivery and extraction increase; at higher intensity, lactate production and acid-base changes become more prominent. Lactate is a metabolic intermediate and fuel, not simply a waste product or a direct measure of tissue hypoxia.

Thermoregulation balances heat production with heat loss by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation. Hypothalamic circuits coordinate sweating, cutaneous blood flow and behavioural responses. Evaporative cooling becomes less effective in humid environments. Fever involves a regulated increase in hypothalamic temperature set-point mediated by inflammatory signalling, whereas hyperthermia reflects heat accumulation without an elevated set-point. This distinction is important in physiology and clinical education.

13. Integrated clinical physiology cases

Case A — dehydration and orthostatic symptoms. A simulated learner scenario describes reduced fluid intake, thirst and dizziness on standing. Students map the response through reduced effective circulating volume, baroreceptor unloading, increased sympathetic activity, renin release and vasopressin secretion. They distinguish compensatory mechanisms from proof of a specific diagnosis and list measurements that would be required in a real clinical assessment.

Case B — exercise and cardiac output. A healthy adult begins dynamic exercise. Students predict changes in heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output, venous return, skeletal-muscle blood flow and ventilation. They explain why cardiac output rises and why local metabolic vasodilation can coexist with sympathetic vasoconstriction in selected vascular beds.

Case C — acid-base disturbance. A teaching blood-gas dataset is supplied by an instructor. Students identify acidemia or alkalemia, determine the likely primary process, calculate expected compensation using an approved teaching formula and consider whether a mixed disorder is possible. No treatment recommendation is inferred from the exercise.

Case D — impaired oxygen delivery. Students compare two hypothetical individuals with the same arterial oxygen partial pressure but different haemoglobin concentrations. They explain why oxygen content and delivery may differ, using the relationship between haemoglobin, saturation, dissolved oxygen and cardiac output.

14. Quantitative physiology: core equations and interpretation

  • Cardiac output: CO = heart rate × stroke volume.
  • Stroke volume: SV = end-diastolic volume − end-systolic volume.
  • Ejection fraction: EF = SV / end-diastolic volume × 100%.
  • Mean arterial pressure (approximation): MAP ≈ cardiac output × systemic vascular resistance, when central venous pressure is small.
  • Alveolar ventilation: V̇A = respiratory rate × (tidal volume − dead-space volume).
  • Minute ventilation: V̇E = respiratory rate × tidal volume.
  • Oxygen content: CaO2 ≈ (1.34 × Hb × SaO2) + (0.003 × PaO2), with conventional units and saturation expressed as a fraction.
  • Oxygen delivery: DO2 = cardiac output × arterial oxygen content, with unit conversion as required.
  • Clearance: Cx = (urine concentration of x × urine flow rate) / plasma concentration of x.
  • Filtered load: filtered load = GFR × plasma concentration, with unit consistency.

In every calculation, write the equation, substitute values with units, show the arithmetic, convert units where necessary and interpret the result. A mathematically correct result can still be physiologically misleading if assumptions, units or sampling conditions are ignored.
