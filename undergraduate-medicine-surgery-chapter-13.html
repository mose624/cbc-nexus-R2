Core framework

Specialty surgery applies common principles to different anatomical regions and disease processes. The first task in trauma is to identify and treat immediately life-threatening problems using a systematic primary survey, with reassessment after each intervention and early activation of appropriate support. A normal-looking external injury does not exclude internal haemorrhage or organ damage. Mechanism of injury, physiological observations, examination and imaging are integrated rather than interpreted separately.

Mechanisms and clinical interpretation

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion and may be hypovolaemic, distributive, cardiogenic or obstructive; these categories guide the search for mechanism but can overlap. Haemorrhage control, appropriate resuscitation and definitive treatment depend on injury pattern and local trauma systems. Breast assessment combines history, clinical examination and appropriate imaging, with tissue diagnosis when indicated; suspicious lesions require coordinated multidisciplinary assessment. Peripheral arterial disease reflects atherosclerotic narrowing and may cause exertional claudication, rest pain, tissue loss or acute limb ischaemia. Acute limb ischaemia is time-critical; assess pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis and temperature, while recognising that findings evolve. Urological emergencies include obstructed infected systems, acute urinary retention, testicular torsion and major trauma. Orthopaedic assessment requires neurovascular examination, assessment of skin and soft tissue, fracture alignment and compartment syndrome risk. Cancer surgery is planned around diagnosis, staging, resectability, margins, nodal assessment, patient fitness and multidisciplinary treatment intent.

Integrated clinical case

A patient arrives after a road traffic collision with tachycardia, cool extremities and abdominal tenderness. Describe the priorities of a structured trauma assessment, explain why occult haemorrhage must be considered, and identify the need for repeated reassessment and definitive haemorrhage control by the trauma team.

Reasoning sequence: identify immediate safety issues; separate reported symptoms from observed findings; form a concise problem representation; explain the mechanism; consider alternatives; identify useful evidence; and state what requires qualified clinical review. A teaching vignette is not a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan.
