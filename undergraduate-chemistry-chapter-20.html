Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Choose the system boundary before doing a calculation. An open system exchanges matter and energy; a closed system exchanges energy but not matter; an isolated system exchanges neither. Intensive variables such as T and P do not scale with sample size, whereas extensive variables such as U, H and S do. State variables specify equilibrium state; heat and work describe energy transfer along a path.

At equilibrium, macroscopic properties are time-independent and there are no net thermal, mechanical or chemical driving forces. Thermal equilibrium means uniform temperature; mechanical equilibrium means pressure forces balance; chemical equilibrium means chemical potentials satisfy the relevant constraints. A metastable state can persist despite not being the global equilibrium state.

The chemistry sign convention is ΔU=q+w, where q is positive when heat enters the system and w is positive when work is done on it. For expansion against constant external pressure, w=−PextΔV. Thus expansion gives negative work for the system. Always identify the boundary and sign convention before inserting numbers.

Enthalpy H=U+PV is especially useful for processes at constant pressure. For a closed system with only PV work, qp=ΔH. Heat capacities describe temperature response; Cp−Cv=R for an ideal gas on a molar basis. Heat capacity can vary with temperature, so constant-C approximations should be stated.

Calorimetry uses a measured temperature change to infer heat transfer. In a coffee-cup calorimeter, qsolution=mcΔT and qreaction is approximately the negative of this value. A bomb calorimeter measures heat at constant volume, giving ΔU; for ideal-gas reactions ΔH=ΔU+ΔngRT.

Hess's law follows because enthalpy is a state function. Reverse a thermochemical equation and reverse its enthalpy sign; multiply an equation and multiply its enthalpy by the same factor. Standard reaction enthalpy is ΣνΔfH°(products)−ΣνΔfH°(reactants). Formation enthalpies refer to formation from elements in their standard states.

Entropy is a state function related to the number and distribution of accessible microstates. For a reversible step dS=δqrev/T. Expansion, mixing and many phase changes increase entropy, but the sign of ΔSsystem alone does not decide spontaneity. Include the surroundings or use Gibbs energy at constant T and P.

The second law requires ΔSuniverse≥0 for a spontaneous or equilibrium process. At equilibrium ΔSuniverse=0 for an infinitesimal reversible change. The third law sets the entropy of a perfect crystal at 0 K to zero, enabling absolute standard molar entropies and entropy changes to be tabulated.

Gibbs energy G=H−TS gives the spontaneity criterion at constant T and P. ΔG<0 is forward-favourable, ΔG>0 reverse-favourable, and ΔG=0 equilibrium. A favourable ΔG does not imply a fast reaction; kinetics is governed by activation barriers and mechanism.

When ΔH and ΔS are nearly constant, ΔG(T)=ΔH−TΔS. If both are positive, the process becomes favourable above T*=ΔH/ΔS; if both are negative, it is favoured below that crossover. Use kelvin and consistent units, and account for heat-capacity effects over wide temperature intervals.

Chemical potential μi=(∂G/∂ni)T,P,nj is the partial molar Gibbs energy. It measures how G changes when a small amount of component i is added. Diffusion, phase transfer and reaction proceed in response to chemical-potential gradients. Activities, not raw concentrations, provide the rigorous composition variables.

For a reaction, ΔrG=ΔrG°+RTlnQ. Q is built from dimensionless activities raised to stoichiometric powers. At equilibrium Q=K and ΔrG=0, so ΔrG°=−RTlnK. K is temperature-dependent but independent of initial amounts; the equilibrium composition depends on mass balance and initial conditions.

Le Châtelier's principle is a useful qualitative guide: changing concentration or pressure perturbs Q and the system responds toward a new equilibrium. A catalyst speeds both directions and does not change K. Temperature changes K because it changes the relative thermodynamic stability of reactants and products.

The phase rule F=C−P+2 gives the number of independent intensive variables for a non-reacting system at equilibrium. A one-component single-phase region has two degrees of freedom; a two-phase boundary has one; a triple point has none. Phase diagrams show stable regions and coexistence boundaries.

Real mixtures are non-ideal because molecules interact. Activity ai=γixi (or an appropriate concentration-based form) corrects ideal composition; fugacity plays a similar role for gases. The standard state and activity-coefficient convention must be stated when using equilibrium constants.
