Detailed topic-by-topic lecture development

These expanded notes explain the underlying models, equations, assumptions and applications for each topic in the chapter.

Choose the system boundary before doing a calculation. An open system exchanges matter and energy; a closed system exchanges energy but not matter; an isolated system exchanges neither. Intensive variables such as T and P do not scale with sample size, whereas extensive variables such as U, H and S do. State variables specify equilibrium state; heat and work describe energy transfer along a path.

At equilibrium, macroscopic properties are time-independent and there are no net thermal, mechanical or chemical driving forces. Thermal equilibrium means uniform temperature; mechanical equilibrium means pressure forces balance; chemical equilibrium means chemical potentials satisfy the relevant constraints. A metastable state can persist despite not being the global equilibrium state.

The chemistry sign convention is ΔU=q+w, where q is positive when heat enters the system and w is positive when work is done on it. For expansion against constant external pressure, w=−PextΔV. Thus expansion gives negative work for the system. Always identify the boundary and sign convention before inserting numbers.

Enthalpy H=U+PV is especially useful for processes at constant pressure. For a closed system with only PV work, qp=ΔH. Heat capacities describe temperature response; Cp−Cv=R for an ideal gas on a molar basis. Heat capacity can vary with temperature, so constant-C approximations should be stated.

Calorimetry uses a measured temperature change to infer heat transfer. In a coffee-cup calorimeter, qsolution=mcΔT and qreaction is approximately the negative of this value. A bomb calorimeter measures heat at constant volume, giving ΔU; for ideal-gas reactions ΔH=ΔU+ΔngRT.

Hess's law follows because enthalpy is a state function. Reverse a thermochemical equation and reverse its enthalpy sign; multiply an equation and multiply its enthalpy by the same factor. Standard reaction enthalpy is ΣνΔfH°(products)−ΣνΔfH°(reactants). Formation enthalpies refer to formation from elements in their standard states.

Entropy is a state function related to the number and distribution of accessible microstates. For a reversible step dS=δqrev/T. Expansion, mixing and many phase changes increase entropy, but the sign of ΔSsystem alone does not decide spontaneity. Include the surroundings or use Gibbs energy at constant T and P.

The second law requires ΔSuniverse≥0 for a spontaneous or equilibrium process. At equilibrium ΔSuniverse=0 for an infinitesimal reversible change. The third law sets the entropy of a perfect crystal at 0 K to zero, enabling absolute standard molar entropies and entropy changes to be tabulated.

Gibbs energy G=H−TS gives the spontaneity criterion at constant T and P. ΔG<0 is forward-favourable, ΔG>0 reverse-favourable, and ΔG=0 equilibrium. A favourable ΔG does not imply a fast reaction; kinetics is governed by activation barriers and mechanism.

When ΔH and ΔS are nearly constant, ΔG(T)=ΔH−TΔS. If both are positive, the process becomes favourable above T*=ΔH/ΔS; if both are negative, it is favoured below that crossover. Use kelvin and consistent units, and account for heat-capacity effects over wide temperature intervals.

Chemical potential μi=(∂G/∂ni)T,P,nj is the partial molar Gibbs energy. It measures how G changes when a small amount of component i is added. Diffusion, phase transfer and reaction proceed in response to chemical-potential gradients. Activities, not raw concentrations, provide the rigorous composition variables.

For a reaction, ΔrG=ΔrG°+RTlnQ. Q is built from dimensionless activities raised to stoichiometric powers. At equilibrium Q=K and ΔrG=0, so ΔrG°=−RTlnK. K is temperature-dependent but independent of initial amounts; the equilibrium composition depends on mass balance and initial conditions.

Le Châtelier's principle is a useful qualitative guide: changing concentration or pressure perturbs Q and the system responds toward a new equilibrium. A catalyst speeds both directions and does not change K. Temperature changes K because it changes the relative thermodynamic stability of reactants and products.

The phase rule F=C−P+2 gives the number of independent intensive variables for a non-reacting system at equilibrium. A one-component single-phase region has two degrees of freedom; a two-phase boundary has one; a triple point has none. Phase diagrams show stable regions and coexistence boundaries.

Real mixtures are non-ideal because molecules interact. Activity ai=γixi (or an appropriate concentration-based form) corrects ideal composition; fugacity plays a similar role for gases. The standard state and activity-coefficient convention must be stated when using equilibrium constants.

Key equations and fully worked calculations

Gibbs energy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Use joules consistently when multiplying entropy by temperature.

Reaction equilibrium: ΔG° = −RT ln K; at equilibrium Q = K and ΔG = 0.

Calorimetry: q = mcΔT; for an isolated reaction-plus-solution arrangement, qreaction = −qsolution.

Worked calculation A: Gibbs energy and spontaneity

A reaction has ΔH° = −92.2 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −198 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 298 K. Convert entropy to −0.198 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

ΔG° = −92.2 − [298 × (−0.198)] = −33.2 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 s.f.). The negative value means the forward reaction is thermodynamically favourable at 298 K under standard-state conditions; it says nothing by itself about the rate.

Worked calculation B: equilibrium constant

At 298 K, ΔG° = −5.70 kJ mol⁻¹. Using ln K = −ΔG°/RT: ln K = 5700/(8.314 × 298) = 2.30, so K ≈ e²·³⁰ = 9.97, approximately 10.0.

The equilibrium mixture favours products relative to the stated standard states, but the actual composition also depends on the initial mixture and stoichiometry.

Worked calculation C: coffee-cup calorimetry

100.0 g of solution warms by 2.40 K. Take c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and neglect the calorimeter heat capacity: qsolution = 100.0 × 4.18 × 2.40 = 1003 J = 1.00 kJ.

Therefore qreaction ≈ −1.00 kJ for the measured event. Divide by the amount reacted to obtain a molar enthalpy, and state the approximation.

Detailed university-level treatment, derivations and applications

Advanced derivations and thermodynamic interpretation

1. Deriving the Gibbs energy criterion

For a system in contact with a large reservoir at fixed temperature and pressure, the combined entropy change is ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr. If the surroundings exchange heat reversibly at temperature T, ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys/T at constant pressure. Therefore TΔSuniv = TΔSsys − ΔHsys = −(ΔHsys − TΔSsys) = −ΔGsys. Since a spontaneous change has ΔSuniv > 0, it follows that ΔGsys < 0. At equilibrium ΔG = 0. This derivation applies to constant T and P and assumes pressure–volume work is the only relevant non-expansion work.

2. Deriving the reaction quotient relation

For each species i, chemical potential is μi = μi° + RT ln ai, where ai is its dimensionless activity relative to the selected standard state. For a reaction with stoichiometric numbers νi (positive for products and negative for reactants), ΔrG = Σνiμi. Substitution gives ΔrG = Σνiμi° + RTΣνi ln ai = ΔrG° + RT ln(Πai^νi) = ΔrG° + RT ln Q. At equilibrium ΔrG = 0 and Q = K, so ΔrG° = −RT ln K.

3. Temperature and spontaneity

At fixed pressure, the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation is (∂(G/T)/∂T)p = −H/T². In introductory applications, if ΔH° and ΔS° are approximately constant, ΔG°(T) ≈ ΔH° − TΔS°. The crossover temperature is T* = ΔH°/ΔS° when the ratio is positive. Do not use this estimate across phase transitions or broad temperature ranges without heat-capacity corrections.

4. Phase equilibrium

At phase equilibrium the chemical potentials of a component in the coexisting phases are equal. The Clapeyron equation follows by differentiating this equality along the phase boundary: dP/dT = ΔHtrans/(TΔVtrans). For liquid–vapour equilibrium, if vapour behaves ideally, liquid volume is negligible and ΔHvap is constant, integration gives ln(P2/P1) = −ΔHvap/R(1/T2 − 1/T1), the integrated Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Fully worked numerical exercises

Worked numerical problem 1: Calculate ΔG° from enthalpy and entropy

Problem: A reaction has ΔH° = +35.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +120 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 298 K.

Solution: Convert entropy: 120 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.120 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. ΔG° = 35.0 − 298(0.120) = −0.76 kJ mol⁻¹. It is slightly favourable at 298 K under standard conditions. The small magnitude means modest changes in temperature or activities may affect direction.

Worked numerical problem 2: Find the crossover temperature

Problem: For ΔH° = 48.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = 150 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, estimate the temperature where ΔG° changes sign.

Solution: T* = ΔH°/ΔS° = 48000/150 = 320 K. Under the constant-ΔH and constant-ΔS approximation, ΔG° becomes negative above 320 K. State that heat-capacity changes and phase changes may shift this estimate.

Worked numerical problem 3: Calculate K from standard Gibbs energy

Problem: At 298 K, ΔG° = −11.4 kJ mol⁻¹. Determine K.

Solution: ln K = −ΔG°/RT = 11400/(8.314×298) = 4.60. Thus K = e^4.60 ≈ 99.5. Products are favoured relative to standard-state activities, but K is not a rate constant.

Worked numerical problem 4: Use Q to predict reaction direction

Problem: For a reaction ΔG° = +5.00 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K and Q = 0.0100, calculate ΔrG.

Solution: ΔrG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = 5000 + (8.314×298)ln(0.0100) = 5000 − 11400 = −6.40 kJ mol⁻¹. Despite positive ΔG°, the actual mixture proceeds forward because Q is far below K.

Worked numerical problem 5: Calorimetric enthalpy

Problem: A 0.0250 mol reaction warms 150.0 g of solution by 1.85 K; c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Neglect calorimeter heat capacity.

Solution: qsolution = mcΔT = 150.0×4.18×1.85 = 1160 J. qreaction = −1.160 kJ. ΔrH ≈ −1.160/0.0250 = −46.4 kJ mol⁻¹. The result assumes negligible heat exchange with the environment.

Worked numerical problem 6: Clausius–Clapeyron vapour pressure

Problem: A liquid has ΔHvap = 40.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and P1 = 20.0 kPa at 300 K. Estimate P2 at 320 K.

Solution: ln(P2/P1)=−40000/8.314(1/320−1/300)=1.002. P2=20.0e^1.002=54.5 kPa. This is an approximate ideal-vapour estimate; ΔHvap is assumed constant.

Short-answer revision with model answers

Model answer 1

Question: Explain why negative ΔG is not a statement about reaction speed.

Answer: ΔG determines thermodynamic direction at fixed T and P. Rate depends on the activation free energy and mechanism. A reaction can have negative ΔG yet be kinetically slow if its activation barrier is high.

Model answer 2

Question: Derive ΔG° = −RT ln K.

Answer: Start from ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Q. At equilibrium ΔrG = 0 and Q = K. Rearrangement gives ΔrG° = −RT ln K.

Model answer 3

Question: Distinguish state functions from path functions.

Answer: U, H, S and G depend only on state and have path-independent changes. Heat and work depend on the path. This is why Hess's law applies to enthalpy but q and w cannot be inferred from endpoints separately.

Model answer 4

Question: Why are pure solids omitted from K?

Answer: Their activities are effectively unity in the conventional standard state, so they do not change the numerical reaction quotient as the amount of pure solid changes while that phase remains present.

Model answer 5

Question: State the assumptions behind the integrated Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Answer: The vapour is treated as ideal, liquid molar volume is neglected relative to vapour volume, and ΔHvap is treated as constant over the temperature interval.

Additional examination questions with explained answers

Model answer 6

Question: A reaction has ΔH°=−80 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS°=−100 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At what temperatures is it favourable?

Answer: ΔG°=−80+0.100T in kJ mol⁻¹. ΔG°<0 when T<800 K. This estimate assumes ΔH° and ΔS° remain constant and standard-state conditions apply.

Model answer 7

Question: Why can an endothermic reaction be spontaneous?

Answer: If ΔSsystem and/or ΔSsurroundings makes total entropy increase, the process can be spontaneous. At constant T and P, a sufficiently positive entropy change can outweigh positive ΔH through the −TΔS term.

Model answer 8

Question: Explain the difference between ΔG and ΔG°.

Answer: ΔG is the Gibbs energy change for the actual composition and equals ΔG°+RT ln Q. ΔG° refers to the chosen standard states. ΔG° alone does not tell the direction for a non-standard mixture.

Model answer 9

Question: A reaction has K=10⁻⁶. What does this say?

Answer: At equilibrium, the activity-based quotient is very small and reactants are strongly favoured relative to products under the stated standard states. It does not mean product concentration is exactly zero.

Model answer 10

Question: State the phase rule and apply it to a one-component two-phase system.

Answer: F=C−P+2. With C=1 and P=2, F=1, so one intensive variable (such as temperature or pressure) can be varied independently while maintaining phase equilibrium.
