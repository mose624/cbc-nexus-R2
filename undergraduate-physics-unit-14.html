1. Historical development and atomic spectra

Atomic spectroscopy began with the observation that excited gases emit light at discrete wavelengths. A prism or diffraction grating separates this radiation into lines rather than a continuous rainbow. The regularity of hydrogen's visible series suggested that atomic energy is quantised. In a transition from an initial state i to a lower state f, the photon energy is ΔE=Eᵢ−E_f=hν=hc/λ. Absorption reverses the process: a photon is absorbed only when its energy matches an allowed energy difference and the transition matrix element is non-zero. The spectrum therefore records differences between energy levels, not the absolute energy of a single level.

For hydrogen, the Rydberg relation 1/λ=R_H(1/n_f²−1/n_i²), with n_i>n_f, organises the observed series. The Lyman series terminates at n_f=1 and lies mainly in the ultraviolet; Balmer terminates at n_f=2 and includes visible lines; Paschen terminates at n_f=3 and lies in the infrared. The series limit corresponds to ionisation from the relevant lower level. Real spectra show fine structure, isotope shifts, hyperfine structure and external-field splitting beyond the simplest Bohr energy formula.

2. Hydrogen atom: Coulomb Hamiltonian and separation

The hydrogen atom consists of an electron and a proton interacting through the attractive Coulomb potential V(r)=−e²/(4πε₀r). Separating centre-of-mass and relative motion replaces the electron mass by the reduced mass μ=mₑm_p/(mₑ+m_p). The relative Hamiltonian is H=−(ħ²/2μ)∇²−e²/(4πε₀r). Because the potential depends only on r, the problem has spherical symmetry. Expressing the Laplacian in spherical coordinates permits separation ψ(r,θ,φ)=R(r)Y(θ,φ).

The angular equation produces spherical harmonics Y_l^m(θ,φ), eigenfunctions of L² and L_z. Their eigenvalues are l(l+1)ħ² and mħ, with l=0,1,… and m=−l,…,+l. The radial equation is commonly written for u(r)=rR(r), removing the first derivative: −(ħ²/2μ)u''+[l(l+1)ħ²/(2μr²)−e²/(4πε₀r)]u=Eu. The centrifugal term acts as an effective repulsive contribution for l>0. Normalisability at infinity and regularity at the origin restrict the allowed energies.

3. Quantum numbers, shells, subshells and orbital interpretation

The principal quantum number n controls the gross energy and characteristic radial scale. The orbital angular momentum quantum number l determines the magnitude of orbital angular momentum, L=√[l(l+1)]ħ, and is associated with subshell labels s, p, d, f for l=0,1,2,3. The magnetic quantum number m_l determines the measured z-component m_lħ. For each l there are 2l+1 possible m_l values. Including two electron spin states gives 2(2l+1) spin-orbitals in a subshell and 2n² states in a complete hydrogenic shell.

An orbital is a one-electron spatial wavefunction, not a classical path. The quantity |ψ|² is a probability density. A radial probability distribution includes the spherical volume element: for a normalised spherically symmetric state, P(r)dr=4πr²|ψ(r)|²dr; for a separated orbital, integrate |Y_l^m|² over angles to obtain the radial factor r²|R_nl|²dr. Thus the most probable radius, the mean radius and the radial density at a point are different concepts. Nodes are surfaces or regions where the wavefunction vanishes.

4. Radial wavefunctions, nodes and radial expectation values

The radial wavefunction determines how probability is distributed with distance from the nucleus. For hydrogenic states, the radial solution contains an exponential factor exp(−Zr/na₀), a polynomial in r, and an angular-momentum-dependent factor. Here a₀=4πε₀ħ²/(μe²) is the reduced-mass Bohr radius. The radial function R_nl is not itself a probability density; the radial probability per unit radius is proportional to r²|R_nl(r)|². This distinction explains why an s orbital may have a finite density at the nucleus while its radial probability is zero exactly at r=0 because the spherical shell volume vanishes there.

The number of radial nodes is n−l−1, while the angular nodal surfaces number l. Total nodes for a hydrogenic orbital are n−1. For example, 1s has no nodes, 2s has one radial node, 2p has one angular nodal plane, and 3p has one radial node plus one angular node. Nodes separate regions of opposite wavefunction sign. They are not regions where the electron is permanently absent in a classical sense; they are locations where the probability density for that stationary state is zero.

5. Many-electron atoms and central-field approximation

In a many-electron atom, each electron experiences nuclear attraction and repulsion from all other electrons. The Hamiltonian contains kinetic-energy terms, electron–nucleus Coulomb terms and pairwise electron–electron repulsion e²/(4πε₀r_ij). This interaction prevents exact separation into independent hydrogenic equations except for special approximations. The central-field model replaces the detailed many-electron environment by an effective spherically averaged potential. Electrons are then described approximately by orbitals with screened nuclear charge and quantum numbers.

Shielding reduces the effective attraction felt by an outer electron; penetration describes how much an orbital samples regions close to the nucleus. An s electron generally penetrates the core more strongly than a p electron of the same principal shell, and p more than d in common ordering arguments. Consequently, subshell energies in many-electron atoms are not determined by n alone. The Madelung n+l rule is a useful empirical filling guide, but exceptions occur because exchange, correlation and near-degeneracy alter energy ordering.

6. Pauli principle, electronic configurations and Hund rules

Electrons are spin-½ fermions, so the complete electronic wavefunction must be antisymmetric under exchange of two identical electrons. No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers n, l, m_l and m_s. This is the Pauli exclusion principle. It allows a spatial orbital to hold at most two electrons, with opposite spin projections. Aufbau filling gives a first approximation to ground-state configurations, while Hund's rules help determine the lowest term within an open subshell.

For a subshell with several degenerate orbitals, Hund's first rule favours maximum total spin S, subject to Pauli exclusion. The second rule favours maximum total orbital angular momentum L for a given spin in the usual LS-coupling regime. The third rule determines the preferred total J: for a subshell less than half-filled, the lowest J is generally lowest in energy; for more than half-filled, the highest J is generally lowest. These rules are approximate consequences of electrostatic and spin–orbit interactions, not universal replacements for solving the atomic Hamiltonian.

7. Orbital and spin angular momentum coupling

For a single electron, orbital angular momentum L and spin S combine to form total angular momentum J=L+S. The allowed values are J=|L−S|,…,L+S in integer steps. For many-electron atoms, individual orbital angular momenta may combine to total L and individual spins to total S before L and S couple. This is LS or Russell–Saunders coupling, often a good approximation for light atoms where electrostatic interactions dominate spin–orbit coupling.

In heavier atoms, spin–orbit interaction becomes stronger and individual electron total angular momenta j_i=l_i+s_i may couple first to total J. This is jj coupling. Real atoms can lie between the limiting schemes. The coupling scheme is a model for organising eigenstates and interpreting spectral patterns; it does not change the underlying quantum-mechanical requirement that states be eigenstates of the relevant full Hamiltonian.

8. Spin–orbit interaction and fine structure

An electron moving in the electric field of a nucleus experiences, in its rest-frame description, an effective magnetic field associated with relativistic transformation and Thomas precession. Its spin magnetic moment interacts with this field, producing spin–orbit coupling. A commonly used operator form is H_SO=ξ(r)L·S, where ξ(r) depends on the radial electric field and relativistic factors. Since L·S=[J²−L²−S²]/2, its expectation value can be evaluated in states labelled by l, s and j.

For a hydrogen-like atom, fine structure also includes relativistic kinetic-energy correction and the Darwin term for s states. The combined leading correction depends on n and j, lifting some degeneracies of the non-relativistic Coulomb model. The separation of fine-structure components generally grows strongly with nuclear charge, approximately with a high power of Z in hydrogenic scaling. Precision comparisons require reduced mass, radiative corrections and nuclear effects as well.

9. Zeeman effect: atoms in a magnetic field

A magnetic field interacts with orbital and spin magnetic moments. In the weak-field LS-coupling regime, the shift of a level is ΔE=μ_B g_J m_J B, with sign depending on the chosen Hamiltonian and magnetic-moment convention. The Bohr magneton is μ_B=eħ/(2mₑ). The Landé factor is g_J=1+[J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)]/[2J(J+1)] for J>0. The magnetic quantum number m_J takes values −J,…,+J.

A spectral transition between magnetic sublevels shifts by the difference between upper and lower level shifts. Electric-dipole selection rules include Δm=0 for π components and Δm=±1 for σ components, with ΔJ=0,±1 but not 0 to 0. The resulting pattern depends on Landé factors and the allowed sublevel transitions. In a strong field where spin and orbital angular momenta decouple, the Paschen–Back regime is more appropriate than the weak-field Landé formula.

10. Stark effect and electric-field shifts

The Stark effect is the change in atomic energy levels and spectra in an external electric field. The perturbation for an electron in a uniform field along z is H′=eEz for the electron's potential energy convention when the field direction is specified consistently. In a non-degenerate parity eigenstate, the first-order shift often vanishes because ⟨z⟩=0. The leading shift is then quadratic in field strength and can be expressed using the static polarizability: ΔE=−½αE².

Hydrogen has degeneracies within a principal shell in the ideal Coulomb model. The electric-field perturbation can mix degenerate states and produce a linear Stark effect after diagonalisation in the degenerate subspace. This illustrates why degeneracy must be treated before applying non-degenerate perturbation theory. The magnitude and pattern depend on the field direction, the basis states and the relevant matrix elements.

11. Hyperfine structure and isotope shifts

Hyperfine structure arises mainly from interaction between electronic angular momentum and nuclear magnetic dipole moment, with electric quadrupole contributions for nuclei with spin greater than one-half. The total atomic angular momentum is F=I+J, with F values from |I−J| to I+J. Hyperfine intervals are often described by an effective Hamiltonian involving I·J. The splitting is much smaller than ordinary fine structure because nuclear magnetic moments are much smaller than electronic magnetic moments.

Isotope shifts arise because isotopes differ in nuclear mass and charge distribution. The mass shift includes normal and specific contributions associated with finite nuclear mass and correlated electron motion. The field shift reflects changes in nuclear charge radius and the electron density near the nucleus. Comparing isotope spectra can therefore reveal nuclear structure information as well as atomic effects.

12. X-ray spectra and Moseley relation

Inner-shell vacancies created by energetic electrons or photons can be filled by electrons from higher shells, emitting characteristic X-rays. The Kα line commonly corresponds to a transition from an n=2 shell to the K shell n=1; Kβ involves a higher initial shell. A simplified hydrogenic screening model gives photon energy proportional to (Z−σ)²(1/n_f²−1/n_i²), where σ is an effective screening constant. This leads to Moseley's approximate relation √ν∝Z for a given spectral series.

Characteristic X-rays differ from bremsstrahlung, the continuous radiation produced when charged particles decelerate in the nuclear field. An X-ray tube therefore produces both a continuous background and sharp characteristic peaks. The short-wavelength cutoff of the continuous spectrum is governed by the maximum photon energy set by accelerating voltage, λ_min=hc/(eV). Increasing tube voltage changes the cutoff and can excite additional characteristic lines.

13. Born–Oppenheimer approximation and molecular structure

A molecule contains nuclei and electrons with very different masses. Because nuclei move much more slowly than electrons, molecular motion can often be separated approximately into electronic and nuclear parts. In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, solve the electronic Schrödinger equation for fixed nuclear positions. The resulting electronic energy as a function of nuclear geometry acts as a potential-energy surface for nuclear motion. Nuclear vibration and rotation then occur on this surface.

The approximation is not exact near electronic degeneracies, avoided crossings and conical intersections, where electronic and nuclear motions become strongly coupled. It is nevertheless the foundation of much molecular quantum chemistry and spectroscopy. A potential-energy curve for a diatomic molecule plots electronic energy against internuclear separation R. Its minimum defines the equilibrium bond length; the depth relative to dissociation is related to bond strength.

14. Molecular rotational spectra

A diatomic molecule can be approximated as a rigid rotor with moment of inertia I=μR_e², where μ is the nuclear reduced mass and R_e the equilibrium bond length. Its rotational Hamiltonian is L²/(2I), giving E_J=ħ²J(J+1)/(2I), with J=0,1,2,… . In wavenumber units, F(J)=BJ(J+1), where B=h/(8π²Ic) when B is expressed in inverse length using consistent units. Adjacent rotational transitions for a rigid heteronuclear diatomic molecule follow ΔJ=±1 and produce approximately equally spaced lines.

A molecule must have a permanent electric dipole moment for a pure electric-dipole microwave rotational spectrum. Homonuclear diatomic molecules such as N₂ and O₂ have no permanent dipole and do not show ordinary pure rotational electric-dipole spectra, although they can be studied by other spectroscopic methods. Centrifugal distortion causes deviations from equal spacing at higher J because the bond stretches as rotation becomes faster.

15. Molecular vibrational spectra and anharmonicity

For a diatomic molecule near equilibrium, the potential can be approximated by V(x)=½kx², where x=R−R_e and k is the force constant. The vibrational angular frequency is ω=√(k/μ), and harmonic oscillator energies are E_v=ħω(v+½). The ideal harmonic selection rule for infrared absorption is Δv=±1, provided the molecular dipole moment changes with vibrational displacement. The fundamental wavenumber is approximately (1/2πc)√(k/μ).

Real bonds are anharmonic. The Morse potential V(R)=D_e[1−exp(−a(R−R_e))]² has vibrational levels with decreasing spacing as v increases and a finite dissociation energy. Anharmonicity permits weak overtones such as Δv=2 and combination bands when dipole-moment expansion contains higher-order terms. The dissociation limit means only a finite number of bound vibrational levels exist.

16. Molecular electronic spectra and vibronic transitions

Electronic absorption promotes a molecule from one electronic state to another. Each electronic state contains vibrational and rotational sublevels, so a nominal electronic transition often appears as a band system rather than a single line. The Franck–Condon principle states that electronic transitions occur so rapidly relative to nuclear motion that nuclear coordinates are approximately unchanged during the transition. Transition intensities are governed by vibrational wavefunction overlap, called Franck–Condon factors.

An absorption band can show a progression because the equilibrium geometry of the excited electronic state differs from that of the ground state. The vertical transition on a potential-energy diagram is not generally the same as the difference between the two potential minima. After excitation, vibrational relaxation and internal conversion may occur before fluorescence. Emission often appears at lower photon energy than absorption, producing a Stokes shift.

17. Molecular orbital theory and bonding

Molecular orbital theory forms molecular orbitals from linear combinations of atomic orbitals. In the simplest two-orbital model, in-phase combination produces a bonding orbital with increased electron density between nuclei, while out-of-phase combination produces an antibonding orbital with a nodal plane or surface between nuclei. The energy splitting depends on overlap, orbital energies and symmetry. Molecular orbitals extend over the molecule and are occupied according to Aufbau, Pauli and Hund principles.

The bond order in a simple closed-shell diatomic molecular orbital picture is (N_b−N_a)/2, where N_b and N_a are the numbers of electrons in bonding and antibonding orbitals. A positive bond order indicates net bonding in this model. The ordering of σ and π orbitals changes among second-period homonuclear diatomics because s–p mixing differs with atomic number. This explains why a single memorised ordering should not be applied indiscriminately.

18. Selection rules and transition moments

The probability amplitude for an electric-dipole transition between states i and f is proportional to the transition dipole matrix element μ_fi=⟨ψ_f|μ̂|ψ_i⟩. If symmetry makes this integral zero, the transition is electric-dipole forbidden in the ideal model. For atomic orbital transitions, common rules include Δl=±1 and Δm=0,±1 for an electric-dipole transition, with spin usually conserved, ΔS=0. More complete rules include total angular momentum and parity constraints.

Forbidden does not always mean impossible. Magnetic-dipole and electric-quadrupole transitions can occur more weakly, and perturbations such as spin–orbit coupling, vibronic coupling or external fields can mix states and lend intensity. Molecular selection rules depend on point-group symmetry and the transformation properties of the dipole operator. Infrared activity requires a vibration to change dipole moment; Raman activity requires a change in polarisability.

19. Laser principles and atomic transitions

A laser requires a gain medium, pumping mechanism, population inversion and optical feedback or an equivalent resonator arrangement. Stimulated emission occurs when an incident photon induces an excited atom or molecule to emit a second photon with matching frequency, phase, direction and polarisation. In thermal equilibrium, stimulated emission is outweighed by absorption for a simple two-level system, so practical population inversion usually requires at least three or four effective levels or additional mechanisms.

The Einstein coefficients A and B describe spontaneous emission, stimulated emission and absorption. For two levels with degeneracies g₁ and g₂, detailed balance relates the coefficients and Planck's radiation law. The natural linewidth of a transition is connected to the excited-state lifetime through an energy-time relation; collisional and Doppler broadening can add to the observed width. The laser linewidth may be much narrower than the gain profile depending on resonator and operating conditions.

20. Spectroscopic instrumentation and line-shape analysis

A spectrometer contains a source or sample excitation arrangement, wavelength selector or dispersive element, detector and data system. A diffraction grating separates wavelengths according to d(sinα+sinβ)=mλ. Resolving power is commonly defined R=λ/Δλ, where Δλ is the smallest separation that can be distinguished under stated conditions. Calibration uses reference lines or standards; wavelength accuracy, resolution, stray light and detector response are distinct performance characteristics.

Observed line profiles are convolutions of physical and instrumental effects. Natural broadening is Lorentzian in the simplest lifetime model; Doppler broadening is Gaussian for a thermal velocity distribution; collisional broadening is often approximately Lorentzian. A Voigt profile is the convolution of Gaussian and Lorentzian contributions. Fit the profile rather than estimating width from a few points, and report the adopted full width at half maximum and uncertainty.

21. Atomic and molecular spectroscopy in research and applications

Atomic emission and absorption spectroscopy support elemental analysis, plasma diagnostics, astrophysics and environmental monitoring. Molecular rotational spectroscopy provides bond lengths and isotopic information; vibrational spectroscopy identifies functional groups and tracks chemical change; electronic spectroscopy probes chromophores, excited states and concentration. In astronomy, line wavelengths identify elements and shifts reveal radial motion through the Doppler effect. In laboratory plasmas, line ratios and widths can constrain temperature, density and electric fields when an appropriate collisional-radiative model is used.

Quantitative analysis requires calibration standards, blank measurements, matrix-effect assessment and a validated response model. Beer–Lambert behaviour A=εlc applies to suitable dilute, non-scattering samples and monochromatic light; deviations arise from chemical equilibria, stray light, concentration effects and instrumental bandwidth. Emission intensity is not automatically proportional to concentration because excitation and self-absorption can vary.

Worked numerical examples

Use the equations and assumptions developed above. Retain guard digits, state units, and explain the physical significance of each result.

Example 1. Hydrogen Balmer-α wavelength

For nᵢ=3 and n_f=2, 1/λ=R_H(1/4−1/9)=5R_H/36. With R_H≈1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, λ≈656.3 nm, in the red visible region.

Example 2. Hydrogen ground-state energy

E₁≈−13.6 eV. The energy required to ionise ground-state hydrogen is 13.6 eV, corresponding to a threshold wavelength λ=hc/E≈91.2 nm.

Example 3. Reduced mass correction

μ/mₑ=m_p/(m_p+mₑ)≈0.99946. The finite proton mass therefore makes the hydrogenic energy magnitude slightly smaller than the infinite-nuclear-mass approximation.

Example 4. Number of orbitals in n=3 shell

The allowed l values are 0,1,2. Their orbital counts are 1,3,5, totalling 9 spatial orbitals. Including two spin states gives 18 spin-orbitals.

Example 5. Radial nodes in 4p

For n=4 and l=1, radial nodes=n−l−1=2. Angular nodes=l=1, giving three total nodal surfaces or nodes under the usual counting.

Example 6. Rotational constant and bond length

If a diatomic has μ=1.14×10⁻²⁶ kg and R=1.10×10⁻¹⁰ m, I=μR²=1.38×10⁻⁴⁶ kg m². B in wavenumbers is h/(8π²Ic), approximately 20.3 m⁻¹ or 0.203 cm⁻¹.

Example 7. Vibrational frequency

For k=500 N m⁻¹ and μ=1.0×10⁻²⁶ kg, ν=(1/2π)√(k/μ)≈3.56×10¹³ Hz. The wavenumber is ν/c≈1187 cm⁻¹.

Example 8. Photon energy from a spectral line

For λ=500 nm, E=hc/λ≈3.97×10⁻¹⁹ J=2.48 eV. One mole of such photons carries about 239 kJ mol⁻¹.

Example 9. Zeeman shift estimate

For g_J=2, m_J=1 and B=0.50 T, |ΔE|=μ_Bg_Jm_JB≈9.27×10⁻²⁴ J, or 5.79×10⁻⁵ eV.

Example 10. X-ray cutoff voltage

At V=30 kV, λ_min=hc/(eV)≈4.13×10⁻¹¹ m=0.0413 nm. This is the short-wavelength limit of the bremsstrahlung continuum.

Example 11. Molecular bond force constant

If a measured vibrational wavenumber is 1600 cm⁻¹ and μ=1.1×10⁻²⁶ kg, use k=μ(2πcṽ)². Substitution gives k≈9.9×10² N m⁻¹.

Example 12. Spectrometer resolving power

At λ=600 nm with R=30,000, the minimum resolvable separation is Δλ=λ/R=0.020 nm. Lines closer than this may not be resolved under the stated criterion.

Laboratory investigations, data analysis and safety

1. Measure the hydrogen Balmer spectrum

Use a low-pressure hydrogen discharge tube, collimator, diffraction grating spectrometer and wavelength calibration lamp. Record angular positions for each visible line on both sides of the central image. For a grating of spacing d, apply d sinθ=mλ for normal incidence, identifying the diffraction order m. Repeat measurements, average symmetric angular readings and calculate wavelength. Compare with the Rydberg prediction. Estimate uncertainty by propagation from angular reading, grating spacing and calibration. Avoid viewing discharge sources directly and follow high-voltage handling procedures.

2. Determine a diatomic rotational constant

Obtain a calibrated microwave absorption spectrum of a suitable polar gas. Assign adjacent rotational transitions and plot transition wavenumber against J+1. For a rigid rotor, the transition from J to J+1 occurs at 2B(J+1) in wavenumber units. The slope gives 2B. Calculate I=h/(8π²cB), then R=√(I/μ). Discuss centrifugal distortion, pressure broadening, temperature-dependent line strengths and isotopic contributions. Report the fit residuals and uncertainty in B and derived bond length.

3. Infrared vibrational spectroscopy

Collect a background spectrum followed by the sample spectrum using an appropriate transmission or ATR arrangement. Convert the instrument axis to wavenumber and identify absorption bands. Compare observed band positions with expected functional-group regions, but do not assign a mode from position alone: consider coupling, hydrogen bonding, solvent and molecular symmetry. If isotopic substitution data are available, compare the shift with the reduced-mass prediction ν∝μ⁻¹ᐟ². State spectral resolution, number of scans, baseline procedure and sample preparation.

4. Zeeman splitting measurement

Observe a suitable atomic line in a controlled magnetic field using a high-resolution optical arrangement. Measure the field with a calibrated probe and determine component wavelengths or frequencies. Convert separations to energy shifts using ΔE=hcΔ(1/λ) for small wavelength intervals. Compare the pattern with allowed m_J transitions and Landé factors. Account for field gradients, polarisation, line blending, Doppler width and spectrometer resolution. This experiment requires authorised high-field and optical-laboratory controls.

5. Molecular potential and computational modelling

Use a quantum-chemistry package or a supplied potential-energy dataset to calculate energy as a function of internuclear separation. Locate the equilibrium distance by fitting near the minimum. Estimate the harmonic force constant from the second derivative k=(d²V/dR²) at Rₑ, then calculate the harmonic vibrational frequency. Compare with measured spectroscopy and discuss basis-set, electronic-correlation and Born–Oppenheimer errors. Demonstrate convergence by increasing basis quality or refining the numerical grid.

Uncertainty and laboratory reporting

For a derived quantity y=f(x₁,…,xₙ), independent uncertainties combine as u_y²=Σ(∂f/∂xᵢ)²uᵢ². Include covariance terms when variables share calibration or fitting parameters. Report raw observations, calibration standards, environmental conditions, fit model, residuals, repeatability, systematic limitations and final uncertainty. Do not quote more significant figures than the measurement supports. Distinguish standard uncertainty from expanded uncertainty and state the coverage factor if one is used.

Radiation, laser and electrical safety

Atomic spectroscopy may involve high voltage, intense ultraviolet light, lasers, hot sources and ionising X-rays. Use enclosed equipment, interlocks, shielding and authorised operating procedures. Wear eye protection rated for the relevant wavelength; ordinary spectacles are not laser safety eyewear. X-ray apparatus must remain shielded and operated only by trained personnel under local radiation rules. Never bypass an interlock, defeat a beam stop or handle energised equipment.

Common errors to avoid

  • Confusing radial wavefunction R(r) with radial probability r²|R(r)|².
  • Using the electron mass instead of reduced mass when precision requires it.
  • Assuming all hydrogenic degeneracies remain in real many-electron atoms.
  • Applying non-degenerate perturbation theory to a degenerate level without first diagonalising the perturbation.
  • Using weak-field Zeeman expressions in the Paschen–Back regime.
  • Calling a transition allowed solely because photon energy matches an energy gap.
  • Applying the rigid-rotor or harmonic-oscillator model beyond its range of validity.
  • Assuming every vibration is both IR and Raman active.
  • Confusing instrument resolution with wavelength accuracy.
  • Ignoring background, line broadening, calibration and uncertainty in spectral assignments.

University examination questions with model answers

For full credit, show the physical model, equations, assumptions, derivation steps and units. Diagrams should have labelled axes and clearly identified states.

1. Explain why atomic spectra contain discrete lines.

Model answer: Bound electrons occupy quantised stationary states. Photons are emitted or absorbed when transitions occur between allowed states, with hν equal to the energy difference. Only transitions with non-zero transition matrix elements have appreciable intensity.

2. Derive the hydrogenic energy dependence on n.

Model answer: Separate the Coulomb Schrödinger equation into radial and angular parts. Require regularity at r=0 and normalisability as r→∞. The radial series terminates only for discrete energies, yielding E_n=−μZ²e⁴/[2(4πε₀)²ħ²n²].

3. Distinguish n, l and m_l.

Model answer: n labels the principal shell and energy scale; l sets orbital angular momentum magnitude; m_l gives the component along a chosen axis. Their allowed integer ranges follow from regular angular solutions.

4. Why is r² included in radial probability?

Model answer: The spherical volume element is r²sinθ dr dθ dφ. Integrating angular probability leaves a radial probability measure proportional to r²|R_nl(r)|²dr.

5. State the Pauli exclusion principle.

Model answer: No two identical electrons in one atom can have the same complete set of four quantum numbers. The complete electronic state is antisymmetric under exchange.

6. What is a term symbol?

Model answer: ²S+1L_J gives spin multiplicity, total orbital angular momentum letter and total angular momentum quantum number. It labels an atomic level or term.

7. Explain spin–orbit splitting.

Model answer: The electron's spin magnetic moment interacts with an effective magnetic field associated with orbital motion and relativistic effects. The L·S interaction separates levels with different J.

8. Compare Zeeman and Stark effects.

Model answer: Zeeman splitting is caused by an external magnetic field interacting with magnetic moments; Stark shifts arise from an external electric field interacting with electric dipole structure and polarizability.

9. What causes hyperfine structure?

Model answer: Coupling of electronic angular momentum to nuclear spin and, where relevant, nuclear electric quadrupole moments.

10. What is Moseley's law used for?

Model answer: It relates characteristic X-ray frequencies to atomic number and established atomic number as a fundamental ordering quantity for elements.

11. State the Born–Oppenheimer approximation.

Model answer: Treat nuclei as fixed while solving the electronic problem, then use the electronic energy as a potential surface for nuclear motion. It is most reliable when electronic states are well separated.

12. Why do homonuclear diatomics lack ordinary pure rotational microwave spectra?

Model answer: They have no permanent electric dipole moment, so the electric-dipole rotational transition matrix element vanishes.

13. Derive the rigid-rotor energy levels.

Model answer: The rotational Hamiltonian is L²/(2I); since L² eigenvalues are J(J+1)ħ², E_J=ħ²J(J+1)/(2I).

14. What is the Franck–Condon principle?

Model answer: Electronic transitions occur much faster than nuclear displacement, so nuclear coordinates are approximately fixed during the transition. Intensities depend on vibrational overlap.

15. Distinguish IR and Raman activity.

Model answer: IR activity requires a change in dipole moment during vibration; Raman activity requires a change in polarisability.

16. Define molecular orbital bond order.

Model answer: In the simple MO model, bond order=(number of bonding electrons−number of antibonding electrons)/2.

17. What is a transition dipole moment?

Model answer: The matrix element ⟨f|μ̂|i⟩ coupling initial and final states through the electric-dipole interaction. Its vanishing makes the transition electric-dipole forbidden.

18. Why can a forbidden line still be observed?

Model answer: Higher multipole interactions or state mixing from spin–orbit, vibronic or external-field perturbations can give a weak transition intensity.

19. What is population inversion?

Model answer: A non-equilibrium population in which the upper laser level has sufficient population relative to the lower level to provide net optical gain.

20. Compare Gaussian and Lorentzian broadening.

Model answer: Doppler broadening is commonly Gaussian; lifetime and collision broadening are commonly Lorentzian. Their convolution is a Voigt profile.

21. What is spectrometer resolving power?

Model answer: R=λ/Δλ, where Δλ is the smallest wavelength separation resolvable under a stated criterion.

22. Explain isotope shifts.

Model answer: Changing isotope changes nuclear mass and charge distribution, altering reduced-mass and finite-nuclear-size contributions to energy levels.

23. Why are observed molecular vibrational levels not equally spaced?

Model answer: Real molecular potentials are anharmonic and approach a finite dissociation limit, so adjacent level spacings decrease with vibrational quantum number.

24. How can rotational spectroscopy determine bond length?

Model answer: Determine the rotational constant from line spacing, calculate moment of inertia I=h/(8π²cB), then use I=μR² to infer R.

25. List essential safeguards for X-ray spectroscopy.

Model answer: Use approved enclosed equipment, interlocks, shielding, authorised operating procedures and dosimetry where required; never bypass shielding or operate an uncontrolled beam.

Advanced applications and deeper derivations

1. Deriving the Rydberg constant from the Coulomb model

The hydrogenic energy can be written Eₙ=−hcR∞/n² when the nuclear mass is treated as infinite. Equating this with the Coulomb Schrödinger result gives the Rydberg constant in terms of fundamental constants. Finite nuclear mass replaces the electron mass with the reduced mass, producing an isotope-dependent Rydberg constant. For a transition nᵢ to n_f, subtract the two level energies: ΔE=hcR(1/n_f²−1/nᵢ²). Dividing by hc gives the wavenumber relation directly. This derivation shows why the Rydberg formula follows from quantised Coulomb bound states. Precision work additionally requires relativistic, radiative, recoil and nuclear-size corrections.

2. Deriving the rigid-rotor line spacing

For a rigid rotor, E_J=BhcJ(J+1) when B is a wavenumber. An electric-dipole transition changes J by one unit. The transition wavenumber from J to J+1 is [E_(J+1)−E_J]/hc=B[(J+1)(J+2)−J(J+1)]=2B(J+1). Adjacent lines are therefore separated by 2B in the ideal model. A plot of line position against J+1 is linear, with slope 2B. Once B is measured, I=h/(8π²cB), and R=√(I/μ). The derivation assumes fixed bond length, one isotopic species, a dipole-allowed transition and negligible centrifugal distortion. Curvature at high J indicates failure of the rigid-rotor approximation.

3. Deriving the harmonic vibrational frequency

Expand a molecular potential around equilibrium: V(R)=V(Rₑ)+V′(Rₑ)x+½V″(Rₑ)x²+… where x=R−Rₑ. At equilibrium V′(Rₑ)=0. Retaining the quadratic term identifies k=V″(Rₑ). The relative nuclear coordinate obeys μx¨=−kx, so x¨+(k/μ)x=0 and ω=√(k/μ). Quantum mechanics gives E_v=ħω(v+½). This connects curvature in the electronic potential-energy surface to a measured infrared band. Strong anharmonicity makes the frequency depend on vibrational level, so the harmonic estimate is only local near the minimum.

4. Deriving a first-order Zeeman pattern

In a weak magnetic field, treat the interaction as a perturbation on a state with good L, S and J. The magnetic moment projected along J is proportional to g_JJ, and the first-order energy shift is μ_Bg_Jm_JB in the common energy-shift convention. Since m_J ranges from −J to +J, one level splits into 2J+1 equally spaced magnetic sublevels. A transition shift is the upper-level shift minus the lower-level shift. Allowed Δm_J values of 0 and ±1 generate π and σ components. Different transitions can coincide in frequency, so the observed number of components may be smaller than the number of allowed transitions.

5. Franck–Condon factors as overlap probabilities

Within the Born–Oppenheimer picture, an electronic transition dipole can be approximated as slowly varying with nuclear coordinate. The vibronic transition amplitude then contains the overlap integral ∫χ′_v′(R)χ_v″(R)dR. Its squared magnitude is the Franck–Condon factor. If the two electronic potential minima are displaced, overlap is distributed across several excited vibrational levels, producing a progression of bands. If the minima nearly coincide, the 0–0 transition can dominate. This approximation explains many molecular absorption and emission envelopes, but needs refinement when the transition moment varies strongly with R or non-adiabatic coupling is important.

6. Line broadening: lifetime and uncertainty

An excited state with finite lifetime τ cannot have an exactly sharp energy. Exponential decay of amplitude in time produces a Lorentzian spectral profile, with natural energy width of order ħ/τ; exact full-width conventions depend on the lifetime definition. Thermal motion produces Doppler shifts: for a Maxwellian gas, the frequency distribution is Gaussian with width proportional to ν₀√(k_BT/mc²). Collisions interrupt phase coherence and often contribute Lorentzian broadening. The observed instrumental profile convolves with the physical profile. A measured width should not be assigned to lifetime until Doppler, pressure and instrumental contributions have been assessed.

7. Boltzmann populations and spectral intensity

At thermal equilibrium, the population of level i is proportional to g_i exp(−E_i/k_BT). The ratio N₂/N₁=(g₂/g₁)exp[−(E₂−E₁)/(k_BT)] explains why high-energy states are sparsely populated in a cool gas and why line intensities vary with temperature. It does not alone determine intensity: transition probabilities, degeneracies, optical depth, collisional excitation and detector response also matter. In a non-equilibrium discharge or laser medium, a Boltzmann distribution may not describe populations. State the thermodynamic assumption before using a population ratio to infer temperature.

8. Beer–Lambert law and concentration measurement

For a homogeneous absorbing sample under ideal dilute-solution conditions, I=I₀exp(−αl). Defining absorbance A=log₁₀(I₀/I) gives A=εlc, where ε is molar absorption coefficient, l is path length and c is concentration. A calibration plot of absorbance against concentration should be linear only over a validated range. Blank correction removes contributions from solvent, cuvette and baseline, but not chemical speciation changes, scattering or stray-light errors. A sound quantitative method uses multiple standards, replicate readings, residual analysis and an independent check sample.

9. Isotope substitution as a structural test

For a diatomic harmonic oscillator, ν is proportional to μ⁻¹ᐟ² if the force constant is essentially unchanged by isotopic substitution. Replacing one nucleus by a heavier isotope increases μ and shifts the vibrational band to lower frequency. For rotation, I=μR² increases and B∝1/I decreases. These shifts help identify molecular species. Deviations may arise from anharmonicity, vibration–rotation interaction, isotope-dependent equilibrium geometry and breakdown of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation. Compare ratios rather than absolute frequencies when testing the leading reduced-mass prediction.

10. Interpreting a spectrum as an inverse problem

Spectroscopy is often an inverse problem: infer energy levels, structure, temperature, field strength or composition from measured intensity as a function of frequency. Begin with candidate physical models, predict line positions and relative strengths, then fit the dataset while accounting for background and instrument response. Parameter correlations can make different models similarly plausible over a narrow interval. Use multiple transitions, isotopic substitution, polarisation, temperature variation or external-field variation to break degeneracies. Report assumptions and uncertainty intervals; a visually good fit is not proof that the interpretation is unique.
