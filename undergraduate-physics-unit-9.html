Unit overview

Classical Mechanics II reformulates motion using energy, constraints and symmetry. Lagrangian and Hamiltonian methods reveal conserved quantities, simplify constrained systems and provide the conceptual bridge to statistical and quantum mechanics. The course extends particle dynamics to central-force orbits, rotating bodies and coupled degrees of freedom.

Level: Undergraduate university physics. Prerequisites: introductory mechanics, algebra, trigonometry, calculus and basic laboratory or mathematical reasoning appropriate to the topic. Study approach: work through each concept, reproduce the derivations, attempt the numerical examples independently, then complete the examination questions.

Core textbook content

Classical Mechanics II reformulates motion using energy, constraints and symmetry. Lagrangian and Hamiltonian methods reveal conserved quantities, simplify constrained systems and provide the conceptual bridge to statistical and quantum mechanics. The course extends particle dynamics to central-force orbits, rotating bodies and coupled degrees of freedom. The sections below develop the main concepts from definitions to applications. For each topic, identify the system, list known quantities, choose a model, state approximations, and test the result against units and physical limits.

1. Generalised coordinates and constraints

A system with N particles has 3N Cartesian coordinates, but constraints reduce the independent degrees of freedom. Generalised coordinates q_i describe the configuration with the smallest convenient set of independent variables. Holonomic constraints can be written f(q,t)=0; non-holonomic constraints cannot generally be reduced to such coordinate equations. Ideal constraint forces do no virtual work for allowed virtual displacements. Selecting coordinates that encode constraints avoids solving unnecessary reaction forces.

Worked example / application. A simple pendulum of fixed length has two Cartesian coordinates but one holonomic constraint x²+y²=l², leaving one degree of freedom θ. Its position is fully described by θ.

2. The principle of stationary action

The action S=∫L(q,q̇,t)dt is stationary under small variations of the path with fixed endpoints. The Lagrangian is often L=T−V for conservative systems. Stationarity does not always mean a minimum; it may be a maximum or saddle. Applying the calculus of variations yields the Euler–Lagrange equations. This formulation is coordinate-independent in its physical content and naturally incorporates constraints through suitable coordinates or multipliers.

Worked example / application. For a free particle L=½m ẋ². The Euler–Lagrange equation gives d(m ẋ)/dt=0, so momentum is constant and ẍ=0.

3. Euler–Lagrange equations and examples

For each generalised coordinate q_i, d/dt(∂L/∂q̇_i)−∂L/∂q_i=0. If a coordinate is absent explicitly from L, its conjugate momentum is conserved. If L has no explicit time dependence, the energy function is conserved under appropriate regularity conditions. The equations can be more efficient than Newton's laws when constraints are complicated or coordinates are curvilinear.

Worked example / application. For a pendulum, T=½ml²θ̇² and V=mgl(1−cosθ). L=T−V gives ml²θ̈+mgl sinθ=0, or θ̈+(g/l)sinθ=0.

4. Noether's theorem and conservation laws

Continuous symmetries of the action correspond to conserved quantities under Noether's theorem. Invariance under spatial translation gives linear momentum conservation; rotational invariance gives angular momentum conservation; time-translation invariance gives energy conservation. The theorem identifies the structural origin of conservation laws. External fields, time-dependent constraints or symmetry-breaking potentials may remove the corresponding conservation law.

Worked example / application. If L is independent of x, ∂L/∂x=0 and p_x=∂L/∂ẋ is constant. This is the Lagrangian expression of translational symmetry.

5. Central-force motion and effective potential

A central force depends only on radial distance and points along the radius vector. Angular momentum is conserved, so motion lies in a plane. The radial equation can be written using an effective potential V_eff(r)=V(r)+L²/(2mr²), where L is angular momentum magnitude. Turning points occur where total energy equals V_eff. The centrifugal term is a consequence of angular momentum conservation, not an additional physical force in an inertial frame.

Worked example / application. For an attractive inverse-square potential V=−k/r, V_eff=−k/r+L²/(2mr²). Circular orbit radius follows dV_eff/dr=0, giving r=L²/(mk).

6. Kepler problem and orbital dynamics

For gravitational attraction between two masses, the relative motion reduces to a one-body problem with reduced mass μ=m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). Bound inverse-square orbits are ellipses with the force centre at one focus. The orbit equation is r(φ)=p/[1+e cos(φ−φ₀)], where p=L²/(μk) and eccentricity e distinguishes circular, elliptical, parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories. Kepler's laws follow from the inverse-square force and angular momentum conservation.

Worked example / application. For a circular orbit around mass M, equate GMm/r² to mv²/r. Then v=√(GM/r) and period T=2π√(r³/GM).

7. Scattering and impact parameters

Scattering describes trajectories deflected by a central potential. The impact parameter b is the perpendicular offset of the incoming asymptote from the force centre. The scattering angle depends on b, energy and potential. In inverse-square Coulomb or gravitational scattering, the Rutherford formula connects differential cross-section to the interaction strength and scattering angle. Cross-sections describe probabilities per incident flux, not literal geometric target areas. Classical predictions fail when de Broglie wavelengths or quantum effects become important.

Worked example / application. For Rutherford scattering, dσ/dΩ is proportional to 1/sin⁴(θ/2). Large-angle events are rarer, providing evidence for a concentrated Coulomb-scattering centre in the appropriate regime.

8. Rotating reference frames

A rotating frame introduces apparent acceleration terms when Newton's laws are expressed in that frame. For angular velocity Ω, acceleration includes Coriolis −2Ω×v_rel, centrifugal −Ω×(Ω×r), and Euler −dΩ/dt×r contributions in addition to relative acceleration. These terms are not new fundamental interactions; they arise from the accelerating coordinates. They explain deflection in rotating systems, including atmospheric circulation and laboratory turntables.

Worked example / application. A body moving eastward in the northern hemisphere experiences a Coriolis acceleration whose horizontal component is deflected to the right of its motion. Magnitude is 2Ωv sin(latitude) for horizontal motion.

9. Rigid-body kinematics and angular velocity

A rigid body preserves distances between its particles. Its motion can be decomposed into centre-of-mass translation and rotation about the centre of mass. Angular velocity ω defines instantaneous rotational motion, with particle velocity v=V_cm+ω×r′. Angular acceleration is dω/dt; in general, ω and angular momentum need not be parallel for an asymmetric body. Euler angles or rotation matrices describe orientation, but coordinate singularities can occur in angle parameterisations.

Worked example / application. A point 0.20 m from an axis rotating at 15 rad s⁻¹ has tangential speed v=ωr=3.0 m s⁻¹.

10. Moment of inertia and inertia tensor

For rotation about a fixed axis, I=Σm_ir_i² or I=∫r_perp²dm. Rotational kinetic energy is ½Iω². The inertia tensor generalises this to three dimensions and relates angular momentum to angular velocity: L=I·ω. Principal axes diagonalise the tensor. The parallel-axis theorem I=I_cm+Md² shifts the axis; the perpendicular-axis theorem applies to planar laminae. Moment of inertia depends on mass distribution and axis, not only total mass.

Worked example / application. A uniform thin rod of mass M and length L has I=ML²/12 about its centre perpendicular to the rod, and ML²/3 about an end. The ratio is four.

11. Euler equations and torque-free rotation

In body-fixed principal axes, Euler's equations are I₁ω̇₁+(I₃−I₂)ω₂ω₃=τ₁, with cyclic permutations. Torque-free rotation conserves angular momentum and kinetic energy, but ω can change direction relative to the body. Rotation about the largest or smallest principal moment is stable to small perturbations, while rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable. This is the tennis-racket or Dzhanibekov effect.

Worked example / application. A rigid body with I₁<I₂<I₃ rotated nearly about the intermediate axis I₂ exhibits growing orientation deviations, even with no external torque.

12. Coupled oscillations and normal modes

For small oscillations around stable equilibrium, expand potential energy to quadratic order. The equations become M q̈+Kq=0. Seeking q=a e^(iωt) gives (K−ω²M)a=0; non-trivial modes require det(K−ω²M)=0. Eigenvectors specify relative amplitudes and phases, while eigenvalues give squared normal-mode frequencies. Orthogonality is defined with respect to the mass matrix for suitable symmetric systems.

Worked example / application. Two identical masses coupled by three equal springs have in-phase and out-of-phase normal modes. The out-of-phase mode has the larger restoring force and higher frequency.

13. Hamiltonian mechanics and phase space

Define canonical momentum p_i=∂L/∂q̇_i and Hamiltonian H=Σp_iq̇_i−L. Hamilton's equations are q̇_i=∂H/∂p_i and ṗ_i=−∂H/∂q_i. Phase space contains positions and momenta; a trajectory represents the complete dynamical state. For many conservative systems H equals total energy, but this is not automatic for every coordinate choice or explicitly time-dependent Lagrangian.

Worked example / application. For H=p²/(2m)+V(x), Hamilton's equations give ẋ=p/m and ṗ=−dV/dx, recovering Newton's second law.

14. Poisson brackets and canonical transformations

The Poisson bracket is {f,g}=Σ(∂f/∂q_i ∂g/∂p_i−∂f/∂p_i ∂g/∂q_i). Time evolution is df/dt={f,H}+∂f/∂t. Canonical transformations preserve the Hamiltonian structure and Poisson-bracket relations. These methods prepare the way for Hamilton–Jacobi theory and quantum commutators. Care is required with generating functions and transformed momenta.

Worked example / application. For one-dimensional motion, {x,p}=1, {x,H}=p/m and {p,H}=−V′(x). Thus ẋ={x,H} and ṗ={p,H}.

15. Hamilton–Jacobi theory and action variables

Hamilton–Jacobi theory seeks a generating function S(q,t) satisfying ∂S/∂t+H(q,∂S/∂q,t)=0. It can reduce dynamics to quadratures when separation is possible. For periodic systems, action variables J=(1/2π)∮p dq are conserved adiabatic invariants under slow parameter changes. These ideas connect classical integrability, semiclassical quantisation and perturbation theory.

Worked example / application. For a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, J=E/ω. The action variable remains approximately constant when oscillator frequency changes slowly compared with its period.

16. Small oscillations and stability

Equilibrium is stable if small displacements produce restoring dynamics and the potential has a local minimum in allowed directions. The Hessian matrix of second derivatives of V, combined with the mass matrix, determines small-oscillation frequencies. Zero eigenvalues can indicate continuous symmetry or marginal directions; negative eigenvalues indicate instability. Linear stability is local and does not establish global stability.

Worked example / application. Near a stable equilibrium q₀, V≈V(q₀)+½(q−q₀)^T K(q−q₀). Positive eigenvalues of the mass-weighted stiffness matrix give real oscillation frequencies.

17. Integrated problem: rolling without slipping

For a rigid body rolling without slipping on a stationary surface, centre-of-mass speed and angular speed satisfy v_cm=Rω. Total kinetic energy is translational plus rotational: K=½Mv_cm²+½I_cmω². Static friction enforces the constraint and may do no work at the instantaneous contact point in ideal rolling. The constraint reduces degrees of freedom; it must be incorporated consistently into the chosen mechanics formulation.

Worked example / application. A solid cylinder rolls down a height h. Energy conservation gives Mgh=½Mv²+½(½MR²)(v²/R²)=¾Mv², so v=√(4gh/3).
