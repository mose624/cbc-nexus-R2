1. Aromaticity: foundations and historical development

Aromaticity is a model for explaining the unusual stability, geometry and reactivity of certain cyclic, conjugated molecules. Benzene is the archetype, but aromaticity is not synonymous with a pleasant smell and is not restricted to six-membered carbon rings. The modern description combines cyclic electron delocalisation, orbital symmetry, energetic stabilisation, magnetic response and characteristic bond equalisation. These criteria are related but not interchangeable: a molecule may show one aromaticity indicator more strongly than another.

2. The Hückel rule and π-electron counting

For a planar, monocyclic, continuously conjugated system with one p orbital at each ring atom, the Hückel rule predicts aromatic character when the system contains 4n+2 π electrons, where n is a non-negative integer. A corresponding 4n-electron system is antiaromatic only if it remains planar and fully conjugated; distortion or loss of conjugation may instead make it non-aromatic. Count electrons in the cyclic π system, not every lone pair or every valence electron in the molecule.

3. Molecular orbital origin of aromatic stabilisation

In a cyclic conjugated system, p orbitals combine to form delocalised molecular orbitals. For a six-membered ring, the bonding orbitals are filled before the antibonding orbitals, producing a closed-shell arrangement with a stabilised electronic configuration. The orbital-energy pattern explains why 4n+2 systems can be especially stable and why 4n systems have a problematic partially occupied frontier level when constrained to planarity.

4. Benzene structure and bond equalisation

Benzene is a planar hexagonal molecule in which each carbon is approximately trigonal planar and contributes one p orbital to the delocalised π system. All six carbon–carbon bonds are equivalent in the real molecule; they are neither alternating isolated single and double bonds nor rapidly switching localised bonds. Resonance structures are useful bookkeeping representations of one delocalised electronic structure, not separate molecules in equilibrium.

5. Resonance, canonical forms and resonance hybrid

6. Experimental evidence for aromaticity

Evidence includes similar carbon–carbon bond lengths, unusual thermochemical stability, characteristic substitution chemistry, ring-current effects in NMR and computed magnetic or energetic descriptors. No single measurement is a universal aromaticity test. Bond metrics reveal structural equalisation; heats of hydrogenation provide energetic comparisons; NMR chemical shifts report magnetic environments; computational indices provide model-dependent estimates. A strong argument combines independent evidence and states the limitations of each method.

7. Aromatic, antiaromatic and non-aromatic classification

Aromatic systems are cyclic, conjugated, sufficiently planar and contain 4n+2 π electrons. Antiaromatic systems meet the structural requirements but contain 4n π electrons, making them destabilised relative to an appropriate reference. Non-aromatic systems fail one or more essential structural conditions, for example because a ring atom is tetrahedral or the ring is strongly non-planar. Do not label every 4n-electron molecule antiaromatic without checking geometry and conjugation.

8. Cyclobutadiene and antiaromaticity

Planar cyclobutadiene has four π electrons and is a classic antiaromatic reference. Its distortion toward a rectangular geometry reduces the equivalence of the bonds and mitigates the electronic instability predicted for an ideal square structure. The example demonstrates that molecular geometry can respond to electronic instability and that a real molecule may avoid the most antiaromatic arrangement.

9. Cyclopentadienyl anion and aromaticity

The cyclopentadienyl anion has five p orbitals and six π electrons: four from two π bonds and two from the negatively charged carbon lone pair in a p orbital. The ring can be planar and conjugated, giving a 4n+2 count with n=1. The negative charge is delocalised over the ring, explaining the unusual stability of the anion and the acidity of cyclopentadiene relative to many ordinary hydrocarbons.

12. Pyridine: structure and basicity

Pyridine is a six-membered aromatic ring in which one CH group is replaced by nitrogen. Nitrogen contributes one electron from a p orbital to the aromatic π system; its lone pair occupies an sp2-like orbital in the plane of the ring and is not part of the six-π-electron circuit. Protonation uses this in-plane lone pair and preserves aromaticity, making pyridine a weak base compared with many aliphatic amines.

13. Pyrrole: lone-pair participation

Pyrrole is a five-membered aromatic heterocycle. The nitrogen lone pair occupies a p orbital and contributes two electrons to the six-electron aromatic circuit. Protonation at nitrogen would remove this pair from the aromatic system and is therefore less favourable than protonation of pyridine at its in-plane lone pair. This contrast is central to understanding the different basicities of the two heterocycles.

18. Electrophilic aromatic substitution: overview

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) replaces a ring hydrogen with an electrophile while restoring aromaticity in the final step. The reaction typically involves generation of an electrophile, attack by the aromatic π system to form a resonance-stabilised sigma complex, and deprotonation to re-establish the conjugated ring. Formation of the sigma complex temporarily sacrifices aromaticity and is commonly the rate-determining event.

20. Nitration of benzene

Benzene nitration uses a strongly acidic mixture to generate the nitronium ion, NO2+, the electrophile. The aromatic ring attacks the nitronium ion, forming a sigma complex; loss of a proton then restores aromaticity and produces nitrobenzene. The reaction illustrates electrophile generation, electrophilic attack, resonance stabilisation and rearomatisation. Temperature and reagent composition affect selectivity and the risk of further substitution.

This reversibility makes sulfonation useful as a temporary blocking strategy in synthesis, allowing a position to be occupied during one transformation and subsequently regenerated as C–H.

24. Friedel–Crafts acylation

Friedel–Crafts acylation installs an acyl group, commonly using an acyl chloride and a Lewis acid to generate an acylium electrophile. The acylium ion is resonance stabilised and does not rearrange like many carbocations. The resulting carbonyl substituent deactivates the ring, often limiting the reaction to monoacylation. Reduction of the carbonyl can provide an indirect route to an alkylbenzene without the usual alkylation rearrangements.

26. Ortho/para directors

Groups that donate electron density by resonance, such as –OH, –OR and –NH2 under suitable conditions, generally direct EAS to ortho and para positions. Alkyl groups are weaker activators through hyperconjugation and inductive donation. Halogens are the important exception: they deactivate the ring by induction but direct ortho/para through lone-pair resonance donation. Steric effects often favour para product when ortho positions are crowded.

27. Meta directors

Strong electron-withdrawing groups such as –NO2, –CN, –SO3H, –CHO, –COR and –CO2H commonly direct EAS to the meta position. Ortho and para attack can generate resonance contributors in which positive charge is adjacent to or on the carbon bearing the electron-withdrawing substituent, creating particularly unfavourable destabilisation. Meta attack avoids the worst of these contributors, although it does not make the ring activated.

Use the examples to practise electron counting, basicity comparisons, directing effects and mechanism selection. In examinations, show the reasoning rather than giving only the final product.

Worked example 1: Classify benzene using the Hückel rule

Reasoning: Six sp2 carbon atoms each contribute one electron to a continuous cyclic π system, giving six π electrons. Six equals 4n+2 for n=1. The ring is planar and conjugated; benzene is aromatic.

Conclusion: Aromatic; n=1.

Worked example 2: Count the π electrons in pyrrole

Reasoning: Two π bonds contribute four electrons. The nitrogen lone pair in a p orbital contributes two more. The total is six; the ring is conjugated and approximately planar.

Conclusion: Six π electrons; aromatic.

Worked example 3: Explain why pyridine is more basic than pyrrole

Reasoning: Pyridine's nitrogen lone pair lies in an in-plane orbital outside the aromatic π sextet, so protonation retains aromaticity. Pyrrole's lone pair is part of the sextet; using it for protonation disrupts aromatic stabilisation.

Conclusion: Pyridine is substantially more basic.

Worked example 4: Predict the major EAS position for toluene

Reasoning: The methyl group donates electron density by hyperconjugation and weak induction. It activates the ring and directs incoming electrophiles to ortho and para positions. Steric crowding can make para product important.

Conclusion: Ortho/para mixture; para often favoured under bulky conditions.

Worked example 5: Predict directing behaviour of nitrobenzene

Reasoning: The nitro group withdraws electron density by resonance and induction, deactivating the ring. Ortho/para sigma complexes are especially destabilised; meta attack avoids the most unfavourable resonance contributor.

Conclusion: Meta substitution is favoured; reaction is slower than benzene.

Worked example 6: Outline nitration of benzene

Reasoning: Concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids generate NO2+. Benzene attacks this electrophile to form an arenium ion. A base removes the proton from the attacked carbon, restoring the π system.

Conclusion: Product: nitrobenzene; mechanism: EAS.

Worked example 7: Choose a route to ethylbenzene avoiding rearrangement risk

Reasoning: Friedel–Crafts acylation with an acetyl electrophile gives acetophenone without a carbocation rearrangement. Reduction of the carbonyl to a methylene group then gives ethylbenzene.

Conclusion: Acylation followed by carbonyl reduction.

Worked example 8: Explain why cyclopentadienyl anion is aromatic

Reasoning: The ring has five conjugated p orbitals and six π electrons: four from two double bonds and two from the anionic lone pair. A planar delocalised arrangement satisfies 4n+2 with n=1.

Conclusion: Aromatic six-π-electron anion.

Worked example 9: Distinguish SNAr from benzyne substitution

Reasoning: SNAr addition–elimination is favoured by strong electron-withdrawing groups ortho or para to the leaving group and proceeds through an anionic sigma intermediate. Benzyne formation requires strong base and elimination of HX, followed by nucleophilic addition; regioisomer mixtures may occur.

Conclusion: Use substrate activation and conditions to identify the pathway.

Worked example 10: Interpret a heteroaromatic NMR clue

Chapter summary

  • Aromaticity requires a cyclic, conjugated orbital pathway and an appropriate electronic configuration; geometry is essential.
  • The ordinary Hückel rule applies to planar monocyclic conjugated systems: 4n+2 π electrons are aromatic; 4n systems are antiaromatic only when the structural conditions are met.

  • EAS proceeds through a sigma complex and rearomatisation; substituents control rate and orientation through electronic and steric effects.
  • SNAr and benzyne pathways are distinct and depend on substrate activation and reaction conditions.
