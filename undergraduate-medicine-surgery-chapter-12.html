Core framework and conceptual foundations

Surgery is a discipline of diagnosis, procedural decision-making, perioperative risk reduction, technical skill, teamwork and longitudinal care. A surgical patient should be assessed using a structured approach that includes physiological stability, the indication for intervention, alternatives, patient goals, comorbidity, medicines, allergies, nutrition, functional status and informed consent. The decision to operate is not determined by anatomy alone; it also depends on disease severity, expected benefit, procedural risk, available expertise and the patient's informed preferences.

Mechanisms, structure and clinical interpretation

Wound healing proceeds through overlapping haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling phases. Platelets and fibrin stabilise the initial clot; neutrophils and macrophages contribute to debridement and signalling; fibroblasts deposit extracellular matrix; angiogenesis supports granulation tissue; collagen is remodelled over time. Infection, poor perfusion, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking, immunosuppression and excessive tension can impair healing. Aseptic technique aims to prevent microbial contamination; sterilisation destroys all forms of microbial life, whereas disinfection reduces microbial burden and may not eliminate spores. Surgical site infection prevention includes appropriate hand hygiene, skin preparation, antibiotic prophylaxis when indicated, temperature and glucose management, and sound operative technique. Acute abdominal pain requires attention to shock, peritonism, obstruction, perforation, ischaemia, haemorrhage and pregnancy-related causes. Postoperative review includes airway and breathing, circulation, pain, consciousness, urine output, wound and drain assessment, thromboembolism risk, nutrition and early mobilisation.

Integrated clinical case

A patient develops increasing abdominal pain, fever and tachycardia after abdominal surgery. Outline a structured assessment that first addresses physiological deterioration, then considers wound infection, intra-abdominal collection, anastomotic leak, bleeding, ileus and thromboembolism. Explain escalation and team communication; do not delay urgent review while completing a teaching checklist.

How to reason through the case: identify the immediate safety issue; separate reported symptoms, observed signs and test results; construct a concise problem representation; explain the relevant mechanism; consider at least two alternatives; identify the evidence that would discriminate between them; and state what requires prompt review by a qualified clinician. Do not infer a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan from a teaching vignette alone.
