Introduction and integrated learning framework

Structure determination is an evidence-integration problem. No single technique answers every structural question. Spectroscopic methods probe different physical properties: UV-visible absorption interrogates electronic transitions; IR and Raman probe molecular vibrations through different selection rules; NMR reports on nuclear magnetic environments, coupling and spatial proximity; and mass spectrometry measures gas-phase ions and their fragmentation. The most reliable interpretation combines these complementary observations with elemental composition, chemical reactivity and sample context.

Before interpreting a spectrum, establish the identity and condition of the sample, solvent, concentration, temperature, instrument configuration and acquisition method. Confirm that the instrument is calibrated and that blanks and reference standards are appropriate. A peak is an observation; assigning it to a functional group, bond, nucleus or fragment is an interpretation that must be supported by a coherent model.

1. Foundations of molecular spectroscopy

Molecular spectroscopy examines how molecules exchange energy with electromagnetic radiation. The measured spectrum is a map of allowed transitions between quantised molecular states. A useful first distinction is between absorption, emission and scattering: absorption removes photons at selected frequencies, emission produces photons as excited states relax, and Raman scattering changes photon frequency through an inelastic interaction. Spectra are interpreted by connecting measured positions, intensities, widths and shapes to molecular structure.

Interpretive approach: Identify the physical quantity being measured, state the relevant units and experimental conditions, and connect the observation to a molecular model. Compare the proposed interpretation with at least one independent feature of the sample. Consider alternative explanations such as overlapping transitions, conformational averaging, solvent effects, impurities and instrumental artefacts. A sound assignment is one that explains the complete data set rather than a single prominent peak.

2. Electromagnetic radiation and photon energy

Electromagnetic radiation is described by wavelength, frequency, wavenumber and photon energy. These quantities are related by c = λν and E = hν = hc/λ. Wavenumber, usually expressed in cm⁻¹, is especially convenient in infrared spectroscopy. Increasing frequency and wavenumber means increasing photon energy. Spectroscopists must state units carefully and distinguish vacuum wavelength from values in other media when high precision is required.

3. Quantised molecular energy levels

A molecule has electronic, vibrational and rotational energy contributions. A simplified total energy expression is E = E_electronic + E_vibrational + E_rotational, with additional nuclear-spin contributions in suitable experiments. Electronic energy gaps are generally larger than vibrational gaps, which are larger than rotational gaps. This hierarchy explains why electronic spectra commonly occur in the UV-visible region, vibrations in the infrared, and many pure rotational transitions in the microwave or far-infrared.

4. Selection rules and transition probability

A transition is observable only when the relevant transition moment is non-zero and the initial state is populated sufficiently. For electric-dipole absorption, the transition dipole integral determines intensity. Selection rules arise from symmetry and angular-momentum constraints; they are not simply arbitrary restrictions. Weak nominally forbidden transitions may gain intensity through vibronic coupling, perturbations or higher multipole contributions. Selection rules therefore guide interpretation but should not be applied as absolute prohibitions in every real molecule.

5. Beer–Lambert law

For a homogeneous, non-scattering sample measured within the instrument's linear range, absorbance A = log10(I0/I) = εlc. Here ε is molar absorptivity, l is optical path length and c is concentration. Calibration curves convert absorbance into concentration when standards and samples have comparable chemical conditions. Deviations arise from high concentration, chemical equilibria, stray light, polychromatic radiation, scattering, fluorescence and instrumental limitations. Blank correction and replicate measurements improve reliability.

6. UV-visible absorption spectroscopy

UV-visible spectroscopy measures electronic transitions, commonly involving π to π*, n to π* and charge-transfer excitations. Conjugation often lowers the energy gap and shifts absorption to longer wavelength. Chromophores absorb characteristic regions, while auxochromes can alter wavelength and intensity through electronic effects. Solvent polarity, pH, aggregation and coordination can change spectra. UV-visible data are valuable for quantitative analysis and for monitoring reactions, but a single absorption maximum rarely proves a unique structure.

7. Chromophores and conjugation

A chromophore is the part of a molecule responsible for an electronic absorption band. Conjugated π systems allow electrons to be delocalised over several atoms, often reducing the HOMO–LUMO energy separation. Carbonyl groups, azo groups, aromatic rings and extended polyenes are common chromophores. Substitution and protonation can change orbital energies. The observed spectrum is a property of the whole molecular environment, not merely a lookup value assigned to an isolated functional group.

8. Auxochromes and substituent effects

Auxochromes such as amino, hydroxy and alkoxy groups can modify absorption when electronically coupled to a chromophore. Electron donation or withdrawal changes orbital energies and transition probabilities. Bathochromic and hypsochromic shifts describe movement to longer and shorter wavelengths; hyperchromic and hypochromic effects describe increased and decreased intensity. These terms describe spectral changes, not mechanisms. Interpretation should compare controlled spectra and consider solvent, pH, protonation and concentration.

9. Solvent and pH effects in UV-visible spectra

Solvent polarity can stabilise ground and excited states to different extents, shifting absorption bands. Hydrogen bonding may alter transitions involving lone pairs, especially n to π*. Acid-base equilibria can change chromophore structure, so pH-dependent spectra may reveal pKa values or tautomeric changes. A careful experiment controls solvent composition, ionic strength, temperature and pH. When a spectrum changes with pH, the change may reflect a new chemical species rather than a simple instrumental effect.

10. UV-visible quantitative analysis

Quantitative UV-visible analysis begins by selecting an analytical wavelength with useful sensitivity and minimal interference. Prepare standards that bracket the expected sample concentration, use an appropriate blank, and construct a calibration curve. Examine residuals rather than relying only on a high correlation coefficient. Dilute samples that exceed the linear range and account for dilution factors. Report uncertainty, detection limits where established, and the conditions under which the calibration is valid.

11. Infrared spectroscopy principles

Infrared spectroscopy measures transitions between vibrational states. A vibration absorbs IR radiation when its dipole moment changes during the motion. The frequency depends on bond force constant and reduced mass, approximated for a diatomic oscillator by ν̄ = (1/2πc)√(k/μ). Real molecules have coupled normal modes and anharmonic potentials. IR spectra are therefore interpreted using characteristic regions together with the complete fingerprint pattern.

12. Vibrational modes and normal coordinates

Molecular vibrations are collective normal modes in which atoms move with a common frequency and defined phase relationships. A nonlinear molecule with N atoms has 3N−6 vibrational normal modes; a linear molecule has 3N−5. Modes may be stretching, bending, rocking, wagging or twisting. Symmetry can make some modes IR inactive but Raman active. Normal-coordinate analysis explains why observed bands cannot always be assigned to one isolated bond.

13. Functional-group region of an IR spectrum

The higher-wavenumber functional-group region, approximately 4000–1500 cm⁻¹, contains many diagnostically useful stretches. O–H and N–H bands often occur at high wavenumber, C–H stretches occur below them, and triple bonds commonly absorb in the 2300–2100 cm⁻¹ region. Carbonyl stretches are often strong in the 1850–1650 cm⁻¹ region. Ranges overlap and shift with conjugation, ring strain, hydrogen bonding and substituent effects.

14. Fingerprint region

The region below roughly 1500 cm⁻¹ contains many coupled bending and stretching modes. Its complexity makes manual assignment difficult, but the pattern can be highly characteristic of a compound. Reference-library matching is useful only when acquisition conditions, sampling method and spectral processing are compatible. Fingerprint agreement supports identity but does not replace orthogonal evidence, purity assessment or appropriate controls.

15. Carbonyl stretching frequencies

Carbonyl stretching frequency is sensitive to electronic and structural context. Conjugation usually lowers the C=O stretching frequency by reducing effective bond order. Ring strain can raise it in small cyclic ketones. Amides typically absorb at lower frequencies than many ketones because nitrogen donation reduces carbonyl bond order. Hydrogen bonding and physical state also influence the band. A carbonyl assignment should be tested against other evidence such as NMR and mass spectrometry.

16. Hydrogen bonding and band shape

Hydrogen bonding can broaden and shift O–H and N–H bands because molecules experience a distribution of interaction strengths. A free hydroxyl group may give a relatively sharper feature than a strongly hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl in a condensed-phase sample. Concentration, temperature, solvent and sample preparation affect the pattern. Band shape is informative, but it must be interpreted with knowledge of whether the spectrum was collected in gas, solution, ATR or transmission mode.

17. IR sampling methods

Transmission IR commonly uses thin films, pellets or solution cells, while attenuated total reflectance (ATR) allows rapid analysis of many solids and liquids with minimal preparation. ATR penetration depth depends on wavelength, incidence angle and refractive indices, so ATR and transmission intensities may differ. Background collection, crystal cleanliness, contact pressure, atmospheric water and carbon dioxide correction all matter. The sampling method should be recorded when comparing spectra.

18. Raman spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy measures inelastic scattering of monochromatic light. A vibration is Raman active when it changes molecular polarisability. Raman and IR selection rules are complementary: a vibration that produces a large dipole change may be strong in IR, while one producing a large polarisability change may be strong in Raman. Raman can be particularly useful for symmetric non-polar bonds and aqueous samples, although fluorescence and laser heating can interfere.

19. Stokes and anti-Stokes Raman scattering

In Stokes scattering, the molecule gains vibrational energy and the scattered photon has lower energy than the incident photon. In anti-Stokes scattering, a molecule initially in an excited vibrational state loses energy and the scattered photon has higher energy. Anti-Stokes intensity is usually lower at room temperature because fewer molecules occupy excited vibrational levels. The intensity ratio can be used for temperature analysis when instrumental response and sample conditions are controlled.

20. Raman instrumentation and applications

A Raman instrument typically includes a laser, sampling optics, a rejection filter for Rayleigh scattering, a spectrograph and a detector. Excitation wavelength influences fluorescence, resonance enhancement and possible sample damage. Applications include carbon materials, minerals, pharmaceuticals, pigments, polymers and in situ reaction monitoring. Laser power, focus, exposure time and sample heterogeneity should be assessed to avoid artefacts or thermal alteration.

21. Nuclear magnetic resonance fundamentals

NMR spectroscopy observes nuclei with non-zero spin in a magnetic field. Nuclear energy levels split in the applied field, and radiofrequency radiation can induce transitions between them. The resonance frequency depends on the gyromagnetic ratio and effective magnetic field. NMR is powerful because local electronic environments modify the field experienced by each nucleus, creating chemical shifts that report on molecular connectivity and environment.

22. Chemical shift

Chemical shift is reported in parts per million relative to a reference, commonly tetramethylsilane for many organic solution spectra. The ppm scale makes values largely independent of spectrometer field strength. Electron density, hybridisation, electronegativity, anisotropy, hydrogen bonding, solvent and temperature influence shifts. Typical ranges are guides rather than rigid rules. Assignments should be consistent with the molecular formula, integration, multiplicity and other spectral data.

23. Shielding and deshielding

Electrons circulate in response to the applied magnetic field and generate local fields. Shielding reduces the effective field at a nucleus, generally moving its resonance upfield; deshielding increases the effective field, generally moving it downfield. Nearby π systems produce anisotropic fields, explaining characteristic effects of alkenes, alkynes, aromatic rings and carbonyl groups. Simple electronegativity arguments are useful but insufficient without considering geometry and anisotropy.

24. Proton NMR integration

The integrated area of a 1H NMR signal is proportional to the number of contributing protons, subject to acquisition and processing conditions. Relative integrals are normally scaled to whole-number proton counts. Exchangeable O–H and N–H signals may broaden or vary with solvent, concentration and temperature. Integration is not a direct measure of sample purity unless quantitative NMR conditions, relaxation delays, pulse calibration and suitable standards are used.

25. Spin-spin coupling and multiplicity

Spin-spin coupling splits resonances through interactions transmitted by chemical bonds. In simple first-order cases, n equivalent neighbouring spin-½ nuclei produce an n+1 multiplet. Coupling constants J are measured in hertz and are independent of field strength. The n+1 rule fails for strongly coupled or non-equivalent systems. Long-range coupling, diastereotopic protons and second-order effects can create more complex patterns.

26. First-order and second-order NMR

First-order spectra occur when chemical-shift separations in hertz are much larger than coupling constants. When this condition is not met, strong coupling causes distorted intensities, roofing and complex line patterns. Increasing magnetic field strength often improves separation in hertz for a fixed ppm difference. Computational simulation and higher-field experiments can help resolve ambiguous patterns, but structural interpretation must still account for equivalence and molecular symmetry.

27. 13C NMR spectroscopy

Carbon-13 NMR identifies distinct carbon environments. Its natural abundance is low, so spectra are commonly acquired with broadband proton decoupling to simplify multiplets. Signal intensities are not generally proportional to carbon counts because relaxation times and nuclear Overhauser enhancement vary. DEPT and related editing experiments distinguish CH, CH2 and CH3 groups, while quaternary carbons are absent from some DEPT spectra.

28. Two-dimensional NMR overview

Two-dimensional NMR spreads correlations across two frequency dimensions and helps assign complex molecules. COSY identifies scalar-coupled proton networks; HSQC correlates directly bonded proton and carbon nuclei; HMBC reveals longer-range heteronuclear correlations; NOESY and ROESY provide through-space proximity information. Worked examples

Worked example 1: Photon energy from wavelength

A UV absorption occurs at 250 nm. Using E = hc/λ, with h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.998×10⁸ m s⁻¹, convert 250 nm to 2.50×10⁻⁷ m. E per photon = 7.94×10⁻¹⁹ J. Multiplying by Avogadro’s constant gives approximately 478 kJ mol⁻¹. Check units and distinguish energy per photon from molar energy.

Worked example 2: Beer–Lambert concentration

A solution has absorbance 0.600 in a 1.00 cm cell. If ε = 1.50×10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, c = A/(εl) = 0.600/(1.50×10⁴ × 1.00) = 4.00×10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹. This result assumes a valid blank, linear response and no significant chemical or scattering interference.

Worked example 3: Reduced mass and vibrational frequency

For a diatomic bond, μ = m1m2/(m1+m2). Substituting isotopic masses changes μ and therefore the vibrational wavenumber. A heavier isotope lowers the frequency when the force constant is effectively unchanged. This is the basis of isotopic shifts used to test vibrational assignments.

Worked example 4: DBE calculation

For C8H8O2, DBE = (2×8+2−8)/2 = 5. Oxygen is omitted. Five units may correspond, for example, to an aromatic ring (four units) plus one additional ring or double bond. The formula alone does not determine which arrangement is present.

Worked example 5: NMR frequency separation

Two resonances separated by 0.20 ppm on a 500 MHz instrument are separated by 100 Hz. Multiply the ppm difference by the spectrometer frequency in MHz to obtain the separation in hertz. Compare this separation with J to judge whether first-order treatment is reasonable.

Worked example 6: Mass error

If the theoretical m/z is 301.1234 and measured m/z is 301.1240, mass error = (measured−theoretical)/theoretical ×10⁶ = approximately +2.0 ppm. State the sign convention and ensure that the ion/adduct assignment is correct.

Worked example 7: Molar absorptivity from calibration

A calibration line has slope 0.0250 absorbance units per μmol L⁻¹ in a 1.00 cm cell. Convert the slope to 2.50×10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Confirm the concentration units before interpreting the slope as ε.

Worked example 8: Isotope envelope interpretation

A near 3:1 M:M+2 pair suggests one chlorine atom, whereas a near 1:1 pair suggests one bromine atom. Check the full envelope and accurate mass because overlapping isotopes and multiple halogens change the expected pattern.

Worked example 9: Quantitative NMR ratio

If an analyte signal integrates to 3.0 and a standard signal to 2.0, account for the number of nuclei represented by each signal before comparing mole amounts. Corrected mole ratio = (I_analyte/N_analyte)/(I_standard/N_standard).

Worked example 10: Integrated structure proposal

For an unknown with a carbonyl IR band, a compatible carbonyl carbon in 13C NMR, and a formula DBE, construct candidate structures and test each against proton count, carbon count, coupling and 2D correlations. Reject any candidate that fails one reliable observation.

Structured revision questions and answers

Use these questions for retrieval practice. Answers provide core marking points; extended responses should include equations, units, assumptions and relevant diagrams where appropriate.

Question 1. Define molecular spectroscopy and distinguish absorption from emission.

Answer / marking guidance: Spectroscopy studies interaction of matter with electromagnetic radiation. Absorption transfers photon energy to a system; emission occurs when an excited system releases radiation during relaxation.

Question 2. Relate wavelength, frequency and photon energy.

Answer / marking guidance: c = λν and E = hν = hc/λ. Frequency and energy increase as wavelength decreases.

Question 3. Why are molecular energy levels quantised?

Answer / marking guidance: Allowed stationary states have discrete energies under quantum-mechanical boundary conditions; transitions occur between permitted states.

Question 4. State the Beer–Lambert law and define its terms.

Answer / marking guidance: A = εlc; A is absorbance, ε molar absorptivity, l path length and c concentration.

Question 5. Give two causes of Beer–Lambert deviations.

Answer / marking guidance: Examples include high concentration, chemical association, stray light, scattering, polychromatic radiation and fluorescence.

Question 6. What is a chromophore?

Answer / marking guidance: A molecular group responsible for an electronic absorption band, such as a conjugated π system or carbonyl-containing group.

Question 7. Explain a bathochromic shift.

Answer / marking guidance: A shift of an absorption band to longer wavelength, corresponding to lower transition energy.

Question 8. What makes a vibration IR active?

Answer / marking guidance: The vibration must produce a change in the molecular dipole moment.

Question 9. How many normal modes does a nonlinear molecule with N atoms have?

Answer / marking guidance: 3N−6 vibrational normal modes.

Question 10. Why is the IR fingerprint region useful?

Answer / marking guidance: Its complex pattern is often characteristic of a molecule and can support comparison with reference spectra.

Question 11. How does conjugation affect a carbonyl stretching band?

Answer / marking guidance: Conjugation generally lowers effective C=O bond order and shifts the stretching frequency to lower wavenumber.

Question 12. What is ATR-IR?

Answer / marking guidance: Attenuated total reflectance IR, a sampling method using an evanescent field at an internal reflection element to probe a sample.

Question 13. State the Raman activity condition.

Answer / marking guidance: A vibration is Raman active when it changes molecular polarisability.

Question 14. Distinguish Stokes and anti-Stokes Raman scattering.

Answer / marking guidance: Stokes scattering leaves the molecule more vibrationally excited and the photon lower in energy; anti-Stokes does the reverse.

Question 15. What determines an NMR chemical shift?

Answer / marking guidance: The local electronic environment, including shielding, electronegativity, hybridisation, anisotropy, solvent and temperature.

Question 16. Why is chemical shift reported in ppm?

Answer / marking guidance: The ppm scale normalises frequency displacement to spectrometer frequency, enabling comparison across field strengths.

Question 17. What does a proton NMR integral represent?

Answer / marking guidance: Relative numbers of contributing protons, provided acquisition and processing support reliable integration.

Question 18. State the simple n+1 splitting rule.

Answer / marking guidance: For a first-order signal coupled to n equivalent spin-½ neighbours, the multiplet has n+1 lines.

Question 19. What is a coupling constant J?

Answer / marking guidance: The frequency separation between lines of a scalar-coupled multiplet, reported in hertz.

Question 20. Why are 13C NMR intensities not normally quantitative?

Answer / marking guidance: Relaxation and nuclear Overhauser enhancement vary among carbons, so peak areas do not directly represent carbon numbers.

Question 21. What information does COSY provide?

Answer / marking guidance: Correlations between scalar-coupled nuclei, commonly neighbouring protons, helping map spin systems.

Question 22. What does HSQC correlate?

Answer / marking guidance: A proton with a directly bonded heteronucleus, most commonly carbon-13.

Question 23. What is the main use of HMBC?

Answer / marking guidance: It reveals longer-range heteronuclear correlations that help connect molecular fragments.

Question 24. How does NOE differ from scalar coupling?

Answer / marking guidance: NOE is a through-space effect; scalar coupling is transmitted through chemical bonds.

Question 25. Define T1 and T2 relaxation.

Answer / marking guidance: T1 is spin-lattice recovery of longitudinal magnetisation; T2 is transverse magnetisation decay.

Question 26. What is m/z in mass spectrometry?

Answer / marking guidance: The mass-to-charge ratio of a detected ion.

Question 27. Why may the molecular ion be absent in EI spectra?

Answer / marking guidance: Energetic ionisation can cause extensive fragmentation before the molecular radical cation is detected strongly.

Question 28. Name two common ESI ion types.

Answer / marking guidance: Protonated [M+H]+ and deprotonated [M−H]− ions; sodium and ammonium adducts are also common.

Question 29. What is the base peak?

Answer / marking guidance: The most intense peak in a mass spectrum, assigned 100% relative abundance.

Question 30. What does high-resolution MS contribute?

Answer / marking guidance: Accurate m/z measurements that constrain elemental composition and candidate molecular formulae.

Question 31. What isotope pattern suggests one chlorine atom?

Answer / marking guidance: An M and M+2 pair with an approximate 3:1 intensity ratio.

Question 32. What isotope pattern suggests one bromine atom?

Answer / marking guidance: An M and M+2 pair with an approximate 1:1 intensity ratio.

Question 33. What is tandem mass spectrometry?

Answer / marking guidance: A method that selects a precursor ion, induces fragmentation and analyses the product ions.

Question 34. Calculate DBE for C6H6.

Answer / marking guidance: DBE = (2×6+2−6)/2 = 4.

Question 35. Why combine IR, NMR and MS?

Answer / marking guidance: They provide complementary evidence on functional groups, molecular environments/connectivity and molecular composition.

Question 36. What is quantitative NMR used for?

Answer / marking guidance: Determining analyte amount, assay or purity using calibrated integrals and a suitable reference.

Question 37. Why is a relaxation delay important in qNMR?

Answer / marking guidance: It allows relevant nuclei to recover sufficiently between scans so signal integrals are reliable.

Question 38. Give two causes of NMR line broadening.

Answer / marking guidance: Chemical exchange, short T2, paramagnetic impurities, poor shimming and unresolved coupling can broaden lines.

Question 39. What is spectral resolution?

Answer / marking guidance: The ability of an instrument to distinguish nearby spectral features.

Question 40. Why should a blank be measured in UV-visible analysis?

Answer / marking guidance: It accounts for absorbance from solvent, cuvette and reagents so the analyte contribution can be isolated.

Question 41. What is a transition dipole moment?

Answer / marking guidance: The matrix element describing coupling between two quantum states through the electric-dipole interaction; its magnitude influences transition intensity.

Question 42. Why can solvent change an electronic spectrum?

Answer / marking guidance: Solvent interactions stabilise ground and excited states differently and can alter equilibria and hydrogen bonding.

Question 43. What is a normal coordinate?

Answer / marking guidance: A collective vibrational coordinate describing the displacement pattern of a molecular normal mode.

Question 44. Why can Raman be useful for aqueous samples?

Answer / marking guidance: Water often gives relatively weak Raman interference compared with its strong IR absorption.

Question 45. What is an adduct ion?

Answer / marking guidance: An ion formed when an analyte associates with another species, such as Na+ or NH4+, during ionisation.

Question 46. What is mass error in ppm?

Answer / marking guidance: (Measured m/z − theoretical m/z)/theoretical m/z × 10⁶, with sign convention stated.

Question 47. Why are reference libraries not definitive proof of identity?

Answer / marking guidance: Different compounds can have similar spectra and matching depends on instrument, sample and processing conditions.

Question 48. What is method selectivity?

Answer / marking guidance: The ability to measure an analyte in the presence of other components without unacceptable interference.

Question 49. Name four elements of a defensible spectral report.

Answer / marking guidance: Instrument and acquisition conditions, units, assignments, labelled spectra, standards, processing details and uncertainty.

Question 50. How should contradictory spectral evidence be handled?

Answer / marking guidance: Recheck sample, data quality and assignments; generate alternatives and test the complete evidence rather than ignoring inconsistencies.

Question 51. What is the difference between an observation and an interpretation?

Answer / marking guidance: An observation is directly measured (for example, a band at a stated wavenumber); an interpretation assigns chemical meaning to it.

Further reading and academic practice

For deeper study, consult university-level texts on molecular spectroscopy, organic structure determination, instrumental analysis and magnetic resonance. Compare textbook examples with primary research spectra and instrument manuals. When using spectral databases, record the source and acquisition conditions. Do not treat an automated library score or predicted spectrum as conclusive evidence without independent confirmation.

Advanced undergraduate expansion: spectroscopic methods in structure determination

1. Integrated spectral reasoning

Structure elucidation is an inverse problem: measured spectra are used to infer molecular structure. Begin with molecular formula and degree of unsaturation, then combine accurate-mass MS, isotope pattern, IR functional-group evidence, UV-visible chromophore information and NMR connectivity. Each technique constrains a different part of the problem. A proposed structure should explain all major observations, including absent signals where chemically meaningful. Do not force a single structure when data support multiple isomers; state the ambiguity and propose the experiment that would resolve it.

2. Derivation: Beer–Lambert law from differential absorption

For monochromatic radiation passing through a thin layer dx, the decrease in intensity is proportional to incident intensity I, absorber concentration c and path increment: dI = −κcI dx. Rearrangement gives dI/I = −κc dx. Integrating from I₀ at x = 0 to I at path length l gives ln(I/I₀) = −κcl. Since absorbance A = log₁₀(I₀/I), A = εcl, where ε = κ/2.303 after unit conversion. Deviations arise from polychromatic radiation, stray light, high concentration, chemical equilibria, scattering and instrumental nonlinearity.

3. Derivation: vibrational frequency and the harmonic-oscillator approximation

For a diatomic molecule approximated as two masses connected by a spring, the vibrational wavenumber is ν̃ = (1/2πc)√(k/μ), where k is the force constant and μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass. Increasing bond force constant raises the frequency; increasing reduced mass lowers it. Isotopic substitution therefore shifts vibrational bands without changing the electronic bonding framework. Real molecular vibrations are anharmonic and coupled, so the expression is a first approximation rather than a complete spectral prediction.

4. NMR: chemical shift, coupling and quantitative limitations

Chemical shift reports the local magnetic environment relative to a reference; it is influenced by electron density, anisotropy, hydrogen bonding, solvent, temperature and conformation. Scalar coupling provides through-bond connectivity and depends on the coupling pathway and molecular geometry. First-order splitting rules require sufficiently separated chemical shifts relative to J; strongly coupled spin systems need full spin analysis. Quantitative NMR additionally requires validated pulse angle, adequate relaxation delay, stable baseline, suitable reference and non-overlapping signals. Integration is not quantitative if nuclei have not relaxed comparably.

5. Laboratory experiment: quantitative UV-visible calibration

Prepare at least five standards spanning the working range from a traceable stock solution using calibrated volumetric equipment. Measure a reagent/solvent blank, select a wavelength near the analyte absorption maximum, and record replicate absorbances using matched cuvettes with consistent orientation and clean optical faces. Plot absorbance against concentration, inspect residuals rather than relying only on R², and calculate the unknown by interpolation within the validated range. Include dilution factors and uncertainty from stock preparation, volumetric operations and regression. Re-check with an independent quality-control standard.

6. Laboratory experiment: unknown identification by IR, NMR and MS

Acquire an IR spectrum and identify diagnostic bands, recording sampling mode and background correction. Obtain an MS spectrum and determine a plausible molecular ion/adduct, accurate mass and isotope pattern. Collect ¹H and ¹³C NMR, followed by suitable COSY, HSQC and HMBC experiments. Build a signal table with chemical shift, multiplicity, integration, coupling constant and proposed assignment. Check atom balance and degree of unsaturation. State solvent, field strength, reference, temperature, ionisation mode and processing choices. Retain raw data and distinguish observation from interpretation.

7. Advanced structured examination questions with model answers

Q1. A solution gives A = 0.625 in a 1.00 cm cell. If ε = 1.25 × 10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, calculate concentration.

Answer: c = A/(εl) = 0.625/(1.25×10⁴×1.00) = 5.00×10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹.

Q2. Estimate the direction of the IR frequency change when ¹²C is replaced by ¹³C in a C–O oscillator.

Answer: Reduced mass increases, so ν̃ decreases according to ν̃ ∝ μ⁻¹ᐟ². The heavier isotope shifts the vibration to lower wavenumber.

Q3. Why is a high R² insufficient to validate an analytical calibration?

Answer: R² does not reveal curvature, heteroscedasticity, influential points, systematic bias or poor performance near the decision level. Examine residuals, replicate standards, range and independent QC results.

Q4. A molecular ion has measured mass 180.0630 Da and calculated mass 180.0634 Da. Calculate mass error in ppm.

Answer: Error = (180.0630−180.0634)/180.0634×10⁶ = −2.22 ppm (approximately). The negative sign indicates measured mass is lower.

Q5. Explain why a missing HMBC cross-peak does not necessarily disprove a proposed long-range connectivity.

Answer: HMBC intensity depends on the magnitude of long-range coupling, pulse optimisation, relaxation, overlap and sensitivity. Absence may reflect an unfavourable coupling pathway or experimental limitation; assess other correlations and acquire complementary data.
