Scope and principles of community health nursing

Community health nursing focuses on individuals, families and populations in the places where they live, learn, work and socialise. It integrates prevention, health promotion, early identification, treatment support, rehabilitation and advocacy. The unit of care may be an individual, household, school, workplace, neighbourhood or wider population.

Equity, participation, cultural safety, intersectoral collaboration and respect for human rights are central. Community nurses work with local leaders and services while protecting confidentiality and avoiding assumptions about communities. Programmes should be shaped by local evidence, community priorities and national policy.

Community assessment and diagnosis

A community assessment gathers information about population structure, health status, determinants, assets, services, environment and expressed priorities. Methods may include household surveys, key-informant interviews, focus groups, observation, service data and participatory mapping. Quantitative indicators should be interpreted alongside community experience.

A community nursing diagnosis describes a population health need, contributing factors and affected groups. Prioritisation considers magnitude, severity, preventability, inequity, community concern, feasibility and available resources. Validate findings with community members before designing interventions.

Epidemiology and measures of health

Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health-related states in populations and applies findings to control health problems. Incidence measures new cases over a defined period among a population at risk; prevalence measures existing cases in a population at a point or over a period. Rates require a clear numerator, denominator and time frame.

Mortality, morbidity, case-fatality, attack rates and other indicators answer different questions. Association does not automatically establish causation. Consider confounding, bias, measurement error and changes in case definitions. Use official surveillance and census data with attention to completeness and date.

Levels of prevention and health promotion

Primary prevention aims to prevent disease before it occurs, such as immunisation or injury prevention. Secondary prevention focuses on early detection and prompt intervention, such as screening where evidence supports it. Tertiary prevention reduces complications and disability after disease is established through treatment, rehabilitation and self-management support.

Health promotion enables people and communities to increase control over health determinants. Effective approaches combine individual skills, supportive environments, community action, healthy public policy and accessible services. Education alone cannot overcome structural barriers such as poverty, unsafe housing or lack of essential services.

Health education and behaviour change

Health education should be accurate, relevant, accessible and culturally appropriate. Start by identifying what people already know, their concerns, preferred language, literacy, resources and readiness. Use dialogue, demonstration, teach-back, visual materials and practical goal setting rather than one-way instruction alone.

Behaviour is influenced by capability, opportunity, motivation, social norms and structural conditions. Avoid blaming people when recommended actions are inaccessible. Evaluate whether education improves understanding, confidence, access or health outcomes, and revise the approach with community feedback.

Communicable disease prevention and control

Communicable disease control uses surveillance, early recognition, testing, reporting, infection prevention, vaccination, treatment pathways and public communication. Transmission may occur through respiratory droplets or aerosols, direct contact, contaminated food or water, blood and body fluids, vectors or other routes.

Apply standard precautions and additional transmission-based precautions according to current guidance. Suspected outbreaks should be reported through designated public health channels. Case definitions, isolation requirements, contact management and reporting timelines are jurisdiction-specific and must be checked with current authorities.

Environmental health, water, sanitation and hygiene

Environmental health examines how physical, chemical, biological and social environments influence health. Safe drinking water, sanitation, waste management, food hygiene, clean air, housing, vector control and occupational safety reduce exposure to preventable hazards. Environmental risks often affect disadvantaged communities disproportionately.

Community assessment may identify unsafe water sources, poor drainage, indoor air pollution, waste accumulation or vector breeding sites. Interventions require collaboration with environmental health officers, local government, utilities, schools and community groups. Nurses should report hazards through the appropriate channels rather than undertaking unsafe remediation.
